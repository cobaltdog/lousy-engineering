    Analysis of Control Volumes

    For closed systems, the conservation of mass principle is implicitly used by requiring that the mass of the system remains constant during a process. For control volumes, mass can cross the boundaries, and so we must keep track of the amount of mass entering and leaving.

    #Conservation of Mass

    #Mass and Volume Flow Rates

    A fluid flows into or out of a control volume, usually through pipes or ducts.

    dm˙=ρVn​ dAc​

    m˙=∫Ac​​ ρVn​ dAc​

    In practical applications, density is essentially uniform over the pipe cross section. However, velocity is never uniform due to the no-slip
    condition at the wall.

    For incompressible flow,

    💡
    m˙=ρAc​Vavg​

    If the velocity were Vavg​ all through the cross section, the mass flow rate would be identical to that obtained by integrating the actual velocity profile.

    V˙=∫Ac​​ Vn​ dAc​

    💡
    V˙=Ac​Vavg​

    The mass and volume flow rates are related by,

    💡
    m˙=ρV˙=vV˙​

    #Conservation of Mass Principle

    The conservation of mass principle for a control volume can be expressed as,

    💡
    min​−mout​=ΔmCV​
    💡
    m˙in​−m˙out​=dtdmCV​​

    Consider a control volume of arbitrary shape.

    dm=ρ dV

    mCV​=∫CV​ ρ dV

    dtdmCV​​=dtd​∫CV​ ρ dV

    Now consider mass flow into or out of the control volume through a differential area on the control surface of the control volume.

    dm˙=ρVn​ dA=ρVcosθ dA

    m˙net​=∫CS​ ρVcosθ dA

    A positive value indicates a net outflow of mass.
    A negative value indicates a net inflow of mass.

    The general conservation of mass relation can be expressed as,

    💡
    0=dtd​∫CV​ ρ dV+∫CS​ ρVcosθ dA

    A simple rule in selecting a control volume is to make the control surface normal to the flow at all locations where it crosses the fluid flow. Thus, the dot product Vcosθ becomes the magnitude of the velocity.

    #Mass Balance for Steady-Flow Processes

    In a steady-flow process, the total amount of mass contained within a control volume does not change with time.

    💡
    ∑m˙in​=∑m˙out​

    For single-stream steady-flow systems,

    💡
    ρ1​A1​V1​=ρ2​A2​V2​

    Volume flow rates into and out of a steady-flow device may be different. The volume flow rate at the outlet of an air compressor is much less than that at the inlet even though the mass flow rate is constant. This is due to the higher density of air at the outlet.

    #Energy of a Flowing Fluid

    Control volumes involves mass flow across their boundaries. Some work is required to push the
    mass into or out of the control volume — flow
    work or flow energy.

    If fluid pressure is P and the cross-sectional area of a fluid element is A, a force is applied on the element.

    F=PA

    To push the entire fluid element into the control volume, this force must act through a distance L.

    Wflow​=FL

    Wflow​=PAL

    Wflow​=PVˉ

    On a per unit mass basis,

    💡
    wflow​=Pv

    #Total Energy of a Flowing Fluid

    Total energy of a simple compressible system consists of,

    💡
    E=U+21​mV2+mgz
    💡
    e=u+21​V2+gz

    Fluid entering or leaving a control volume possesses an additional form of energy Pv.

    θ=Pv+e

    θ=Pv+u+21​V2+gz

    💡
    θ=h+21​V2+gz

    θ represents the total energy of a flowing fluid on a per unit mass basis.

    #Energy Transfer by Mass Flow

    Total energy of a flowing fluid is,

    💡
    Em​=m(h+21​V2+gz)
    💡
    E˙m​=m˙(h+21​V2+gz)

    When the kinetic and potential energies of a fluid stream are negligible, the equations simplify to
    E=mh and E˙=m˙h.

    The properties of the mass at each inlet or exit may be changing with time as well as over the cross section. Thus, the total energy transported by mass through an inlet or exit is obtained by integration — mi​θi​ and me​θe​.

    💡
    Ein​=∫mi​​ h+21​Vi​2+gz dmi​

    #Energy Analysis of Steady-Flow

    During a steady-flow process, no intensive or extensive properties within the control volume change with time. Thus, the volume Vˉ, the mass m, and the total energy E remains constant.

    Since volume of a control volume is a constant,
    there is no boundary work. Thus, min​=mout​.

    💡
    ∑m˙in​=∑m˙out​=∑m˙

    Since total energy remains constant, there is no change in total energy, E˙in​=E˙out​.

    💡
    Q˙​in​+W˙in​+∑in​m˙θ=Q˙​out​+W˙out​+∑out​m˙θ

    For a general steady-flow system,

    💡
    Q˙​−W˙=∑out​m˙θ−∑in​m˙θ

    For a single-stream device,

    💡
    Q˙​−W˙=m˙[Δh+21​Δ(V2)+gΔz]

    Dividing by m˙,

    💡
    q−w=h2​−h1​+21​(V2​2−V1​2)+g(z2​−z1​)

    If the fluid experiences negligible changes in its kinetic and potential energies,

    💡
    q−w=h2​−h1​

    Enthalpy change of a fluid can easily be determined by reading the enthalpy values at the
    exit and inlet states from the table. For ideal gases, it can be approximated by     Δh=cpavg​​ΔT.

    #Nozzles and Diffusers

    A nozzle is a device that increases the velocity of a fluid at the expense of pressure.

    A diffuser is a device that increases the pressure of a fluid by slowing it down.

    Characteristics of nozzles and diffusers:

    • Negligible heat transfer between the fluid and surroundings
    • Negligible change in potential energy of the fluid
    • No work is done

    #Turbines and Compressors

    Turbines produce power output whereas compressors require power input.

    Characteristics of turbines:

    • Negligible heat transfer since they are typically well insulated
    • Negligible changes in potential energy of the fluid
    • Significant change in kinetic energy of the fluid

    Fluid velocities encountered in most turbines are very high, thus there is a significant change in its kinetic energy. However, this change is usually very small relative to the change in enthalpy. Hence, it is often disregarded.

    Characteristics of compressors:

    • Negligible heat transfer unless there is intentional cooling
    • Negligible changes in potential energy of the fluid
    • Negligible changes in kinetic energy of the fluid

    #Throttling Valves

    Description of throttling valves:

    • Any kind of flow-restricting devices that cause a significant pressure drop
    • Produce pressure drop without involving any work, unlike turbines
    • Pressure drop often accompanied by a large drop in temperature

    Characteristics of throttling valves:

    • Flow through devices may be assumed to be adiabatic
    • Negligible change in potential energy of the fluid
    • Increase in kinetic energy is generally insignificant
    • No work is done

    The conservation of energy principle for a throttling valve reduces to,

    💡
    h1​=h2​
    💡
    u1​+P1​v1​=u2​+P2​v2​

    Since enthalpy values are the same, a throttling valve can be called an isenthalpic device.

    For a liquid, if flow energy increases, it can do so at the expense of internal energy. Thus, internal energy of the liquid decreases, which is usually accompanied by a temperature drop. For an ideal gas, since h=h(T), temperature has to remain constant.

    #Example

    At P1​=0.8MPa,

    T1​=31.31°C

    At P2​=0.12MPa,

    T2​=−22.32°C

    uf​=22.38 kJ/kg

    ufg​=195.15 kJ/kg

    u2​=uf​+xufg​

    88.8=22.38+195.15x

    x=0.340

    ΔT=−22.32−31.31

    ΔT=−53.63°C

    • No work is done

    #Pipe and Duct Flow

    Flow through a pipe or duct usually satisfies the steady-flow conditions, and thus can be analyzed as a steady-flow process. The control volume can be selected to coincide with
    the interior surfaces of the portion of the pipe or duct.

    Characteristics of pipes:

    • Potential energy changes may be significant when fluid undergoes elevation change
    • Kinetic energy changes are usually insignificant
    • Work interactions should be considered (if any)

    Changes in kinetic energy are usually insignificant, especially when the pipe or duct
    diameter is constant and the heating effects are negligible. However, it may become significant in ducts with variable cross-sectional areas when the compressibility
    effects are significant.

    #Energy Analysis of Unsteady-Flow

    Unsteady-flow processes involve changes within the control volume — mass within the system boundaries does not remain constant during a process. Unsteady-flow systems may involve moving boundaries, and thus boundary work.

    💡
    min​−mout​=ΔmCV​
    💡
    Ein​−Eout​=ΔEsys​

    #Uniform-Flow Process

    Assumptions made:

    • Fluid flow at any inlet or exit is uniform and steady
    • Fluid properties do not change with time or position over cross section of inlet or exit
    • If properties do change, they are averaged and treated as constants for entire process

    💡
    (Qin​+Win​+∑in​mθ)−(Qout​+Wout​+∑out​mθ)=(m2​e2​−m1​e1​)sys​
    💡
    Q−W+mi​θi​−me​θe​=(E2​−E1​)sys​

    If kinetic and potential energy changes are negligible,

    💡
    Qnet,in​−Wnet,out​=(∑out​mh−∑in​mh)+(m2​u2​−m1​u1​)sys​

    If no mass enters or leaves the control volume, the equation reduces to the energy balance equation for closed systems. Work can be in the form of boundary work, electrical work, etc.
