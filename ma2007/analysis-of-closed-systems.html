    Analysis of Closed Systems

    #Moving Boundary Work

    Consider the gas enclosed in a piston-cylinder device.

    The initial pressure of the gas is P, the total volume is V,
    and the cross-sectional area of the piston is A. The piston is allowed to move a distance ds in a quasi-equilibrium manner.

    dW=PA ds=P dV

    💡
    Wb​=∫P dV

    P is the absolute pressure, which is always positive. Thus, boundary work is positive during an expansion process and negative during a compression process.

    Characteristics of boundary work:

    • Area under the process curve represents work
    • Work done will be different for each process
    • Area enclosed by two curves is net work done

    #Polytropic Process

    A polytropic process is a thermodynamic process that obeys the relation,

    💡
    PVn=Constant

    Wb​=∫12​P dV

    Wb​=∫12​CV−n dV

    Wb​=C1−nV2​−n+1−V1​−n+1​

    Wb​=1−nP2​V2​−P1​V1​​

    For an ideal gas, the equation can be written as,

    💡
    Wb​=1−nmR(T2​−T1​)​          (n=1)

    n=0 for an isobaric process
    n=γ for an isentropic process

    #Example

    V2​=2(0.05)=0.1 m3

    x=0.250.1−0.05​=0.2 m

    Fspring​=150(0.2)=30 kN

    P2​=0.2530​=120 kPa

    Without the spring, the pressure would remain the same at 200kPa while the piston is rising. Under the effect of the spring, the pressure rises linearly from 200kPa to 320kPa.

    Wtot​=21​(200+320)(0.1−0.05)

    Wtot​=13 kJ

    Region 1 is the work done against the piston and the atmosphere.
    Region 2 is the work done against the spring.

    Wspring​=21​(320−200)(0.1−0.05)

    Wspring​=3 kJ

    The work done against the spring equals the increase in potential energy.

    Wspring​=21​(150)(0.2)2

    Wspring​=3 kJ

    #Energy Balance for Closed Systems

    The general energy balance equation can be written as,

    💡
    ΔEsys​=Ein​−Eout​=(Qin​−Qout​)+(Win​−Wout​)+(Emin​​−Emout​​)

    For a closed system, since there is no mass flow across the boundary,

    💡
    ΔEsys​=Q−W

    For a stationary system, ΔEsys​=ΔU and the equation becomes U2​−U1​=Q−W.

    #Example 1

    ΔE=Q−W

    ΔU=Q−W

    U2​−U1​=Q−Wother​−Wb​

    U2​−U1​=Q−Wother​−P(V2​−V1​)

    (U2​+PV2​)−(U1​+PV1​)=Q−Wother​

    Since H=U+PV,

    H2​−H1​=Q−Wother​

    ΔH=Q−Wother​

    Welec​=120(0.2)(300)=7.2 kJ

    Qout​=3.7 kJ

    State 1: Saturated Vapor

    P1​=300 kPa

    h1​=hg@300​=2724.9 kJ/kg

    m(h2​−h1​)=Q−Wother​

    0.025(h2​−2724.9)=−3.7−(−7.2)

    h2​=2864.9 kJ/kg

    State 2: Superheated vapor

    P2​=300 kPa

    h2​=2684.9 kJ/kg

    T2​=200°C

    v1​=vf@25​=0.001003 m3/kg

    #U, H and Specific Heats of Ideal Gases

    The specific heat is the energy required to raise the temperature of a unit mass of a
    substance by 1°. There are two types of specific heats we are interested in, cv​ and cp​.

    cv​ can be defined as the change in internal energy of a substance per unit change in temperature at constant volume. cp​ can be defined as the change in enthalpy of a
    substance per unit change in temperature at constant pressure.

    cv​=(∂T∂u​)v​

    du=cv​ dT

    u2​−u1​=cv,avg​(T2​−T1​)

    💡
    Δu=cv,avg​ΔT

    cp​=(∂T∂u​)p​

    dh=cp​ dT

    h2​−h1​=cp,avg​(T2​−T1​)

    💡
    Δh=cp,avg​ΔT


    u and h are strong functions of temperature and weak functions of pressure.
    Thus, for an ideal gas, the partial derivatives can be replaced by ordinary derivatives.

    Variation of specific heats with temperature is smooth and may be approximated as linear
    over small temperature intervals     (<100°). Thus, the specific heats can be replaced with
    specific heat values at the average temperature.

    The Δu and Δh relations are not restricted to any kind of process. They are valid for any ideal gas undergoing any process (cv,avg​ is not just valid for a constant-volume process only).

    #Specific Heat Relations of Ideal Gases

    Using the definition of enthalpy and the ideal-gas equation,

    h=u+Pv

    Pv=Rspecific​T

    💡
    h=u+Rspecific​T

    A special relationship can be obtained between cv​ and cp​.

    h=u+RT

    dh=du+R dT

    cp​ dT=cv​ dT+R dT

    💡
    cp​=cv​+Rspecific​

    The specific heat ratio k is defined as,

    💡
    k=cv​cp​​

    kcv​=cp​ ⟶ kcv​=cv​+R ⟶ cv​=k−1R​

    cv​=kcp​​ ⟶ cp​−R=kcp​​ ⟶ kcp​=cp​+kR ⟶ cp​=k−1kR​

    #U, H and Specific Heats of Solids and Liquids

    A substance whose specific volume is constant is a incompressible substance. The constant-volume and constant-pressure specific heats are identical for incompressible substances.

    💡
    cp​=cv​=c

    #Internal Energy and Enthalpy Changes

    The specific heats of incompressible substances depend on temperature only.

    du=c dT

    u2​−u1​=c(T2​−T1​)

    💡
    Δu=cavg​ΔT

    For small temperature intervals, a specific heat at the average temperature can be used.

    h=u+Pv

    dh=du+v dP+P dv

    dh=du+v dP+0

    h2​−h1​=u2​−u1​+v(P2​−P1​)

    💡
    Δh=cavg​ΔT+v ΔP

    For solids, the term v ΔP is insignificant, thus Δh=cavg​ΔT.

    For liquids, two special cases are commonly encountered:

    • Constant-pressure process (heaters) — Δh=cavg​ΔT
    • Constant-temperature process (pumps) — Δh=v ΔP

    The last relation can be expressed as h2​−h1​=v(P2​−P1​). Taking state 2 as the compressed liquid state at a given temperature and pressure, and state 1 to be the
    saturated liquid state at the same temperature.

    💡
    hcomp​=hsat@T​+vsat@T​(Pcomp​−Psat@T​)

    #Example 1

    At T=100°C, the saturation pressure Psat​=101.42 kPa.

    Since P>Psat​, the water exists as a compressed liquid.

    Using Compressed Liquid Table

    At T=100°C and P=15 MPa,

    h=430.49 kJ/kg

    Approximating as a Saturated Liquid

    At T=100°C,

    h=419.17 kJ/kg

    Using the Correction Formula

    hcomp​=hsat@T​+vsat@T​(Pcomp​−Psat@T​)

    h=419.17+(0.001043)(15000−101.42)

    h=434.71 kJ/kg
