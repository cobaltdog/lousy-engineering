    #Fuels and Combustion

    Any material that can be burned to release thermal energy is called a fuel, the most
    familiar being hydrocarbon fuels, which are denoted by the general formula Cn​Hm​.

    A chemical reaction in which a fuel is oxidized and a large quantity of energy is released
    is called combustion. The oxidizer most often used in combustion processes is air, as it
    is free and readily available.

    Dry air can be approximated as 21% oxygen and 79% nitrogen by mole numbers. Thus, each mole of O2​ is accompanied by 3.76 mol of N2​.

    The presence of N2​ greatly affects the outcome of a combustion process as it enters in large quantities at
    a low temperature and exits at higher temperatures, absorbing a large proportion of the chemical energy released during combustion.

    Air that enters also contains some water vapor, which
    is treated as an inert gas. When combustion gases
    are cooled below the dew-point temperature, some moisture condenses.

    💡
    1 kmol O2​+3.76 kmol N2​=4.76 kmol Air

    Reactants are components that exist before combustion, while products are components
    that exist after combustion.

    Fuel must be brought above its ignition temperature
    to start the combustion. The proportions of the fuel and air must be in the proper range as well.

    #Combustion Process

    A combustion process is complete if:

    • All the carbon burns to CO2​
    • All the hydrogen burns to H2​O
    • All the sulfur (if any) burns to SO2​

    A combustion process is incomplete if it contain any unburned fuel or products such as C, H2​, CO or OH.

    Insufficient oxygen is one reason for incomplete combustion. However, incomplete
    combustion occurs even when more oxygen is present in the combustion chamber
    than is needed for complete combustion. This may be attributed to insufficient
    mixing during the limited time that the fuel and the oxygen are in contact.

    Oxygen has a much greater tendency to combine with hydrogen than it does with
    carbon. Thus, hydrogen in the fuel normally burns to completion, forming     H2​O,
    even when there is less oxygen than needed for complete combustion.

    • Stoichiometric air is minimum amount of air needed for complete combustion
    • Amount of air in excess of stoichiometric amount is called excess air
    • Amount of air less than stoichiometric amount is called deficiency of air

    Since water vapor is often present in the combustion process, it may condense, which
    can cause corrosion. Thus, it is important to determine the dew point temperature of
    the water vapor to minimize condensation.

    #Enthalpy of Formation and
    Enthalpy of Combustion

    During a chemical reaction, some chemical bonds that bind the atoms into molecules are
    broken and new ones are formed. Since the chemical energy associated with these bonds
    are different for reactants and products, changes in chemical energies must be accounted
    for in an energy balance.

    Neglecting any changes in kinetic and potential energies,

    💡
    ΔEsys​=ΔEstate​+ΔEchem​

    Combustion involves no work interactions. Thus, the steady-flow energy balance is,

    💡
    Q=Hproducts​−Hreactants​

    The above is the enthalpy of reaction hR​, which is the difference between the enthalpy
    of the products at a specified state and the enthalpy of the reactants at the same state
    for a complete reaction.

    For combustion processes, enthalpy of reaction is referred to as enthalpy of combustion.

    💡
    hR​=hC​=Hproducts​−Hreactants​

    However, it is not practical to list the hC​ of all fuels and fuel mixtures and is not of much use
    when the combustion is incomplete. Thus, we rely on the enthalpy of formation hˉf​, which
    is the enthalpy of a substance at a specified state due to its chemical composition.

    The enthalpy of combustion at the reference state of 25°C and 1 atm is,

    💡
    hˉC°​=∑ N(hˉf°​)p​−∑ N(hˉf°​)r​

    where hˉC°​ is expressed in kJ/kmol fuel

    At states other than the reference state, the enthalpy of combustion is,

    💡
    hˉC​=hˉC°​+∑ N(Δhˉ)p​−∑ N(Δhˉ)r​

    where difference in sensible enthalpy Δhˉ=hˉ−hˉ°,
    and     hˉ° is the sensible enthalpy at 25°C and 1 atm,
    and     hˉ is the sensible enthalpy at given temperature

    #Heating Value

    Heating value of a fuel is the amount of heat released when the fuel is burned completely
    in a steady-flow process and the products are returned to the state of the reactants.

    💡
    HV=∣hC​∣ expressed in kJ/kg fuel

    Heating value depends on the phase of H2​O in the products. It is called the higher heating value (HHV) when H2​O is in the liquid phase, and lower heating value (LHV) when H2​O is
    in the vapor phase.

    💡
    HHV=LHV+(m⋅hfg​)H2​O​

    where m is the mass of H2​O in the products per unit mass of fuel, and
    hfg​ is the enthalpy of vaporization of water at the specified temperature

    #Analysis of Reacting Systems

    #Steady-Flow System

    Enthalpy of a component is,

    💡
    H=N[hˉf°​+(hˉ−hˉ°)]=N[hˉf°​+Δhˉ]

    where hˉf°​ is enthalpy of formation at 25°C and 1 atm,
    and     hˉ° is the sensible enthalpy at 25°C and 1 atm,
    and     hˉ is the sensible enthalpy at given temperature

    The energy balance for a chemically reacting steady-flow system is,

    💡
    Qin​+Win​+∑N(hˉf°​+Δhˉ)r​=Qout​+Wout​+∑N(hˉf°​+Δhˉ)p​

    Simplifying the above equation,

    💡
    Q−W=∑N(hˉf°​+Δhˉ)p​−∑N(hˉf°​+Δhˉ)r​

    Note that the equation for the first law is expressed in kJ/kmol fuel.

    If the enthalpy of combustion for a reaction is known,

    💡
    Q−W=hˉC​+∑N(Δhˉ)p​−∑N(Δhˉ)r​

    The enthalpy of combustion is determined from the enthalpy of formation.

    #Closed System

    For a closed system, the first law of thermodynamics is,

    Q−W=ΔU

    Q−PΔV=ΔU

    Q=ΔU+PΔV

    Q=Hproducts​−Hreactants​

    Internal energy of a component is,

    💡
    U=N[hˉf°​+Δhˉ−Pvˉ]

    Recall that h=u+Pv. Rearranging the terms, we get u=h−Pv.

    The energy balance for a closed system is,

    💡
    Q−W=∑N(hˉf°​+Δhˉ−Pvˉ)p​−∑N(hˉf°​+Δhˉ−Pvˉ)r​

    Alternatively, for ideal gases, u=h−Ru​T.

    💡
    Q−W=∑N(hˉf°​+Δhˉ−Ru​T)p​−∑N(hˉf°​+Δhˉ−Ru​T)r​

    #Adiabatic Flame Temperature

    In the absence of work interactions, chemical energy released during combustion is either lost as heat to
    the surroundings or used internally to raise the temperature of the products.

    Assuming there’s no heat loss, the temperature of
    the products reaches a maximum — adiabatic
    flame/combustion temperature.

    💡
    ∑N(hˉf°​+Δhˉ)p​=∑N(hˉf°​+Δhˉ)r​

    The enthalpy of the reactants Hreac​ can be easily determined as their states are specified.
    The enthalpy of the products     Hprod​ is not so straightforward as the temperature of the
    products is not known. Thus, a temperature is assumed for the products and the     Hprod​ determined for this temperature. If it is not equal to Hreac​, another temperature is chosen.
    The adiabatic flame temperature is then determined from these two results by interpolation.

    Adiabatic flame temperature depends on:

    • State of the reactants
    • Amount of air used
    • Degree of completion of reaction

    For a specified fuel at a specified state that is burned with air at a specified state, the maximum adiabatic flame temperature is when complete combustion
    occurs with the theoretical amount of air.

    In practice, the adiabatic flame temperature is not attainable.

    • Heat losses are unavoidable
    • Excess air dilutes the products, lowering temperature
    • Combustion may not be complete, even with excess air
    • Dissociation (energy-absorbing phenomenon) may occur at high temperatures

    #Example

    #Part (a)

    C8​H18​+ath​(O2​+3.76N2​)→xCO2​+yH2​O+3.76ath​N2​

    C8​H18​+12.5(O2​+3.76N2​)→8CO2​+9H2​O+47N2​

    ∑N(hˉf°​+Δhˉ)p​=∑N(hˉf°​+Δhˉ)r​

    8(hˉf°​+Δhˉ)CO2​​+9(hˉf°​+Δhˉ)H2​O​+47(hˉf°​+Δhˉ)N2​​=1(hˉf°​+Δhˉ)C8​H18​​+12.5(hˉf°​+Δhˉ)O2​​+47(hˉf°​+Δhˉ)N2​​

    8(hˉf°​+Δhˉ)CO2​​+9(hˉf°​+Δhˉ)H2​O​+47(0+Δhˉ)N2​​=1(hˉf°​)C8​H18​​+12.5(0)O2​​+47(0)N2​​

    Note that the reactants are at the reference state, change in enthalpy Δhˉ=0.
    Also, for stable substances such as     O2​ and N2​, enthalpy of formation hˉf°​=0.

    Rearranging the equation,

    8(hˉf°​+Δhˉ)CO2​​+9(hˉf°​+Δhˉ)H2​O​+47(Δhˉ)N2​​=(hˉf°​)C8​H18​​

    8(Δhˉ)CO2​​+9(Δhˉ)H2​O​+47(Δhˉ)N2​​=(hˉf°​)C8​H18​​−8(hˉf°​)CO2​​−9(hˉf°​)H2​O​

    From Table A26,

    (hˉf°​)C8​H18​​=−249950 kJ/kmol

    (hˉf°​)CO2​​=−393520 kJ/kmol

    (hˉf°​)H2​O​=−241820 kJ/kmol

    Substituting the values into the equation,

    8(Δhˉ)CO2​​+9(Δhˉ)H2​O​+47(Δhˉ)N2​​=5074630 kJ

    8(hˉ−hˉ°)CO2​​+9(hˉ−hˉ°)H2​O​+47(hˉ−hˉ°)N2​​=5074630 kJ

    From properties tables, at T=298 K,

    (hˉ°)CO2​​=9364 kJ/kmol

    (hˉ°)H2​O​=9904 kJ/kmol

    (hˉ°)N2​​=8869 kJ/kmol

    Substituting the values into the equation,

    8(hˉ−9364)CO2​​+9(hˉ−9904)H2​O​+47(hˉ−8869)N2​​=5074630 kJ

    8(hˉ)CO2​​+9(hˉ)H2​O​+47(hˉ)N2​​=5646081 kJ

    Assuming the temperature of the products to be Tp​=2350 K,

    8(122091)+9(100846)+47(77496)=5526654 kJ

    Assuming the temperature of the products to be Tp​=2400 K,

    8(125152)+9(103508)+47(79320)=5660828 kJ

    5660828−55266545646081−5526654​=2400−2350Tp​−2350​

    Tp​=2395

    Thus, combustion of a lean or rich mixture will always result in a lower temperature
    of the products. Only when the fuel reacts with the stoichiometric ratio will the combustion results in the highest temperature of the products.
