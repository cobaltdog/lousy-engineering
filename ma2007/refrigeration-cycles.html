    Refrigeration Cycles

    #Reversed Carnot Cycle

    Consider a reversed Carnot cycle executed
    within the saturation dome of a refrigerant.

    The refrigerant absorbs heat QL​ isothermally from
    a low-temperature source at TL​ (process 1-2), and
    is then compressed isentropically to state 3.

    The refrigerant then rejects heat QH​ isothermally
    to a high-temperature sink at TH​ (process 3-4),
    and expands isentropically to state 1.

    💡
    COPR​=TL​TH​​−11​
    💡
    COPHP​=1−TH​TL​​1​

    However, the reversed Carnot cycle is not a suitable model for refrigeration cycles. Process 2-3 cannot
    be approximated as it involves compression of a
    liquid-vapor mixture, which requires a compressor
    that can handle two phases. Process 4-1 involves
    the expansion of high-moisture content refrigerant
    in a turbine.

    #Vapor-Compression Cycle

    #Ideal Vapor-Compression Cycle

    The ideal vapor-compression cycle consists of:

    • Process 1-2: Isentropic compression
    • Process 2-3: Constant-pressure heat rejection
    • Process 3-4: Throttling in expansion device
    • Process 4-1: Constant-pressure heat absorption

    Description of cycle:

    • Refrigerant enters the compressor as saturated vapor (state 1) and leaves as superheated vapor (state 2) after being compressed isentropically
      to condenser pressure
    • Refrigerant enters the condense as superheated vapor and leaves as saturated liquid (state 3) as
      a result of heat rejection to surroundings
    • Refrigerant is throttled to evaporator pressure by the expansion valve and its temperature drops below that of the refrigerated space
    • Refrigerant enters evaporator as low-quality saturated mixture (state 4) and completely evaporates by absorbing heat
    • Refrigerant leaves the evaporator as saturated vapor and re-enters the compressor

    The ideal vapor-compression cycle is not an internally reversible cycle as it involves an irreversible throttling process. If the throttling device was replaced with an isentropic turbine, the refrigerant would enter the evaporator at state 4’ instead of state 4. As a result, the refrigeration capacity would increase and the net work input would decrease.

    Since the condenser and evaporator do no work and the compressor can be approximated as adiabatic, the COPs of refrigerators and heat pumps operating on this cycle are,

    💡
    COPR​=wnet​qL​​=h2​−h1​h1​−h4​​
    💡
    COPHP​=wnet​qH​​=h2​−h1​h2​−h3​​

    #Actual Vapor-Compression Cycle

    An actual vapor-compression cycle differs from the ideal one due to irreversibilities such as fluid friction and heat transfer to or from the surroundings.

    Ideally, the refrigerant leaves the evaporator and
    enters the compressor as saturated vapor. However,
    it may not be possible to control the state precisely. Thus, the refrigerant leaves the evaporator slightly suprheated to ensure it’s completely vaporized
    when it enters the compressor.

    The actual compression process involves frictional effects, which increases entropy, and heat transfer, which can either decrease or increase entropy, depending on the direction of heat transfer.

    The refrigerant may not leave the condenser as saturated liquid due to pressure drop within the condenser itself. Thus, the refrigerant is subcooled somewhat before it enters the expansion valve.

    The subcooled refrigerant enters the evaporator with lower enthalpy and can absorb more heat from the refrigerated space.

    #Cascade Refrigeration System

    A cascade system has two or more refrigeration cycles that operate in series. The cycles
    are connected through a heat exchanger, which serves as an evaporator for one cycle
    and a condenser for the other cycle.

    Assuming heat exchanger is insulated and kinetic and potential energies are negligible,

    ∑m˙i​hi​=∑m˙e​he​

    m˙A​(h8​−h5​)=m˙B​(h3​−h2​)

    💡
    m˙B​m˙A​​=h8​−h5​h3​−h2​​

    The COP of a cascade refrigeration system is,

    💡
    COPR,cascade​=W˙net​Q˙​L​​=m˙A​(h6​−h5​)+m˙B​(h2​−h1​)m˙B​(h1​−h4​)​

    The terms can be determined using the first law of thermodynamics for open systems.

    In a cascade system, the refrigerants in both cycles may not necessarily be the same
    since there is no mixing taking place in the heat exchanger. Thus, if different refrigerants
    are used in each cycle, there would be a separate saturation dome for each fluid and the
    T-s diagram for each cycle would be different.

    When the same refrigerant is used throughout the cascade system, the heat exchanger
    can be replaced by a mixing chamber since it has better heat transfer characteristics.

    The liquid refrigerant expands in the first expansion valve and in the process, part of it vaporizes. This saturated vapor (state 3) is mixed with superheated vapor from the low-pressure compressor (state 2). The remaining saturated liquid (state 7) expands through
    the second expansion valve into the evaporator.

    #Multipurpose Refrigeration System with a Compressor

    Some applications require refrigeration at more than one temperature.

    Most refrigerated goods have a high water content and the refrigerated space must
    be maintained above the ice point to prevent freezing. However, the freezer is to be maintained at sub-zero temperatures. Thus, two separate expansion valves are
    required to produce a different refrigerant temperature for each.

    #Absorption Refrigeration System

    Absorption refrigeration system becomes economically attractive when there is a source
    of inexpensive thermal energy at a temperature of 100°C to 200°C. This type of system involves the absorption of a refrigerant by a transport medium.

    Unlike the compressor in the vapor-compression system, the absorption system makes use of a complex absorption mechanism. The refrigerant is dissolved in the transport medium — water, before being pumped to the generator. Heat is then transferred to the solution and vaporizes some of it. The vapor refrigerant then enters the condenser.

    #Gas Refrigeration Cycle

    The gas refrigeration cycle is also known as the reversed Brayton cycle.

    The area under process 4-1 represents the heat QL​ removed from the refrigerated space, and the
    enclosed area represents the net work input.

    The COP for a gas refrigeration cycle is,

    💡
    COPR​=wnet​qL​​=wcomp​−wtur​qL​​

    where qL​=h1​−h4​
    wcomp​=h2​−h1​
    wtur​=h3​−h4​

    The gas refrigeration cycle deviates from the reversed Carnot cycle as the heat transfer processes are not isothermal. Thus, it has a lower COP relative to the vapor-compression cycle or the Carnot cycle.

    #Regenerative Gas Cycle

    Adding a regenerator allows for a lower temperature to be achieved after the refrigerant expands in the turbine. High-pressure gas from the compressor is further cooled to T4​ before expanding in the turbine. Lowering the inlet temperature automatically lowers the turbine exit temperature.

    #Heat Pump Systems

    Energy sources for heat pumps:

    • Atmospheric air (air-to-air system)
    • Water (underground water)
    • Ground (soil temperature)

    Since heat pump systems have the same mechanical components as refrigeration systems, a system can be used as a heat pump in the winter, and an air conditioner in summer, through the use of a reversing valve. The condenser of the heat pump functions as the evaporator of
    the air conditioner in the summer.
