    Vapor Power Cycles

    In vapor cycles, the working fluid is alternately vaporized and condensed during the cycle.

    #Rankine Cycle

    The ideal Rankine cycle consists of 4 processes:

    • Isentropic compression in a pump
    • Constant-pressure heat addition
    • Isentropic expansion in a turbine
    • Constant-pressure heat rejection

    Description of cycle:

    • Steam enters pump as saturated liquid and is compressed isentropically to boiler pressure
    • Steam enters boiler as a compressed liquid
      and leaves as a superheated vapor (state 3)
    • Vapor expands isentropically in the turbine
    • Vapor produces work by rotating a shaft
    • Pressure and temperature of vapor drops
      during this process to the values at state 4
    • Vapor enters the condenser as a saturated
      liquid-vapor mixture with a high quality
    • Steam leaves condenser as a saturated liquid
      and enters the pump, completing the cycle

    #Deviation from Ideal Rankine Cycle

    Irreversibilities such as fluid fricton and heat loss to surroundings result in decreased cycle efficiency.

    Fluid friction causes pressure drops in the boiler, condenser, and the piping between components.
    Thus, to compensate for pressure drops, the water must be pumped to a sufficiently higher pressure.
    This requires a larger pump and higher work input.

    Heat loss to surroundings is inevitable. Thus, more heat needs to be transferred to the steam in the
    boiler to maintain the same net work ouput.
    Thus, cycle efficency decreases.

    Irreversibilities within the pump and turbine results in higher work input and lower work output.

    ηpump​=wa​ws​​=h2a​−h1​h2s​−h1​​
    💡
    ηtur​=ws​wa​​=h3​−h4s​h3​−h4a​​

    Increasing thermal efficiency of a cycle is achieved by:

    • Increase average temperature at which heat is transferred to working fluid in boiler
    • Decrease average temperature at which heat is rejected from fluid in condenser

    #Lowering the Condenser Pressure

    Lowering the condenser pressure results in an increase in net work output, represented by
    the colored area in the diagram.

    However, lowering the condenser pressure creates
    the possibility of air leakage into the condenser.
    Also, it increases the moisture content of the
    steam in the turbine, which decreases turbine efficiency and erodes the turbine blades.

    #Superheating the Steam

    Superheating the steam results in an increase in
    net work output. Also, it decreases the moisture content of the steam at the turbine exit.

    Temperature to which steam can be superheated
    is limited by metallurgical considerations.

    #Increasing the Boiler Pressure

    Increasing the boiler pressure automatically raises the temperature at which heat is transferred to the steam and thus, raises the thermal efficiency.

    For a fixed turbine inlet temperature, the cycle
    shifts to the left and moisture content of the steam
    at the turbine exit increases. This undesirable side effect can be corrected by reheating the steam.

    #Rankine Cycle with Reheating

    In the first stage (high-pressure turbine), steam is expanded isentropically to
    an intermediate pressure and sent back to the boiler, where it is reheated to
    the inlet temperature of the first turbine stage.

    💡
    qin​=qinitial​+qreheat​=(h3​−h2​)+(h5​−h4​)

    #Rankine Cycle with Regeneration

    As a part of the heat addition process takes place over a range of low temperatures, thermal efficiency of the cycle is reduced. Thus, by using regeneration, which is accomplished by bleeding steam from the turbine at various points, feedwater
    is heated before entering the boiler, improving efficiency.

    #Open Feedwater Heaters

    Condensed water is compressed isentropically to the feedwater heater pressure. It is
    then routed to the feedwater heater, where it mixes with steam extracted from the
    turbine. The fraction of steam extracted is such that the mixture leaves the heater
    as a saturated liquid.

    Energy balance for the open feedwater heater is,

    💡
    yh6​+(1−y)h2​=h3​

    where y=m˙5​m˙6​​ (the fraction of steam extracted)

    #Closed Feedwater Heaters

    In a closed feedwater heater, the two streams can be at different pressures, since
    they do not mix. Ideally, the feedwater is heated to the exit temperature of the
    extracted steam, which leaves the heater as a saturated liquid.

    Energy balance for the closed feedwater heater is,

    💡
    yh7​+(1−y)h2​=yh3​+(1−y)h9​

    #Co-generation

    #Ideal Co-generation Plant

    Cogeneration is the production of more than one useful form of energy, such as process heat and
    electric power, from the same energy source.

    Process heat is the application of heat during
    industrial processes, which is usually supplied
    by steam.

    Either a Rankine, Brayton, or combined cycle can be used as the power cycle in a cogeneration plant.

    Since there is no condenser, no heat is rejected as waste heat. Thus, all the energy transferred to the steam is utilized.

    Utilization factor for a cogeneration plant is,

    💡
    ϵu​=Q˙​in​W˙net​+Q˙​P​​

    where Q˙​P​ is heat supplied by process heater

    #Actual Co-generation Plant

    The ideal steam-turbine cogeneration plant is not practical as it
    cannot adjust to the variations in power and process-heat loads.

    Normal operation:

    • Some steam is extracted from the turbine
    • Rest of the steam expands to condenser pressure
    • Heat is rejected from the condenser (waste heat)

    High demand for process heat:

    • All the steam is routed to the process heater
    • No heat is rejected from the condenser

      If this is insufficient, some steam leaving the
      boiler is throttled by an expansion valve and
      directed to the process heater.

    Note that when the superheated steam from the boiler is throttled using the expansion
    valve, the process is non-isentropic. Thus, entropy increases during the process (state 4).

    #Combined Gas-Vapor Cycle

    The more popular combined cycle is the gas-turbine (Brayton) cycle topping a steam-
    turbine (Rankine) cycle, which has a higher thermal efficiency than either of the cycles
    executed individually.

    Gas-turbine cycles typically operate at considerably higher temperatures than steam-
    turbine cycles. However, the gas leaves the turbine at very high temperatures, which
    erases any potential gains in thermal efficiency. Thus, it is desirable to use the high-
    temperature exhaust gases as an energy source for the bottoming cycle.

    In general, more than one gas turbine is needed to supply sufficient heat to the steam. Also,
    the steam cycle may involve regeneration as well as reheating. Energy for the reheating
    process can be supplied by burning additional fuel in the oxygen-rich exhaust gases.
