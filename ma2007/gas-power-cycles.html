    Gas Power Cycles

    In gas cycles, the working fluid remains in the gaseous phase throughout the entire cycle.

    #Analysis of Power Cycles

    Idealizations and simplifications in analysis:

    • Cycle does not involve friction (working fluid does not experience pressure drop)
    • Expansion and compression processes take place in a quasi-equilibrium manner
    • Pipes connecting the various components are well-insulated (negligible heat transfer)
    • Change in kinetic and potential energies of working fluid is assumed to be negligible

    In devices that involve shaft work, such as turbines, compressors and pumps, the kinetic and potential energy terms are negligible. Fluid velocities in devices such as condensers, boilers
    and mixing chambers are typically low, and the fluid streams experience little change in their velocities. The only devices where the changes in kinetic energy are significant are nozzles
    and diffusers, which are specifically designed to create large changes in velocity.

    #Air-Standard Assumptions

    In gas power cycles, the working fluid remains a gas throughout the entire cycle.

    Air-standard assumptions:

    • Working fluid is air, which circulates in a closed loop and behaves as an ideal gas
    • All processes that make up the cycle are internally reversible
    • Combustion process is replaced by a heat-addition process
    • Exhaust process is replaced by a heat-rejection process

    In internal combustion engines, the composition of the working fluid changes from air and
    fuel to combustion products during the cycle. However, since air is predominantly nitrogen
    (inert), the working fluid closely resembles air at all times.

    Even though internal combustion engines operate on a mechanical cycle, the working fluid
    does not undergo a complete thermodynamic cycle. It is expelled from the engine at some
    point instead of being returned to its initial state, thus ICEs are open systems.

    #Carnot Cycle

    Four reversible processes:

    • Isentropic compression (process 4-1)
    • Isothermal heat addition (process 1-2)
    • Isentropic expansion (process 2-3)
    • Isothermal heat rejection (process 3-4)

    Isothermal heat transfer is very difficult to achieve
    in reality because it would require very large heat exchangers and it would take a very long time.

    💡
    ηth(Carnot)​=1−TH​TL​​

    #Reciprocating Engines

    Description of reciprocating engine:

    • Top dead center — smallest volume in cylinder
    • Bottom dead center — largest volume in cylinder
    • Stroke — distance between TDC and BDC
    • Bore — diameter of piston

    Air-fuel mixture is drawn into the cylinder through
    the intake valve and the combustion products are expelled from the cylinder through the exhaust valve.

    The compression ratio r of an engine is,

    💡
    r=Vmin​Vmax​​

    Mean-effective pressure (MEP) is the pressure that,
    if acted on the piston during the entire power stroke, would produce the same amount of net work as that produced during the actual cycle.

    💡
    MEP=Vmax​−Vmin​Wnet​​

    #Otto Cycle (Spark-Ignition Engines)

    In most spark-ignition engines, the piston executes four complete strokes within the cylinder, and the crankshaft completes two revolutions for each thermodynamic cycle.

    Process 0-1 (intake):

    • Air-fuel mixture enters the cylinder through
      the intake valve at atmospheric pressure P0​
    • Concurrently, piston moves from TDC to BDC

    Process 1-2 (isentropic compression):

    • Intake valve is closed at state 1
    • Mixture is compressed isentropically

    Process 2-3 (heat addition):

    • Heat is transferred at constant volume
    • Represents the combustion process

    Process 3-4 (isentropic expansion):

    • Mixture expands isentropically to state 4

    Process 4-1 (heat rejection):

    • Heat is rejected at constant volume
    • Represents the expulsion of exhaust gases

    Work done during the constant-pressure intake is w0 → 1​=P0​(v1​−v0​), while during
    the constant-pressure exhaust is w1 → 0​=P0​(v0​−v1​). The two processes cancel each other as the work output during the intake is equal to the work input during the exhaust. Thus, the Otto cycle is executed in a closed system.

    Using the first law of thermodynamics,

    💡
    qin​=u3​−u2​=cv​(T3​−T2​)
    💡
    qout​=u4​−u1​=cv​(T4​−T1​)

    Thermal efficiency of the ideal Otto cycle is,

    ηth(Otto)​=qin​wnet​​

    ηth(Otto)​=qin​qin​−qout​​

    ηth(Otto)​=1−qin​qout​​

    💡
    ηth(Otto)​=1−T3​−T2​T4​−T1​​

    From the isentropic relations of an ideal gas,

    T1​T2​​=(V2​V1​​)k−1

    T1​T2​​=(Vmin​Vmax​​)k−1

    T1​T2​​=rk−1

    T3​T4​​=(V4​V3​​)k−1

    T3​T4​​=(Vmax​Vmin​​)k−1

    T3​T4​​=rk−11​

    💡
    T3​T4​​=T2​T1​​ ⟶ T1​T4​​=T2​T3​​

    Rewriting the thermal efficiency equation,

    ηth(Otto)​=1−T2​T1​​(T3​/T2​−1T4​/T1​−1​)

    ηth(Otto)​=1−T2​T1​​

    💡
    ηth(Otto)​=1−rk−11​

    where r is the compression ratio V2​V1​​

    #Diesel Cycle (Compression-Ignition Engines)

    Diesel engines differ from spark-ignition engines
    in the method of initiating combustion.

    Instead of a spark plug, in a diesel engine, air is compressed to a temperature that is above the autoignition temperature of the fuel, and
    combustion starts on contact as the fuel
    is injected into this hot air.

    Process 2-3 represents the combustion process in the ideal Diesel cycle, which is approximated as a constant-pressure heat addition process.

    Fuel is injected when the piston approaches TDC
    and continues during the first part of the power stroke. Thus, the combustion process takes place
    over a longer interval.

    Using the first law of thermodynamics,

    qin​−wb,out​=u3​−u2​

    qin​=u3​−u2​+P2​(v3​−v2​)

    💡
    qin​=h3​−h2​=cp​(T3​−T2​)

    Since the heat addition process is carried out at constant pressure, there is boundary work.

    Using the first law of thermodynamics,

    💡
    qout​=u4​−u1​=cv​(T4​−T1​)

    Thermal efficiency of the ideal Diesel cycle is,

    ηth(Diesel)​=1−qin​qout​​

    ηth(Diesel)​=1−cp​(T3​−T2​)cv​(T4​−T1​)​

    💡
    ηth(Diesel)​=1−k(T3​−T2​)T4​−T1​​

    From the isentropic relations of an ideal gas,

    T1​T2​​=(V2​V1​​)k−1

    T1​T2​​=(Vmin​Vmax​​)k−1

    T1​T2​​=rk−1

    T3​T4​​=(V4​V3​​)k−1

    T3​T4​​=(V2​V3​​⋅V4​V2​​)k−1

    T3​T4​​=(V2​V3​​⋅Vmax​Vmin​​)k−1

    T3​T4​​=(rrc​​)k−1

    Combining the two equations,

    T3​T4​​=T2​/T1​(rc​)k−1​

    T1​T4​​=T2​T3​​(rc​)k−1

    T1​T4​​=V2​V3​​(rc​)k−1

    T1​T4​​=rc​(rc​)k−1

    P3​=P2​

    V3​mRT3​​=V2​mRT2​​

    V2​V3​​=T2​T3​​

    rc​=T2​T3​​

    💡
    T1​T4​​=rc​k

    where rc​ is the cutoff ratio V2​V3​​

    Rewriting the thermal efficiency equation,

    ηth(Diesel)​=1−kT2​T1​​(T3​/T2​−1T4​/T1​−1​)

    ηth(Diesel)​=1−kT2​T1​​(rc​−1rc​k−1​)

    💡
    ηth(Diesel)​=1−k(rk−1)1​(rc​−1rc​k−1​)

    Since cutoff ratio rc​>1, for a given compression ratio r, thermal efficiency ηth(Diesel)​<ηth(Otto)​.

    #Gas Turbine Engines

    Gas turbines usually operate on an open cycle
    as fresh ambient air is drawn into the engine and
    exhaust gases are expelled instead of recirculated.

    The open cycle can be modeled as a closed cycle by replacing the combustion and exhaust processes with
    a constant-pressure heat addition/rejection process.

    The ideal cycle that the working fluid undergoes in
    this closed loop is the Brayton cycle. The processes
    are executed in steady-flow devices, and as such, should be analyzed as steady-flow processes.

    #Brayton Cycle

    Using the first law of thermodynamics,

    💡
    qin​=h3​−h2​=cp​(T3​−T2​)
    💡
    qout​=h4​−h1​=cp​(T4​−T1​)

    Backwork ratio is,

    💡
    rbw​=wtur​wcomp​​=h3​−h4​h2​−h1​​

    Percentage of work output used to drive compressor.

    Thermal efficiency of the ideal Brayton cycle is,

    💡
    ηth(Brayton)​=1−T3​−T2​T4​−T1​​

    Actual cycle differs from the ideal Brayton cycle in that there is some pressure drop during the heat addition/rejection process.

    Also, actual work input to the compressor is more
    and the actual work output from the turbine is less due to irreversibilities. Deviation can be accounted
    for by utilizing isentropic efficiencies of the turbine and compressor.

    Using isentropic relations of an ideal gas,

    T1​T2​​=(P1​P2​​)kk−1​

    T1​T2​​=rp​kk−1​

    T3​T4​​=(P3​P4​​)kk−1​

    T3​T4​​=rp​(k−1)/k1​

    💡
    T3​T4​​=T2​T1​​ ⟶ T1​T4​​=T2​T3​​

    Rewriting the thermal efficiency equation,

    ηth(Brayton)​=1−T2​T1​​(T3​/T2​−1T4​/T1​−1​)

    ηth(Brayton)​=1−T2​T1​​

    ηth(Brayton)​=1−rp​(k−1)/k1​

    where rp​ is the pressure ratio P1​P2​​

    For an isentropic process, PVk=const.

    rp​=P1​P2​​=(V2​V1​​)k=rk

    💡
    ηth(Brayton)​=1−rk−11​

    Thermal efficiency equation for a Brayton
    cycle is the same as that for the Otto cycle.

    #Brayton Cycle with Regeneration

    In gas-turbine engines, the temperature of the exhaust gases leaving the turbine is often higher than the temperature of the air leaving the compressor. Thus, the high-pressure compressed air can be heated further by transferring heat from the exhaust gases to it.

    The use of a regenerator is recommended only when the turbine exhaust temperature is higher than the compressor exit temperature. Otherwise, heat will flow in the reverse direction, decreasing the efficiency.

    Thermal efficiency increases as a result since regeneration decreases the heat input (fuel) requirements for the same net work output.

    Effectiveness of a regenerator is,

    💡
    ϵ=qregen(ideal)​qregen(actual)​​=h4​−h2​h5​−h2​​

    Extent to which a regenerator approaches an
    ideal regenerator is called the effectiveness.

    Achieving a higher effectiveness requires the use of a larger regenerator, which carries a
    higher price tag and causes a larger pressure drop. Thus, the use of a regenerator with
    a very high effectiveness cannot be justified economically unless the savings from the
    fuel costs exceed the additional expenses involved.

    Thermal efficiency with a regenerator is,

    💡
    ηth(Brayton+Regen)​=T3​−T5​(T3​−T4​)−(T2​−T1​)​

    For an ideal regenerator, T5​=T4​.

    ηth(Brayton+Regen)​=1−T3​−T4​T2​−T1​​

    ηth(Brayton+Regen)​=1−T3​T1​​(1−T4​/T3​T2​/T1​−1​)

    ηth(Brayton+Regen)​=1−T3​T1​​⋅1−rp​(k−1)/k1​rp​(k−1)/k−1​

    💡
    ηth(Brayton+Regen)​=1−T3​T1​​⋅rp​(k−1)/k

    #Brayton Cycle with Intercooling,
    Reheating and Regeneration

    Net work of a gas turbine is the difference between the turbine work output and the compressor work input, which can be increased by either decreasing compressor work or increasing turbine work.

    Work input decreases when the compression
    process is carried out in stages and the gas
    is cooled in between — intercooling.

    Similarly, work output increases when the gas
    is allowed to expand in stages and is reheated
    in between — reheating.

    The steady-flow compression/expansion work is proportional to the specific volume of the fluid.
    Thus, the specific volume should be as low as
    possible during compression and vice versa.

    Intercooling and reheating:

    • Gas exits compressor at a lower temperature
    • Gas exits turbine at a higher temperature
    • Greater potential for regeneration

    💡
    ηth(Brayton)​=qin​wtur​−wcomp​​

    qin​ is minimized by regeneration
    wtur​ is maximized by reheating
    wcomp​ is minimized by intercooling

    #Jet Propulsion

    Aircraft gas turbines operate on an open cycle called a jet-propulsion cycle, which differs
    from the Brayton cycle in that the gases are not expanded to the ambient pressure in the turbine. Instead, they are expanded to a pressure such that the power produced by the
    turbine is just sufficient to drive the compressor and auxiliary equipment.

    The net work output of a jet-propulsion cycle is zero. The gases that exit the turbine are subsequently accelerated in a nozzle to provide the thrust to propel the aircraft. Also, the
    fluid passes through a diffuser first, where it is decelerated and its pressure is increased
    before it enters the compressor.

    Description of the ideal turbojet cycle:

    • Pressure of air rises slightly as it is decelerated in the diffuser
    • Air is compressed isentropically by the compressor
    • Air is mixed with fuel in the combustion chamber
    • Air-fuel mixture is then burned at constant pressure
    • Exhaust gases expand partially in the turbine
    • Gases expand in the nozzle to ambient pressure
    • Gases exit the engine at a high velocity

    In the ideal case, turbine work is assumed to be equal to compressor work. The processes in
    the diffuser, compressor, turbine and nozzle are assumed to be isentropic. Irreversibilities associated with the steady-flow devices will reduce the thrust produced by the engine.

    Difference in the momentum of inlet air and exhaust gases creates thrust.

    💡
    Fthrust​=m˙(Ve​−Vi​)

    The velocities are relative to the aircraft and m˙ is taken as the mass flow rate of air.

    The propulsive power developed from the thrust of the engine is,

    💡
    W˙prop​=Fthrust​⋅Vaircraft​

    The propulsive efficiency of an engine is,

    💡
    ηprop​=Q˙​in​W˙prop​​

    The net work of a turbojet engine is zero. Thus, the efficiency of a turbojet engine cannot be defined in the same way as a stationary gas-turbine engine. Instead, it should be defined as
    the ratio of the desired output to the required input.
