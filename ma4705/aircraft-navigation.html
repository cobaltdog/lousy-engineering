    Aircraft Navigation

    #Map Projections

    Mercator projection:

    • Cylindrical map projection
    • Lines of constant course are represented as straight segments
    • Distorts the size and shape of large objects as scale increases from Equator to the poles

    Latitudes (horizontal lines) and longitude (vertical lines) are shown as straight lines.
    At higher latitudes, features are exaggerated due to non-uniform scale of the map.

    Lambert conformal conic projection:

    • Latitudes are curved concave toward the nearest Pole
    • Longitudes are straight lines converging toward the nearest Pole
    • Angle between latitudes and longitudes are accurately represented
    • Scale is constant throughout the chart, thus actual land shapes are preserved

    #Aeronautical Terms

    A nautical mile is one minute of an arc of a great circle of Earth.

    💡

    1 nmi≈6080 ft≈1852 m

    A great circle is the shortest distance between two points on a surface of a sphere.

    Latitude and longitude:

    • Reference system to pinpoint locations on Earth
    • Latitude measures north and south of the equator
    • Longitude measures east and west of the prime meridian
    • Prime meridian is the vertical line representing a longitude of 0°

    Latitude and longitude are expressed in terms of degrees, minutes, and tenth of a minute.

    💡

    [Direction] [Degrees] [Minutes] [Tenths]

    The airport at Miami, Florida is located 25 degrees north of the equator and 80 degrees west
    of the prime meridian. Its location is N2547.1 and W08016.6, and interpreted as 25 degrees
    north and 47.1 minutes from the equator, and 80 degrees west and 16.6 minutes west from
    the prime (zero) meridian.

    Compass directions:

    • Measurement in clockwise direction
    • Runways are laid out according to the numbers on a compass
    • Runway with a marking of “14” has a direction of approximately 140°
    • Runway referred to as “09-27” is oriented east-west which represents 90°-270°

    Airways:

    • An airway is a designated space of air through which aircraft are directed to fly
    • Controlled airspace maintains a safe distance between aircraft (horizontal and vertical)
    • Required to maintain horizontal distance of 5 nmi between 2 aircraft at the same altitude
    • Below 29,000ft vertical separation must be maintained at a minimum of 1,000ft
    • Above 29,000ft vertical separation must be maintained at a minimum of 2,000ft
    • Reduced vertical separation minima (RVSM) condition — separation of 500ft

    Dead reckoning:

    • Basic navigational method
    • Used by low speed, small aircraft pilots
    • Relies on mathematical calculations to plot a course
    • Calculate time-speed-distance, fuel consumption, density altitude, etc.
    • Calculate current position of a moving object using a previously known position

    A “fix” is a position in the sky reached by an aircraft following a specific route.

    • Pilots can compute the flight time flying from one fix to the next
    • If pilot reaches the fix at the computed time, aircraft is on course
    • “Fixes” (positions) are based on the last known/calculated position

    #Autonomous Navigation Systems

    #Magnetic Compass

    Magnetic compass:

    • Sensing magnet (float) aligns itself with the direction of magnetic North
    • Numerical compass card is constructed around the perimeter of the float
    • Magnetic deviation — electromagnetic influences of aircraft’s electrical system
    • Magnetic variation — difference in location of magnetic North and geographic North

    Magnetic compassing:

    • Declination — angle difference between headings of magnetic North and true North
    • Inclination — angle of magnetic field above or below the horizontal axis

    #Remote Indicating Compass

    A better solution to magnetic deviation is to remotely locate the
    magnetic compass in a wing tip or vertical stabilizer where there
    is very little interference with the Earth’s magnetic field.

    Remote indicating slaved gyro compass (flux gate compass):

    • Combines the use of a gyro, magnetic compass, and remote indicating system
    • Flux valve/gate senses the Earth’s magnetic field and thus, aircraft direction
    • Gyro’s measurement is mainly during quick changes in direction as the flux
      gate requires time to settle before obtaining an accurate measurement

    #Inertial Navigation System (INS)

    Inertial navigation system:

    • Self-contained system (does not require input radio signals from ground navigation)
    • Uses accelerometers and gyroscopes to track position and orientation of an aircraft
    • Derives attitude, velocity and other information directly from aircraft’s acceleration
    • Dead reckoning computer (calculate current position based on start position)

    Inertial navigation systems do accumulate error over time. Thus, they are integrated with
    GPS. The INS “fills in the gaps” where GPS signal is unreliable, ensuring that the location, orientation, and velocity of the aircraft can still be measured accurately.

    Components of an INS:

    • Navigation unit — contains an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) and a computer
    • Control display unit
    • Mode select panel
    • Battery

    A computer performs navigation equations. It integrates the sensed acceleration to give velocity, which is integrated again to give position. To integrate in the correct direction, attitude is required, which is obtained by integrating the sensed angular velocity.

    Gimbaled INS:

    • Accelerometers are mounted on a stable platform in gimbals
    • Accelerometers oriented North-South, East-West, Up-Down
    • Mechanical gyroscopes keep the platform stable and level
    • Less reliable due to wear and tear which affects accuracy
    • Biggest issue is “gimbal lock” when two gimbals align

    Strapdown INS:

    • Sensors are mounted on a platform that changes orientation with the aircraft
    • 3 gyroscopes that sense the rate of roll, pitch, and yaw to obtain orientation
    • More reliable due to fewer moving parts and simpler design
    • Require more accurate gyroscopes and computers

    Error in INS:

    • Integration drift — small errors in the measurement of acceleration are integrated into progressively larger errors in velocity, then compounded into larger errors in position
    • Position provided by INS must be periodically corrected by input from other systems
    • Use of multiple navigation systems allows a higher degree of accuracy to be achieved

    Advantages of INS:

    • Autonomous — does not rely on external aids or high visibility conditions
    • Immune to jamming — does not receive or emit detectable radiation
    • Stealthy — requires no external antenna (lower radar signature)

    Disadvantages of INS:

    • Mean-squared navigation errors increase with time
    • High acquisition and operating cost — requires time to initialize position and attitude
    • High maintenance cost — electromechanical tends to fail more than electronics
    • Weight, power requirements, and heat dissipation (scales with size of the system)

    #Doppler Navigation

    Doppler navigation:

    • Self-contained dead-reckoning system
    • Based on the Doppler Shift principle where Δf=cvf​
    • Measures the frequency difference between the transmitted waves
      and reflected waves to calculate the forward velocity of the aircraft

    #Radio Navigation Systems

    #Automatic Direction Finder (ADF)

    Automatic direction finder:

    • Rely on directional property of radio waves for navigation
    • Operates in the frequency 190kHz - 1.75MHz (medium wave)
    • Consists of a rotatable dipole antenna and a sense antenna, and receiver

    In the early days, ADF made extensive use of commercial radio stations as their non-directional beacons (NDB) for navigation. Accuracy of an NDB is ±5° for approach
    and     ±10° for enroute. Higher accuracy for approach is due to lower flight speed
    of the aircraft.

    Principle of operation:

    • Rotatable loop antenna scans for the highest signal strength from transmitter
    • Loop antenna ambiguity — two possible directions of highest signal strength
    • Sense antenna, which is omnidirectional, is used to resolve this ambiguity
    • Polar diagram of loop antenna is represented by two small circles
    • Polar diagram of sense antenna is represented by one large circle
    • Combined polar diagram of both antennas is cardioid shape
    • Indent on cardioid is the actual direction of the transmitter

    It is easier to detect the direction of lowest reception (null point) as the rate of change
    of signal strength near the null point is much higher. By rotating a loop antenna, the direction of the transmitter can be identified.

    Antennas and receiver:

    • In the past, need to manually rotate the loop antenna to identify direction
    • Modern aircraft uses two loop antennas placed in a “+” configuration
    • One antenna aligned along x-axis, and the other aligned along y-axis
    • Receiver receives loop sine signal, loop cosine signal, and sense signal
    • Signals are sent to a digital bus (ARINC 429)

    Information display:

    • Receiver outputs to indicator displaying magnetic heading and bearing to NDB
    • Newer aircraft uses radio magnetic indicators (RMI) which are multipurpose
    • RMI can display both ADF and VOR information

    ADF system performance:

    • ADF signals are transmitted by ground waves and sky waves
    • Ground waves — limited by line-of-sight, attenuation of signal
    • Sky waves — reflected waves by the ionosphere can cause errors
    • Frequency band of ADF is susceptible to static discharges and electrical equipment

    ADF errors:

    • Ionosphere error
      • Specifically during sunset and sunrise due to changes in the F layer
      • Ionosphere reflects signals and causes fluctuations in the needle (fading)
    • Electrical interference
      • Areas of high electrical activity (thunderstorms)
      • Needle will point towards source of electrical activity
    • Terrain error
      • Mountains can cause bending/reflection of signals
      • Results in erroneous readings in such areas
    • Bank error
      • When aircraft is banking, position of antennas is compromised
      • Antennas are most accurate when oriented in the horizontal plane
    • Shoreline error
      • Refraction of ground waves as they pass over land to water and vice versa
      • Bending of the signals results in erroneous readings

    #VHF Omnidirectional Range (VOR)

    VHF omnidirectional range:

    • Primary short-range aircraft navigation system
    • Operates in the VHF band 108MHz - 117.95MHz
    • Transmission of VHF signals is only via space waves (requires LOS)

    Ground waves suffer from signal attenuation such that VHF signals can only travel short distances. Transmission via sky waves is impossible as the ionosphere does not reflect signals within the VHF range. Ionosphere reflects signals of 300kHz - 30MHz.

    VOR stations:

    • VOR is usually paired with a DME station
    • VOR operates in the same frequency range as ILS
    • ILS channels occupy the odd tenths of each 0.5MHz increment (108.10, 108.15)
    • VOR channels occupy the even tenths of each 0.5MHz increment (108.20, 108.40)

    Principle of operation:

    • VOR beacon transmits two signals — reference phase and variable phase
    • Reference signal is omnidirectional, and variable signal is directional
    • Aircraft will receive each signal at a different time (phase difference)
    • Phase difference corresponds to number of degrees from magnetic North
    • VOR only informs the pilot on which radial aircraft is on (1 degree = 1 radial)
    • VOR does not give positional or heading information of the aircraft

    Effective range of VOR,

    💡

    d=1.1h​

    where d≡ range in nautical miles, and h≡ aircraft altitude in feet

    Course deviation indicator:

    • TO/FROM indicates whether aircraft is flying towards or away from VOR station
    • If the aircraft is flying above the VOR station, the indicator shows “OFF” status
    • Course select knob — where the pilot wants to go (not accounting for wind etc.)
    • Course deviation bar — how much the aircraft deviates from the selected course

    VOR system performance:

    • Relatively simple and thus extremely reliable system
    • When two VOR radials intersect, they provide a unique navigation position “fix”

    #Distance Measuring Equipment (DME)

    Distance measuring equipment:

    • Measures the “slant range” of the aircraft from the DME station
    • Slant range incorporates ground range and altitude of aircraft
    • Distance reading more accurate the lower the aircraft’s altitude
    • Gives inaccurate reading when aircraft is directly above the station

    Principle of operation:

    • Interrogator (aircraft) transmits a pair of pulses to the station
    • Station then transmits a new pair of pulses 50ms after reception
    • New pulse pair is transmitted above/below interrogator’s frequency
    • Delay between transmission and reception gives the slant distance

    #Global Positioning System (GPS)

    Global positioning system:

    • Provide time, space, position, and velocity information
    • GPS signal is available anywhere, anytime in any weather condition
    • Constellation of satellites ensure 5 are visible at anywhere and anytime
    • Satellites can cover large areas, reception not affected by terrain/geography

    #Range and Pseudo-Range

    GPS Range (true range):

    • Actual geometric distance between GPS satellite and receiver
    • Based on the speed of light and time taken for signal to travel
    • Requires satellite and receiver clocks to be perfectly synchronized

    Pseudo-range (approximate):

    • Approximation of range between GPS satellite and receiver
    • In practice, satellite and receiver do not have perfectly synchronized clocks
    • Imperfect synchronization introduces a timing error, which affects accuracy

    Correcting for clock error:

    • Receiver calculates a common time bias Δt from at least 4 satellites
    • Time bias will affect all the range measurements by an equal amount
    • True range can be solved using the equation PR=TR+cΔt

    #Range Errors

    Types of errors:

    • Satellite clock error — atomic clocks lose about 3ns a day
    • Satellite ephemeris — satellite isn’t where we thought it was (usually very small)
    • Receiver error — caused by electrical noise, computation errors
    • Multipath error — caused by reflection of radio waves (especially near the ground)

    #Geometric Dilution of Precision (GDOP)

    GDOP is the uncertainties in the calculation of the position of a receiver,
    which may depend on the relative position of the satellites involved.

    Components of GDOP:

    • TDOP — time dilution due to clock error
    • PDOP — position dilution due to geometry of satellites’ orbits
      • HDOP — horizontal component of position dilution of precision
      • VDOP — vertical component of position dilution of precision

    #Instrument Landing System (ILS)

    Instrument landing system:

    • Ground-based navigation aid to guide aircraft during final approach and landing
    • Localizer — provides lateral guidance (ensures alignment with runway centerline)
    • Glideslope — provides vertical guidance (helps pilot maintain correct descent angle)

    System operation:

    • ILS only works for a single approach path
    • Disturbance of localizer/glideslope beam causes autopilot to disengage (go-around)

    Localizer antenna:

    • Located at the far end of the runway
    • Operates in the same VHF band as VOR
    • Transmits two lobes to the left and right of runway modulated at 90Hz and 150Hz
    • Combined signal strength at runway centerline will be equal (aircraft is aligned)
    • Also transmits a 3-letter morse code audio identification for crew to hear

    Glideslope antenna:

    • Operates in the VHF band 328.6MHz - 335MHz at 150kHz spacing
    • Transmits two lobes above and below the correct descent angle
    • Lobes are modulated with 90Hz and 150Hz respectively
    • Lobes overlap to produce a glide path of 2.5° - 3.5°
    • GS frequency is automatically selected when LOC frequency is tuned

    Marker beacons:

    • 3 marker beacons that transmit at 75MHz
    • Outer — 6 to 12km from runway, 400Hz audio tone
    • Middle — 3500ft from runway, 1300Hz audio tone, aircraft 200ft above runway
    • Inner — 200 to 1500ft from runway, 3kHz audio tone

    As an aircraft flies over the outer marker, a blue/purple light is illuminated in the cockpit.
    As the aircraft flies over the middle marker, an amber light is illuminated in the cockpit.
    As the aircraft flies over the inner marker, a white light is illuminated in the cockpit.

    Automatic approach and landing:

    • Autoland only for Cat 2 and Cat 3
      (approved by regulatory authorities)
    • 3 autopilot channels must be engaged before autoland can be attempted

    #GBAS Landing System (GLS)

    GBAS landing system:

    • Alternative system to instrument landing system (ILS)
    • Makes use of the Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS)
    • Ground-based augmentation system (GBAS) augments GPS data from GNSS
    • Ground facility uses VHF data link to provide GPS corrections, approach info
    • GLS — select a 5-digit channel number, ILS — tune to a specific radio frequency

    System operation:

    • GBAS ground facility receives signals from GPS satellites
    • GBAS consists of reference antennas in known surveyed positions
    • Reference receivers estimate distance travelled by the signal to antenna
    • GBAS compares the estimated distance with the actual distance based on
      broadcast satellite position and the true position of the reference receiver
    • Average error measured by all reference receivers is the correction to be applied
    • Ground facility transmits GPS corrections to all aircraft within its vicinity
    • Aircraft uses the corrections to improve the accuracy of their GPS data

    Ground facility typically has 3 or more GPS antennas and a VHF data broadcast (VDB) transmitter, which are locally situated on the airport. GBAS avionics consists of a GPS antenna, VHF antenna, and receivers. Multi-mode receiver (MMR) technology allows simultaneous implementation of GPS, GBAS, and ILS using common antennas.

    Advantages of GBAS:

    • Greater flexibility — different precision approach paths can be flown
    • Non-linear approaches — varying descent angles along approach path
    • One GBAS can support an area within 23nmi of the GBAS reference point
    • Uses less of the VHF spectrum, with a single frequency supporting all approaches
    • Proper GBAS siting can eliminate critical areas that impede aircraft movement

    Satellite-based augmentation system (SBAS):

    • Covers a wider area than GBAS due to its use of several geostationary satellites
    • Provide integrity assurance, increase accuracy and reduce position errors to <1m

    #Area Navigation (RNAV)

    Area navigation:

    • Combining or filtering inputs from multiple navigation sensors to define position
    • Permits operation of aircraft on any desired flight path, allows its position to be
      continuously determined wherever it is rather than only along tracks between
      individual ground navigation aids
    • Facilitates navigation along any path within range of navigation aids
    • Flight path can be planned with autonomous navigation equipment

    Required navigation performance (RNP):

    • Family of navigation specifications under Performance Based Navigation (PBN)
    • Permit operation of aircraft along a precise flight path with a high level of accuracy
      and the ability to determine an aircraft’s position with both accuracy and integrity

    Sensor inputs to RNAV:

    • VOR/DME — provides a waypoint in space (flexible and efficient flight planning)
    • Path between waypoints is a leg, and multiple legs form a route
    • IRS/GPS — inertial navigation system
    • IRS accumulates errors over time, GPS periodically zero off the IRS errors

    Benefits of RNAV:

    • Customized routes to avoid congested airspace
    • Optimized routes that bypasses ground nav aids
    • Shorter distance — fly direct to waypoint if cleared by ATC
    • Increased airspace utilization — flying parallel tracks with lateral separation

    #Air Traffic Management (ATM)

    Divisions under air traffic management:

    • Air traffic control (ATC)
    • Air traffic flow management (ATFM)
    • Airspace management (ASM)

    Procedures for air navigation services:

    • Provide adequate air traffic services — communications, navigation, surveillance
    • Ensure an acceptable level of safety in the provision of these air traffic services
    • Facilitate the harmonization of air traffic services in adjacent airspaces
    • Implement formal and systematic safety management program

    Air traffic control:

    • Prevent collisions
    • Organize and expedite the flow of air traffic
    • Provide information and support for pilots
    • Key objectives — safety, capacity, and efficiency

    Challenges:

    • Continued traffic growth results in an imbalance in demand and capacity
    • Necessity to incorporate measures to minimize environmental impact
    • Limited airspace as a national treasure
    • Ability to handle weather related issues
    • Integration with airport operations

    #Separation

    • Separation — maintain a specific minimum distance between aircraft
    • Clearance — permission given by ATC to proceed under certain conditions
    • Traffic information — information on other aircraft likely to pose a hazard to flight
    • Flight rules — visual flight rules (VFR), instrument flight rules (IRF), special VFR (SVFR)

    Vertical separation:

    • Below 29,000ft — vertical separation of at least 1,000ft
    • Above 29,000ft — vertical separation of at least 2,000ft
    • Reduced vertical separation minima (RVSM) — 29,000ft and 41,000ft, aircraft with
      modern altimeter & autopilot systems can be vertically separated by at least 1,000ft

    Horizontal separation:

    • Procedural separation — based on position of aircraft as reported by pilots
    • Procedural separation does not require radar to provide information to ATC
    • Lateral separation — based on position of aircraft that is derived visually
    • Position obtained from dead reckoning, internal nav sources, or beacons

    Longitudinal separation:

    • For aircraft that are following the same route
    • Based upon time or distance as measured by DME
    • No two aircraft must come within 15min flying time of each other
