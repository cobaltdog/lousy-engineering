    #Question 2

    Explain the principle of radio ranging.

    • Use of radio waves to determine the range of a target
    • Pulse of radio waves is transmitted towards the target
    • Receiver detects the radio waves reflected by the target
    • Range can be determined based on travel time of waves

    What are the characteristics of radio waves propagation which can
    limit the use of terrestrial radio navigation and communication?

    • Reflection of radio waves is dependent on the ionosphere
      • Properties of ionosphere vary from day to night
      • Solar activity can disrupt transmission of radio waves
    • Refraction of radio waves due to different electron density
    • Scattering of radio waves results in weak signals arriving at the receiver

    #Question 3

    Explain the term “Dead Reckoning” used in aircraft navigation.
    Identify a navigation system which uses it and state its pros and cons.

    Dead reckoning:

    • Used to determine current position based on last known position
    • Estimate distance travelled based on elapsed time, last known speed and direction

    Inertial navigation/reference system (INS/IRS):

    • Self-contained autonomous system that does not require external aids
    • Stealthy system as it does not require the use of external antennas
    • Immune to jamming
    • Mean-square error increases with time due to integration drift
    • Drift needs to be corrected periodically by inputs from other systems
    • High acquisition, operating and maintenance costs
    • Heavy system due to the use of heavy gyros

    #Question 6

    #Part (a)

    Explain the principle of GPS used in commercial aircraft.

    • Determine a target’s position using trilateration
    • Minimum 3 GPS satellites transmit GPS signals to the target
    • Signals carry satellite position data and time data of when signal was sent
    • Distance of each satellite to the target is determined from travel time of signal
    • Calculated distance can be interpreted as a 2D circle with satellite at the center
    • Region where the 3 circles (3 satellites) overlap gives the location of the target

    #Part (b)

    Why is normal radio ranging not used in GPS?

    • Requires both satellite and target to have perfectly synced clocks
    • Weather, time of day, ionospheric effects can cause inaccuracies
    • Range can be distorted due to reflection of radio waves (multipath)
    • Reflected radio waves may be too weak to be picked up due to attenuation
    • To ensure reflected waves can be received, high power transmitter is required

    Explain the principle and use of “pseudo-ranging” in GPS.

    • Approximate range between satellite and target
    • Does not require satellite and target to have perfectly synced clocks
    • Receiver calculates a common time bias Δt from at least 4 GPS satellites
    • Time bias Δt will affect all range measurements by an equal amount
    • True range can be solved using the equation TR=PR+cΔt

    #Part (c)

    Explain how GBAS reduce errors to enable precision landing of aircraft.

    • GBAS augments satellite data from the Global Navigation Satellite System
    • Ground facility consists of reference antennas in known surveyed positions
    • Reference receivers calculate the estimated distance travelled by a GPS signal
    • Receivers compare the estimated distance to the actual distance based on the satellite’s broadcast position and the true position of the reference receiver
    • Difference between estimated and actual is the error to be corrected
    • Ground facility uses VHF data links to provide GPS corrections to aircraft
    • Aircraft makes use of this data to correct their own GPS measurements
    • An aircraft’s position along the approach path can be precisely known

    #Question 7

    #Part (a)

    Describe the operation of a typical Instrument Landing System (ILS).

    • Ground navigation aid to guide aircraft along an approach path
    • Localizer provides lateral guidance, glideslope provides vertical guidance
    • Localizer transmits two lobes to the left and right of the runway centerline
    • Lobes overlap along the centerline to align aircraft to the centerline
    • Glideslope transmits two lobes with one lobe oriented above the other
    • Lobes overlap at the desired descent angle to guide aircraft along desired path
    • Along approach path are 3 marker beacons to inform pilot range to the runway

    #Part (b)

    Describe the function of the different categories of automatic landing using ILS.

    Fail operational system:

    • Aircraft must be equipped with at least 3 autopilots
    • During approach, 2 of the 3 autopilots must be engaged
    • In the event of 1 autopilot failure, the other will continue the approach
    • Having a 2nd autopilot engaged ensures redundancy during autoland
    • This redundancy allows for a “no decision height” approach

    Fail passive system:

    • During approach, only 1 autopilot is engaged
    • In the event of failure, aircraft will not deviate from approach path
    • Pilot must then immediately assume control of the aircraft
    • If sufficient visual reference of the runway, continue landing
    • If insufficient visual reference of the runway, carry out missed approach
    • Lowest allowable decision height of 50ft

    #Part (c)

    What does the term “fly-by-wire (FBW)” mean?
    What are the advantages and concerns of operating FBW aircraft?

    • Pilot makes control inputs via conventional controls (yoke, rudder pedals, etc.)
    • Transducers convert pilot inputs into analog signals for transmission to ACE
    • Actuator control electronics (ACE) convert analog signals into digital signals
    • Digital signals are transmitted to the Primary Flight Computer (PFC)
    • PFC ensures inputs are correct and fall within the flight envelope
    • PFC sends it back to ACE for conversion into analog signals
    • Analog signals are sent to control units of hydraulic actuators
    • Control units power the actuators to deflect control surfaces

    Advantages of FBW:

    • Weight reduction — mechanical systems are heavy due to use of cables, pulleys
    • Automation — flight control systems are more easily integrated with autopilot
    • Pilot assist — prevent inputs that exceed structural/aerodynamic constraints
    • Pilot assist — automatically adjust trim during flight to ensure stability
    • Stability — capable of making rapid adjustments to maintain stability
    • Maintenance — less moving parts results in improved reliability

    Concerns of FBW:

    • Adequate redundancy in the event of electrical failure (loss of control)
    • FBW aircraft typically have mechanical backup system for basic flight control
    • Loss of tactile feedback as control surfaces are not directly linked to the yoke
    • Complex electronics and software increase development and certification costs

    #Part (a)

    What are the limitations of Continuous Wave (CW) radar?
    How are these limitations overcome in modern radar?

    Limitations of CW (primary) radar:

    • Only provides the range and bearing of the target
    • Unable to detect stationary targets using Doppler Effect
    • Continuous transmission results in high power consumption
    • Require a large radar dish to be able to collect the weak reflected signals
    • Prone to interference from weather and terrain which impede propagation

    Pulse modulated radar:

    • Radio waves are transmitted in pulses instead of continuously
    • Receiver listen for the echo (reflected signals) in between pulses

    Secondary radar:

    • Use of a transponder located on the target (2-way communication)
      • Provide more information regarding aircraft’s flight conditions
      • Signals transmitted by transponder are stronger than reflected signals

    #Part (b)

    Describe how FMCW is used to measure height in Low Range Radar Altimeter.

    • Carrier frequency changes at a constant rate (e.g. 40MHz every 10μs)
    • Radio wave is transmitted downwards when transmitter frequency is 400MHz
    • Reflected wave is received when transmitter frequency has changed to 440MHz
    • Difference in frequencies directly relates to the travel time of radio signal
    • Height above ground is half of the total distance travelled by the signal

    #Part (c)

    Explain how pulse width and pulse repetition frequency (PRF)
    affect blind area and range discrimination of a pulse radar.

    Pulse width (duration):

    • Pulse width is the length of time the transmitter is switched on per pulse
    • High pulse duration results in a larger blind area around the transmitter
      as radar is still transmitting and reflected signals from targets close to
      the radar will not be received (targets will not be detected)
    • High pulse duration results in lower accuracy in target discrimination;
      two close targets will encounter the same pulse and cause reflected
      signals from each to overlap, which will appear as a single echo

    Pulse repetition frequency:

    • PRF is the number of pulses per second (pps)
    • High PRF results in a shorter interval between pulses to listen for echoes;
      targets that are too far will not be detected as interval between pulses
      is too short for the reflected signals to travel back in time, thus high
      PRF reduces the maximum range of the radar
    • High PRF results in better target discrimination as each pulse is transmitted for
      an extremely short duration such that two close targets will not encounter the
      same signal, and two distinct echoes will be received by the radar

    #Question 4

    #Part (a)

    Explain the principle and operation of the GPS used by modern commercial aircraft.
    What are the possible causes of Dilution Precision in satellite navigation?

    Global positioning system:

    • Determines position of a target using trilateration (distance measurements)
    • Multiple GPS satellites transmit signals to the target receiver
    • Signals carry satellite position data, and time data of when signal was sent
    • Receiver calculates distance to each satellite based on travel time of signal
    • Distance measured is interpreted as a 2D circle around each satellite
    • Intersection of these circles gives the position of the target

    Dilution of precision:

    • Clock error — atomic clocks lose 3ns/day
    • Multipath error — reflection of radio waves
    • Ephemeris — satellite isn’t where we think it is
    • Receiver error — noise and computation errors

    #Part (b)

    What is “pseudo-range” and why is it used in GPS instead of normal radio ranging?

    Pseudo-range:

    • Approximation of range between GPS satellite and receiver
    • Satellite and receiver do not have perfectly synchronized clocks in practice
    • Imperfect synchronization introduces a timing error which affects accuracy
    • Normal ranging requires satellite and receiver clocks to be perfectly synchronized

    Correcting for clock error:

    • Receiver calculates a common time bias Δt from at least 4 satellites
    • Time bias will affect all the range measurements by an equal amount
    • True range can be solved using the equation TR=PR+cΔt

      where TR≡ true range, PR≡ pseudo-range and c≡ speed of light

    #Question 5

    #Part (a)

    Explain the operation of ILS used in most airports today.

    Instrument landing system:

    • Ground-based navigation aid to guide aircraft during approach and landing
    • Localizer provides lateral guidance (align aircraft to runway centerline)
    • Glideslope provides vertical guidance (ensure proper descent angle)
    • 3 marker beacons located along approach path indicate position
    • Beacons transmit a morse code audio tone that is heard by the crew

    Localizer antenna:

    • Located at the far end of the runway
    • Transmits two symmetrical lobes to the left and right of runway centerline
    • At the centerline, signal strength of each lobe is equal and aircraft is aligned

    Glideslope antenna:

    • Transmits two symmetrical lobes above and below the proper descent angle
    • Lobes overlap to produce a glide path of 2.5° to 3.5°
    • At the proper descent angle, aircraft has the proper descent angle
    • Glideslope frequency is automatically selected when localizer is tuned into

    #Part (b)

    Explain the operation of GBAS landing system.
    Do a comparative evaluation of GBAS vs ILS.

    GBAS landing system:

    • Makes use of the Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS)
    • Ground-based augmentation system augments GPS data from GNSS
    • Ground facility uses VHF data links to provide GPS corrections to aircraft
    • Aircraft are typically equipped with Multi-mode receiver (MMR) that allows simultaneous implementation of GPS, GBAS, and ILS using common antennas

    System operation:

    • GBAS ground facility receives signals from GPS satellites
    • GBAS consists of reference antennas in known surveyed positions
    • Reference receivers estimate distance travelled by the signal to antenna
    • GBAS compares the estimated distance with the actual distance based on
      broadcast satellite position and the true position of the reference receiver
    • Average error measured by all reference receivers is the correction to be applied
    • Ground facility transmits GPS corrections to all aircraft within its vicinity
    • Aircraft uses the corrections to improve the accuracy of their GPS data

    GBAS vs ILS:

    • Greater flexibility — different precision approach paths can be flown
    • Non-linear approaches — varying descent angles along approach path
    • One GBAS can support an area within 23nmi of the GBAS reference point
    • Uses less of VHF spectrum, with a single frequency supporting all approaches

    ILS can only provide guidance for a single approach path as it requires a
    glideslope and a localizer antenna, which only transmits in one direction.
    Therefore, different approaches would require its own set of antennas.

    #Question 6

    #Part (a)

    Digital data buses are extensively used on modern aircraft.
    Explain the main advantages of using digital media buses.

    • Simplify maintenance — easier to diagnose failures compared to individual wiring
    • Weight savings — signals are consolidated into a bus, less point-to-point wiring
    • Integration — common network allows for centralized monitoring and control
    • Multiplexing — time/frequency division allow multiple data types on a single bus
    • Data integrity — less susceptible to noise and interference; digital buses
      also incorporates error detection and correction should there be errors
    • Flexibility — components can be upgraded with minimal changes to the bus,
      which allows for retrofitting of newer components without significant rework

    What are the challenges and advantages of using optical
    fibers in place of copper wires in digital data busses?

    Advantages of optical fibers:

    • Significantly lighter and smaller in size; translates into weight savings
    • Immune to electromagnetic interference as signals are transmitted via light
    • Wide bandwidth allows multiple signals to be multiplexed on a single fiber
    • Eliminates crosstalk (unwanted transfer of signals between channels)
      as it does not create undesired effects caused by inductive coupling
    • Low signal attenuation due to low loss characteristic of optical fibers,
      which allows the transmitter and receiver to be located further apart
    • Data security — extremely difficult to tap into existing optical fiber
    • Lower cost per unit of information transfer

    Challenges of optical fibers:

    • Difficult to join — connections required to join fibers are very expensive;
      if not joined correctly will result in light loss and thus transmission errors
    • Unable to transmit DC power through fiber, separate current-carrying
      conductors must be run alongside the fiber if power is needed
    • Proper care must be taken in installation; ensure fiber is installed with
      strain relief and an adequate bend radius to prevent damage during
      operation when the airframe expands and contracts

    #Part (b)

    Explain the Successive Approximation process used
    to convert analog signals to digital for transmission.

    Conversion process of analog to digital:

    • Approximates analog signal in successive steps until closest digital value is found
    • Comparator compares current approximation to the actual analog signal
    • Register then stores the current approximation

    Algorithm:

    • Initialization — Start with the MSB set to 1, other bits set to 0
    • Comparison — If current approx. lower than actual, keep MSB at 1
    • Iteration — At the next clock cycle, next most significant bit is set to 1
    • Comparator then compares new digital value to the actual analog signal

    Explain the principle and operation of VOR.
    What is the effective range of VOR for an aircraft flying at 40,000 ft?
    Assume the VOR station is at sea level.

    Principle of operation:

    • VOR beacon transmits two signals — reference phase and variable phase
    • Reference signal is omnidirectional, and variable signal is directional
    • Aircraft will receive each signal at a different time (phase difference)
    • Phase difference corresponds to number of degrees from magnetic North
    • VOR only informs the pilot on which radial aircraft is on (1 degree = 1 radial)
    • VOR does not give positional or heading information of the aircraft

    Effective range of VOR,

    d=1.1h​=1.140000​=220 nm

    #Part (b)

    Explain the concept of “More Electric Aircraft (MEA)”.
    How does MEA impact design, operation, and maintenance of aircraft?

    More electric aircraft:

    • Use of electrical devices instead of hydraulic/pneumatic devices
    • Use of more generators to meet the increased electrical demand
    • Actuators are replaced with more efficient and smaller electric motors
    • Heavy hydraulic/pneumatic lines are replaced with lighter electrical wiring

    Impact on design:

    • Electrical signals are susceptible to interference by EM radiation
    • Requires high-intensity radiation frequency (HIRF) protection

      as well as electromagnetic interference (EMI) protection

      • Sheathing of wires, EMI filters, etc.
    • More robust safety systems to detect electrical shorts
    • Innovative thermal management as electrical devices generate heat
    • Remote power distribution units to distribute electricity efficiently

    Impact on operation:

    • Use of lighter electric motors and wiring translate into weight savings
    • Additional capacity from weight savings allows for heavier payloads
    • Better fuel efficiency since bleed air from the engine is not required
      • In conventional aircraft, bleed air is typically used for cabin pressurization
      • In MEA, electric motors drive compressors for cabin pressurization
      • Electric anti-icing system for the wings instead of using bleed air
    • Electric motors tend to be quieter than hydraulic pumps
      • Quieter cabin which improves passenger comfort

    Impact on maintenance:

    • Fewer moving parts improve reliability and reduce downtime (time = money)
    • Damaged electrical devices can be replaced much more easily as it does not
      require draining of hydraulic fluids before maintenance can be carried out
    • Electrical devices can be an all-in-one unit, similar to an LRU
      • Plug-and-play installation which is reduces maintenance time
      • May also have fewer maintenance tasks as devices are powered electrically
      • Detaching electrical wiring as compared to disassembly of hydraulic tubing

    #Part (c)

    Describe the impact of Information Technology (IT) on aircraft design and operation.
    IT includes data buses, Aircraft Information System, Integrated Modular Avionics.

    Data buses:

    • Allow multiple systems to share a common communication channel
    • Does not rely on point-to-point wiring, which is bulky and prone to errors
    • ARINC 429 — unidirectional data flow, 1 transmitter to multiple receivers
    • ARINC 629 — multi-drop bus, allow for multiple transmitters and receivers
    • Weight savings — data bus can supply information to multiple devices
    • Improve reliability — less points of failure as compared to individual wiring
    • Simplify maintenance — Easier troubleshooting via centralized data monitoring

    Aircraft information system:

    • Paperless philosophy — information is stored on electronic libraries
      • Flight crew operating manual
      • Master minimum equipment list (MMEL)
      • Master configuration deviation list (MCDL)
      • E-documentation of maintenance procedures
    • Flight Management System (FMS)
      • Aids flight crew by eliminating routine tasks
      • Optimize flight route, manage fuel, and improve efficiency
      • Uses info from various computers and sensors, and pilot inputs to optimize
    • Aircraft Condition Monitoring System (ACMS)
      • Continuously monitors performance and detects anomalies in real-time
      • Allows for predictive maintenance by identifying potential failures
      • Minimizes unscheduled downtime due to unforeseen failures
    • Electronic Flight Instrument System (EFIS)
      • Integrates output of multiple avionics into a single display
      • Primary flight display contains info on airspeed, altitude, attitude, etc.
      • Flight director can provide guidance to the pilot on the PFD if needed

    Integrated modular avionics:

    • Shared resources for avionics through a common communication network
    • Multiple types of software can be run independently on the same hardware
    • Improve reliability — software can be run spare modules in the event of failure

    #Question 2

    #Part (a)

    Explain the operation of Primary Radar.
    Identify the different types of Primary Radar used for weather
    detection, low range radio altimeter, and Doppler navigation.

    Primary radar:

    • Operates by transmitting a radio wave (pulse) towards a target
    • Wave hits the target and reflects a portion of the signal back to radar system
    • Radar receiver detects the reflected signal to determine location, distance, speed
    • Target does not actively contribute to the signal; simply reflects radar signal back

    Types of primary radar:

    • Continuous wave (CW) Doppler radar
    • Frequency modulated continuous wave radar (FMCW)

    #Part (b)

    Explain the terms: mean power, peak power, pulse repetition frequency (PRF)
    and pulse duration in a pulse modulated radar. How does each of these affect
    the performance of the radar?

    Mean PowerAverage power output of the transmitter over the
    entire pulse duration (including transmission and
    reception of the radio waves)
    Peak PowerMaximum power transmitted during the active portion
    of the radar’s pulse (during the transmission of 1 pulse)
    PRFNumber of pulses transmitted per second (p.p.s.)
    Pulse DurationLength of time transmitted is switched on

    Effect on radar performance:

    • Peak power affects the ability to detect distant targets
      • Higher peak power allow radio waves to propagate further
      • Reflected waves from distant targets are sufficiently strong to be detected
    • PRF affects the ability to detect distant targets and discriminate between targets
      • Low PRF allows for more time to “listen” to echoes from
        distant targets between the transmission of each pulse
      • High PRF results in shorter intervals between transmission such that the
        radar may be unable to distinguish between echoes from multiple pulses
    • Pulse duration the ability to discriminate between targets
      • Longer pulse duration results in overlapping of reflected radio waves from
        two closely spaced targets such that it will appear as 1 echo on the radar

    #Part (c)

    What are the limitations of Continuous Wave (CW) radar?
    How are these limitations overcome in modern radar?

    Limitations of CW (primary) radar:

    • Only provides the range and bearing of the target
    • Unable to detect stationary targets using Doppler Effect
    • Continuous transmission results in high power consumption
    • Require a large radar dish to be able to collect the weak reflected signals
    • Prone to interference from weather and terrain which impede propagation

    Pulse modulated radar:

    • Radio waves are transmitted in pulses instead of continuously
    • Receiver listen for the echo (reflected signals) in between pulses

    Secondary radar:

    • Use of a transponder located on the target (2-way communication)
      • Provide more information regarding aircraft’s flight conditions
      • Signals transmitted by transponder are stronger than reflected signals

    #Question 3

    #Part (a)

    GPS uses the principle of trilateration, using 3 satellites to determine position.
    Explain the causes of Dilution of Precision (DOP) in satellite navigation.

    Dilution of precision:

    • Clock error — atomic clocks lose 3ns/day
    • Multipath error — reflection of radio waves
    • Ephemeris — satellite isn’t where we think it is
    • Receiver error — noise and computation errors
    • Clock synchronization error — inaccurate calculation of signal travel time

    #Part (b)

    Why is pseudo-ranging used in GPS instead of normal radio ranging?
    Explain the principle of pseudo-ranging.

    Pseudo-range:

    • Approximation of range between GPS satellite and receiver
    • Satellite and receiver do not have perfectly synchronized clocks in practice
    • Imperfect synchronization introduces a timing error which affects accuracy
    • Normal ranging requires satellite and receiver clocks to be perfectly synchronized

    Correcting for clock error:

    • Receiver calculates a common time bias Δt from at least 4 satellites
    • Time bias will affect all the range measurements by an equal amount
    • True range can be solved using the equation TR=PR+cΔt

      where TR≡ true range, PR≡ pseudo-range and c≡ speed of light

    #Part (c)

    Normal GPS resolution is not sufficient for precision approach and landing in airports. Explain how GBAS improves the resolution to make precision landing possible.

    GBAS landing system:

    • Makes use of the Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS)
    • Ground-based augmentation system augments GPS data from GNSS
    • Ground facility uses VHF data links to provide GPS corrections to aircraft
    • Aircraft are typically equipped with Multi-mode receiver (MMR) that allows simultaneous implementation of GPS, GBAS, and ILS using common antennas

    System operation:

    • GBAS ground facility receives signals from GPS satellites
    • GBAS consists of reference antennas in known surveyed positions
    • Reference receivers estimate distance travelled by the signal to antenna
    • GBAS compares the estimated distance with the actual distance based on
      broadcast satellite position and the true position of the reference receiver
    • Average error measured by all reference receivers is the correction to be applied
    • Ground facility transmits GPS corrections to all aircraft within its vicinity
    • Aircraft uses the corrections to improve the accuracy of their GPS data

    #Part (d)

    Explain the main differences between ILS and GBAS.
    What are the advantages and limitations of each system?

    Advantages of GBAS:

    • Greater flexibility — different precision approach paths can be flown
    • Non-linear approaches — varying descent angles along approach path
    • One GBAS can support an area within 23nmi of the GBAS reference point
    • Uses less of VHF spectrum, with a single frequency supporting all approaches

    Limitations of ILS:

    • Single approach path can pose an issue for airports in mountainous regions
    • ILS beam can be disturbed by aircraft or vehicles crossing the approach path
    • However, ILS is a simple and reliable system to implement

    #Question 4

    #Part (a)

    Explain the basic principle involved in navigating an aircraft from 1 point to another.
    (Hint: Explain Great Circle, dead reckoning, terrestrial and satellite radio navigation.)

    Dead reckoning:

    • Used to determine current position based on last known position
    • Estimate distance travelled based on elapsed time, last known speed & direction

    Inertial Navigation System (INS):

    • Dead reckoning computer
    • Self-contained system (does not require input radio signals from ground aids)
    • Uses accelerometers and gyroscopes to track position and orientation of aircraft
    • Derives attitude, velocity and other info directly from aircraft’s acceleration
    • Determines the current position of aircraft based on last known position

    Automatic Direction Finder (ADF):

    • Rely on directional property of radio waves for navigation
    • Rotatable loop antenna scans for highest signal strength from transmitter
    • Sense antenna resolves direction ambiguity of the rotatable loop antenna
    • Polar diagram is in the shape of a cardioid (indent indicates the direction)
    • Pilot tunes to ADF ground stations and fly from one station to another

    VHF Omnidirectional Range (VOR):

    • VOR beacon transmits two signals — reference phase and variable phase
    • Reference signal is omnidirectional, and variable signal is directional
    • Aircraft will receive each signal at a different time (phase difference)
    • Phase difference corresponds to number of degrees from magnetic North
    • Pilot selects the desired course and fly to/from the VOR station
    • Indicator shows how far the aircraft deviates from selected course

    Global Positioning System (GPS):

    • Aircraft uses GPS to determine its own position and continuously updates it
    • Receiver onboard aircraft calculates distance to each satellite
    • Position of the aircraft can be obtained using trilateration
    • GPS data can be used to zero off integration drift of INS
    • Pilot enters the GPS coordinates of waypoints along the desired flight route

    #Part (b)

    Explain the terms: heading, track, drift, true air speed, and ground speed.

    • Heading — direction in which the aircraft’s nose is pointing towards
    • Track — actual flight path of the aircraft (corrected for drift due to wind)
    • Drift — angle between heading and drift (wind pushes aircraft off course)
    • TAS — speed of the aircraft relative to the surrounding air mass
    • GS — speed of the aircraft relative to the ground

    #Part (b)

    Explain the concept of Fly-by-Wire (FBW) aircraft.
    What are the advantages and concerns of FBW aircraft?

    • Pilot makes control inputs via conventional controls (yoke, rudder pedals, etc.)
    • Transducers convert pilot inputs into analog signals for transmission to ACE
    • Actuator control electronics (ACE) convert analog signals into digital signals
    • Digital signals are transmitted to the Primary Flight Computer (PFC)
    • PFC ensures inputs are correct and fall within the flight envelope
    • PFC sends it back to ACE for conversion into analog signals
    • Analog signals are sent to control units of hydraulic actuators
    • Control units power the actuators to deflect control surfaces

    Advantages of FBW:

    • Weight reduction — mechanical systems are heavy due to use of cables, pulleys
    • Automation — flight control systems are more easily integrated with autopilot
    • Pilot assist — prevent inputs that exceed structural/aerodynamic constraints
    • Pilot assist — automatically adjust trim during flight to ensure stability
    • Stability — capable of making rapid adjustments to maintain stability
    • Maintenance — less moving parts results in improved reliability

    Concerns of FBW:

    • Adequate redundancy in the event of electrical failure (loss of control)
    • FBW aircraft typically have mechanical backup system for basic flight control
    • Loss of tactile feedback as control surfaces are not directly linked to the yoke
    • Complex electronics and software increase development and certification costs

    #Part (c)

    Explain the main tasks of the Flight Management System.
    How has the introduction of FMS impacted aircraft operation?

    Flight Management System (FMS):

    • Automatic flight control
      • Aids the flight crew by eliminating routine tasks and manual computations
      • Can be coupled with flight director to provide guidance to pilots
      • Can be coupled with autopilot to fly the aircraft during cruise
    • Performance management
      • Manages performance during takeoff, climb, cruise, descent and approach
      • ECON mode — minimum operating cost per unit distance travelled
      • MIN FUEL mode — target speed to achieve usage of minimum fuel
      • MIN TIME mode — highest speed possible within flight envelope
    • Navigation and guidance
      • FMS is updated with data regarding airports, flight routes and ground aids
      • Automatically tunes into communication and navigation aid stations
      • Automatically corrects for INS errors with navigation data
      • Generates maps for cockpit displays
    • Status and warning
      • Engine Indicating and Crew Alerting System (EICAS)
      • Provides operating information about the engines and other systems
      • Provides alerts and warnings that require action by the flight crew

    Impact on aircraft operation:

    • Automation
      • Pre-flight planning — allows pilots to input and review flight plans
        before takeoff so as to ensure an accurate and efficient flight route
      • Real-time adjustments — automates in-flight adjustments such as trim
    • Precise navigation
      • Integration of data from INS, GPS, and other navigation aids
      • Ensure accurate and reliable navigation information
      • Reduce pilot workload in navigating
    • Enhanced safety
      • Continuously monitors aircraft systems and warns pilots of potential issues
      • Automation of tasks minimizes the likelihood of human errors in operation
