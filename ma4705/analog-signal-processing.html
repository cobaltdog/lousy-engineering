    Analog Signal Processing

    #Analog Signal

    Analog signal processing involves the manipulation of continuous
    signals to either enhance or extract information embedded in them.

    Importance of analog signal processing:

    • Offer lower latency compared to digital systems (does not require conversion to digital)
    • Can be more energy efficient for certain tasks (temperature sensors, hearing aids, etc.)
    • Cost effective — less expensive and easier to produce

    #Classes of Amplifiers

    Description of amplifier:

    • Electronic device that increases the amplitude of a signal
    • Boost weak signals to a level suitable for further processing
    • Capable of handling different types of signals and different frequency ranges

    Class A amplifier:

    • Collector resistor makes it such that signal output does not equal to +Vcc​
    • Emitter resistor creates a feedback to minimize distortion of signal output
    • Addition of emitter resistor introduce an issue — DC bias in signal output
    • Capacitor in signal output line minimizes the DC bias caused by resistor
    • Voltage divider (pair of resistors) ensures transistor is always switched on
    • Capacitor in signal input line minimizes DC bias caused by voltage divider

    An AC input signal alternates between positive and negative voltages but a transistor requires at least +0.7V at the base to be switched on. Thus, voltage divider ensures
    that when the AC voltage is negative, the transistor will still operate.

    Class B amplifier:

    • Positive AC input signal goes to the NPN transistor
    • Negative AC input signal goes to the PNP transistor
    • Switching of the circuit causes distortion in output signal (crossover distortion)

      Crossover distortion occurs due to transistors switching off at an input signal of 0V.

    Class AB amplifier:

    • Modified version of the Class B amplifier to minimize crossover distortion
    • Diodes ensure transistors are slightly switched on even when input signal is 0V
    • Slightly increasing the voltage of each transistor also minimize crossover distortion
    • Since transistors are always switched on, they will fight each other for output signal
    • Resistors prevent transistors fighting each other for output

    #Comparison of Classes

    Class A amplifier:

    • Operation — conducts current throughout the entire input signal cycle (360°)
    • Efficiency — least efficient among the classes of amplifiers (below 25%)
    • Linearity — highly linear with low distortion
    • Bandwidth — wide bandwidth

    Class B amplifier:

    • Operation — conducts current for half of the cycle (180°)
    • Efficiency — more efficient than Class A (up to 78.5%)
    • Linearity — less linear due to crossover distortion
    • Bandwidth — can be broad but need to manage distortion

    Class AB amplifier:

    • Operation — conducts current for more than half, but less than the full cycle
    • Efficiency — between Class A and Class B efficiencies
    • Linearity — better than Class B but not as good as Class A
    • Bandwidth — wide bandwidth

    Class C amplifier:

    • Operation — conducts current for less than half of the cycle
    • Efficiency — very high efficiency (suitable for RF amplification)
    • Linearity — not very linear, significant harmonic distortion
    • Bandwidth — narrow bandwidth

    #Impedance Matching

    Impedance matching:

    • Practice of designing input impedance of an electrical load
    • Also a practice of designing output impedance of signal source
    • Ensures maximum power transfer from the amplifier to the load
    • Reduces signal reflections from the load that can cause distortions

    Impedance matching is crucial for power amplifiers to ensure maximum power
    is delivered to the load, while minimizing any power losses. Thus, efficiency of
    the system can be maintained.

    #Signal-to-Noise Ratio

    A measure that compares the level of a desired signal to the level of background noise.

    💡
    SNR=10×log10​(Power of NoisePower of Signal​)

    S.I. unit of ratio is decibels (dB)

    Harmonic distortion:

    • Distortions that are multiples of a fundamental frequency
    • Generates frequencies that are not present in the original signal

    Intermodulation distortion:

    • Occurs when two or more signals mix in a non-linear system
    • Mixing produces unwanted sum and difference frequencies
    • Unwanted frequencies can interfere with desired signals

    #Amplifier Parameters

    #Gain

    A measure of how much an amplifier increases the amplitude of a signal.

    • Voltage gain — ratio of output voltage to input voltage
    • Current gain — ratio of output current to input current
    • Power gain — ratio of output power to input power

    Factors affecting gain:

    • Component values (resistors, inductors, capacitors)
    • Frequency of input signal
    • Feedback mechanisms
    • Temperature

    #Decibels (dB)

    A bel represents a logarithmic ratio, specifically for power levels.

    Power ratio in decibels,

    💡
    L=10log10​(P2​P1​​)

    Voltage ratio in decibels,

    💡
    L=20log10​(V2​V1​​)

    If P1​ is twice as large as P2​,

    L=10log10​(P2​2P2​​)=3 dB

    A change of ±3 dB indicates that power has been doubled/halved.

    #Frequency Response and Bandwidth

    Frequency response describes how the gain of an amplifier varies with frequency.

    • Amplifier bandwidth — region between frequencies where gain drops by 3 dB
    • Feedback — stabilize gain and extend bandwidth

    Effects of feedback on frequency response:

    • Positive feedback — increased gain but may make the amplifier unstable
    • Negative feedback — stabilizes amplifier, reduces distortion, increases bandwidth

    #Bandwidth

    The range of frequencies over which an amplifier can provide amplification with minimal distortion or attenuation. Frequency range is bounded by two pre-defined points where
    the gain drops to certain percentage of its maximum value.

    Factors affecting bandwidth:

    • Component capacitance — internal capacitance has low-pass filter characteristics
    • Class of amplifier — different classes have different inherent bandwidths
    • Feedback — negative feedback can increase bandwidth
    • Circuit design

    #Operational Amplifiers

    Operational amplifiers are made up of transistors and resistors.

    • If V2​>V1​ the output will saturate towards +5V
    • If V1​>V2​ the output will saturate towards ground
    • Once output reaches saturation, it is at the maximum rated gain

    Comparator:

    • If Vref​>Vin​ the output will saturate towards Vs+​
    • If Vin​>Vref​ the output will saturate towards Vs−​
    • If input signal is an AC signal, output will be a digital signal

    Negative feedback loop:

    • High input impedance so that unnecessary current is not drawn into amplifier
    • Low output impedance such that the amplifier can drive the output load
