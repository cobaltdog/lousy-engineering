    RF Communication

    #The Atmosphere

    Layers of Earth’s atmosphere:

    • Troposphere (0 to 12km)
    • Stratosphere (11km to 50km)
    • Mesosphere (50km to 80km)
    • Thermosphere (80km to 800km)
    • Exosphere (800km to 3000km)

    The ionosphere is a region within the thermosphere containing a relatively
    large number of electrically charged
    atoms and molecules.

    #The Ionosphere

    Use of ionosphere in radio communication:

    • Radio waves generally travel in straight lines
    • Curvature of the Earth limits the range of transmissions
    • Certain frequencies can bounce or reflect off of certain ionosphere layers
    • Taking advantage of this phenomenon allows us to extend the range of transmission
    • Need to account for the shifts or disappearance of the layers between day and night

    D region:

    • Lowest ionospheric region (70km to 90km)
    • Its free electrons almost totally disappear during the night
    • At night, radio waves pass through to the strongly reflecting E and F layers above
    • During the day, some reflection can be obtained, but strength of radio waves is reduced
    • During the day, responsible for absorption of radio waves at lower frequencies (<4MHz)

    E region:

    • Located above the D region (95km to 150km)
    • During the night, ionization of this region is considerably reduced
    • During the day, reflects waves with frequencies lower than 5MHz
      but tends to absorb radio waves above this frequency

    F region:

    • Region with the greatest concentration of free electrons (above 160km)
    • During the day, has two layers with F2​ being the more highly ionized dominant layer
    • At night, the layers merge into a single layer capable of reflecting up to about 35MHz

    #Radio Wave Propagation

    #Types of Waves

    The troposphere and ionosphere layers are typically used for the propagation of radio waves.

    Ground waves:

    • Waves travel close to the surface of the Earth (glides over the Earth’s surface)
    • Attenuation is relatively high such that HF and VHF can only travel short distances
    • At longer wavelengths (lower frequencies), require antennas that are physically large

    Sky waves (ionospheric waves):

    • Radiated upwards and returned at some distant location due to refraction by ionosphere
    • Waves are relatively unaffected by the Earth’s surface and can propagate further
    • Longer distance communication without attenuation and loss of signal strength

    Space waves:

    • Direct wave that requires line-of-sight (LOS) between transmitter and receiver
    • Space waves used are commonly high frequencies waves (VHF, UHF, etc.)
    • High frequency waves are able to penetrate through the atmosphere

    #Nomenclature

    Lowest usable frequency (LUF):

    • Minimum frequency required for reliable communication, especially in HF bands
    • D layer of the ionosphere is responsible for the absorption of HF waves
    • Sunspots and solar flares can influence ionospheric conditions
    • Seasonal variations of the ionosphere

    Maximum usable frequency (MUF):

    • Maximum frequency that can be used for reliable communication
    • Waves above the MUF are either refracted so slowly that they return to Earth
      beyond the desired receiving location, or penetrate through the ionosphere

    Skip distance:

    • Shortest distance from the transmitter at which a sky wave signal returns to Earth
    • Dependent on wave frequency, angle of incidence, degree of ionization
    • Shorter during the night, and longer during the day

    Silent zone:

    • Region within the skip distance where no sky waves are received
    • Dependent on wave frequency, ionospheric conditions, time of day
    • Communication within this region is difficult, can lead to gaps in coverage

    #Propagation Effects

    Reflection:

    • Bouncing back of radio waves at a surface/boundary that the waves cannot pass through
    • Waves are reflected with minimal distortion and without any change in velocity
    • Occurs when object is relatively large compared to the signal’s wavelength
    • Factors influencing reflection — angle of incidence, frequency of wave

    Refraction:

    • Bending of radio waves due to a change in electron density in different regions
    • E and F layers of the ionosphere are capable of bending radio waves
    • Enables long-distance communication using HF radio waves

    Diffraction:

    • Waves spread out when encountering an obstacle comparable in size to their wavelength
    • Does not require a change in medium or a reflective surface

    Scattering:

    • Wave encounters more than one object in its path that is a fraction of its wavelength
    • Occurs more readily at higher frequencies

    Tropospheric scattering:

    • Waves encounter small atmospheric particles in the lower atmosphere
    • Tiny water droplets, dust, etc. interact and cause waves to scatter
    • Scattered waves are redirected along unpredictable paths

    Tropospheric ducting:

    • Waves are trapped in a layer of the atmosphere and can travel longer than usual
    • Caused by a temperature inversion, where cold air is overrun by warm air

    #Modulation of Radio Waves

    Modulation is the process of varying one or more properties of a periodic
    waveform, called the carrier signal, with a separate signal that contains
    the information to be transmitted.

    Types of modulation:

    • AM — amplitude of carrier is varied according to amplitude of input signal
    • FM — frequency of carrier is varied according to amplitude of input signal

    AM:

    • Lower bandwidth
    • More susceptible to noise
    • Used for long-distance communication
    • Commonly used between aircraft and control towers

    FM:

    • Higher bandwidth
    • Less susceptible to noise
    • Used for high quality, short-range transmissions
    • Commonly used for in-flight entertainment systems

    #Modulation

    Benefits of modulation:

    • High frequency modulation allows for smaller antennas on aircraft, reducing weight
    • FM is less susceptible to noise and interference, thus minimizing miscommunication
    • Multiplex allows multiple signals to be sent over a single channel, optimize bandwidth

    Modulation index for AM,

    💡
    m=Ac​Am​​

    where Am​ is the peak amplitude of the modulating signal,
    and     Ac​ is the peak amplitude of the carrier wave

    Modulation index for FM,

    💡
    β=fm​Δf​

    where Δf is the peak frequency deviation of the carrier,
    and     fm​ is the frequency of the modulating signal

    #De-modulation

    Demodulation is the reverse process of modulation. It is the technique used to
    extract the original information from a modulated signal that has been received.

    AM de-modulation:

    • Extract original information by recovering the variations in amplitude
    • Common methods — envelope detection and product detection
    • Long-range communication between aircraft and control towers

    FM de-modulation:

    • Extract original information by recovering the variations in frequency
    • Common methods — phase-locked loop and quadrature detector
    • High-quality audio and short-range communications

    #Transmitters and Receivers

    #Transmitters

    AM transmitter:

    • Microphone converts sound waves into an electrical signal
    • AF and AF power amplifiers amplify the weak electrical signal
    • RF oscillator generates the carrier wave which is then amplified
    • Carrier wave is modulated according to the amplitude of the electrical signal
    • Modulated wave is passed to the antenna coupling unit for impedance matching

    Impedance matching is the process of designing the antenna’s input impedance or matching it to the RF circuitry’s output impedance. A mismatch causes reflection
    of the signal, reducing the power that is actually radiated by the antenna, leading
    to decreased range, efficiency, and potential damage to the transmitter.

    FM transmitter:

    • Microphone converts sound waves into an electrical signal
    • Carrier wave is modulated according to the frequency of the electrical signal
    • Modulated wave is amplified before being passed to the antenna coupling unit

    #Receivers

    Tuned radio frequency (TRF) receiver:

    • Relies on the principle of resonance to tune to a specific frequency
    • Limited sensitivity — struggle to pick up weak signals
    • Limited selectivity — difficult to discriminate signals on adjacent channels
    • Frequency stability — may suffer from frequency drift, require frequent adjustments
    • Generally not suitable for use in commercial radio equipment

    Components of TRF receiver:

    • RF amplifier — amplify received signals
    • Tuning — select desired frequency
    • Detector — convert desired RF signal for further processing
    • Audio amplifier — amplify audio signals that have been detected and decoded
    • Antenna coupling unit — reduce level of any unwanted harmonic components

    Superheterodyne receiver (Superhet):

    • Uses frequency mixing to convert received signal to fixed intermediate frequency (IF)
    • Received RF signal is mixed with a signal generated by a local oscillator
    • Mixing produces a new signal at a fixed IF
    • IF is amplified and filtered

    Transceivers in aircraft:

    • Combines both transmission and reception capabilities within a single unit
    • Space efficient as only one unit is required and simplifies system design
    • Capable of handling multiple types of communication and navigation

    Working principle of transceivers:

    • Signal transmission — transmit signals to ground stations, other aircraft, etc.
    • Signal reception — receive incoming signals for processing and display
    • Automatic gain control — adjust gain based on distance of the target
      to ensure consistent signal strength

    #Simple Antenna Theory

    Antennas serve as the “ears” and “mouth” of an aircraft’s communication and navigation systems. Hence, proper function is vital for the safety and efficiency of flight operations.

    Law of reciprocity:

    • Properties of an antenna remain unchanged whether it is transmitting or receiving
    • Antennas do not have distinct transmit and receive radiation patterns

    Standing wave:

    • Occurs when there is a mismatch between antenna and transmission line
    • Forms when incident wave (travelling from transmitter) and
      reflected wave (from the antenna) interfere with each other

    Standing wave ratio is a measure of impedance mismatch.

    💡
    SWR=Vmin​Vmax​​

    where Vmax​ is the maximum voltage along the line,
    and     Vmin​ is the minimum voltage along the line

    High SWR indicates a significant mismatch, resulting in power loss and reduced antenna efficiency. An SWR of 1:1 is ideal, with no reflected power and maximum power transfer.

    Resonance:

    • Occurs when antenna’s reactive components cancel out, only radiation resistance is left
    • Results in minimum reflected power and maximum power transfer (highest efficiency)
    • At resonance, most of the electrical power is converted into electromagnetic power
    • Bandwidth — resonance defines the frequency range for optimal performance

    Antenna gain:

    • Measure of how well an antenna directs or focuses energy in a specific direction
    • Ratio of the intensity radiated by the antenna in the direction of its maximum output
      at an arbitrary distance, to the intensity radiated by an isotropic antenna at the same distance

    Gain is closely related to an antenna’s directional properties. High-gain antenna focus
    energy more narrowly, which can be beneficial for long-distance communication, but
    is limited in terms of coverage area.

    Radiated power is the amount of electrical power converted into electromagnetic waves.

    💡
    Pr​=I2R

    where I is current through the antenna and R is the radiation resistance

    Efficiency is the ratio of radiated power to the input power.

    💡
    η=Pr​+Ploss​Pr​​

    where Ploss​ is the power dissipated in the ohmic resistance present

    #Isotropic Radiator

    Description of antenna:

    • Theoretical concept used as a reference in antenna theory
    • Idealized antenna that radiates power uniformly in all directions
    • Used as a reference when calculating gain and directional properties of real antennas

    #Half-Wave Dipole

    Description of antenna:

    • Consists of a single conductor that is split in the center
    • Split at the center allows for the connection to a feeder
    • Length of antenna from end to end is equal to half the wavelength of the wave

    Electrical length of the antenna from end to end,

    💡
    L=2λ​=2fc​

    Note that electrical length does not refer to the physical length of the antenna.

    Radiation pattern:

    • Doughnut-shaped radiation pattern
    • Radiates more power perpendicular to the axis of the antenna
    • Minimum radiation occurs along the axis (length) of the antenna

    #Yagi Beam Antenna

    Description of antenna:

    • Consists of a driven element, reflector, and one or more directors
    • Reflector and directors are passive components (unpowered)
    • Only the driven element is connected to a feed line
    • Directors serve to focus energy in a specific direction

    Characteristics of antenna:

    • Highly directional with a higher gain than the dipole antenna
    • Ideal for applications requiring high gain and directivity
    • Fixed frequency device as it is sensitive to frequency
