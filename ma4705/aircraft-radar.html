    Aircraft Radar

    #Primary Radar

    Primary radar:

    • Operates by transmitting a radio wave (pulse) towards a target
    • Wave hits the target and reflects a portion of the signal back to radar system
    • Radar receiver detects the reflected signal to determine location, distance, speed
    • Target does not actively contribute to the signal; simply reflects radar signal back

    Advantages of primary radar:

    • Able to detect any object within its range, including aircraft without transponders
    • Works without requiring any additional equipment on the target aircraft

    Disadvantages of primary radar:

    • Prone to interference from clutter (buildings, mountains, weather, etc.)
    • Limited information about target (no info on aircraft altitude, identification)

    Doppler effect:

    • Frequency of reflected signal changes depending on aircraft’s motion
    • Moving towards the radar, reflected signal has a higher frequency (positive shift)
    • Moving away from the radar, reflected signal has a lower frequency (negative shift)
    • Velocity measurement and direction, distinguishing moving from stationary targets

    #Radar Altimeter

    Frequency-modulated continuous-wave radar altimeter:

    • Carrier frequency changes at a specified rate (40MHz every 100μs)
    • Wave is radiated downwards when transmitter frequency is 400MHz
    • Reflected wave is received when transmitter frequency is 440MHz
    • Difference in frequencies relates to time taken for signal to travel
    • Time taken is then used to determine altitude of the aircraft

    #Pulse-Modulated Radar System

    Components of system:

    • Transmitter — produce short-duration, high-power pulses of RF energy
    • Antenna — produce a narrow beam of RF energy (used for scanning)
    • Receiver — amplify the very weak reflected signals
    • Indicator — shows range and bearing of target

    Pulse-modulated transmitter:

    • Pulse repetition frequency (PRF) — number of pulses per second
    • Pulse duration/width — length of time transmitter is switched on for each pulse

    Factors relating to pulse duration/width:

    • Blind area — too high of a pulse duration results in a blind area as targets that are
      close to the radar reflect signals back while transmitter is still transmitting pulse
    • Target discrimination — two targets that are close together will cause overlapping
      of reflected signals which will appear as a single echo on the indicator (one target)
    • Frequency — lower RF can penetrate clouds, higher RF will suffer attenuation
    • Peak power — transmitted energy must be strong enough to return an echo
    • Bandwidth — receiver bandwidth must be able to receive the band of frequencies associated with the pulse so as to prevent distortion and attenuation of the pulse

    #Secondary Radar

    Secondary radar:

    • Requires the target to carry an active device (transponder)
    • Radar system sends an interrogation signal to the transponder
    • Transponder responds with an encoded signal containing information
    • Transmitted information — altitude, airspeed, identification code, etc.

    Advantages of secondary radar:

    • Provides more detailed information about the target
    • Less susceptible to interference (transponder signals are stronger than reflected signals)

    Disadvantages of secondary radar:

    • Can only detect targets equipped with transponders
    • Requires transponder to be functioning, which may be turned off or malfunction

    #Secondary Surveillance Radar (SSR)

    Secondary surveillance radar:

    • Radar system used by air traffic control
    • Improve ability to detect and identify aircraft, and automatically provide Flight Level
    • Modes A and C interrogation operate continuously as antenna rotates
    • Mode S interrogation operates selectively (listen to certain aircraft)
    • Aircraft transponder within LOS “listens” for interrogation signal and transmits a reply
    • Aircraft displayed as tagged icon on ATC radar screen at measured range and bearing

    Transponder modes:

    • Mode A — aircraft’s transponder squawk code (4-digit code assigned by ATC)
    • Mode C — squawk code and aircraft’s pressure altitude (from Air Data Computer)
    • Mode S — transmit information about aircraft to TCAS receivers and ADS-B system

    Mode S:

    • Designed to avoid over-interrogation of aircraft transponder
    • Occurs when aircraft transponder is interrogated by multiple radar systems,
      which causes the transponder to respond more frequently than necessary
    • Over-interrogation can lead to operational and performance issues
      • Increased transponder workload
      • Increased electromagnetic interference
      • Reduced performance and reliability

    Mode S interrogation characteristics:

    • Ground station transmits two pulses P1 and P2
    • P1 antenna is highly directional, initiates the interrogation process
    • P2 antenna is omni-directional, weaker pulse that is transmitted after P1
    • P2 is used to distinguish between on-beam and off-beam transponders
    • If strength of pulse P1 > P2, aircraft will respond to the interrogation
    • If strength of pulse P1 < P2, aircraft instructed to not respond

    Though P1 antenna is highly directional, the antenna has side lobes. Aircraft responding
    to the P1 side lobes will result in an erroneous bearing as the station is not interrogating these aircraft. Only the aircraft in the main lobe is being interrogated.

    #Traffic Alert and Collision Avoidance System (TCAS)

    TCAS:

    • Air-to-air secondary surveillance radar
    • Ensure pilots are situationally aware of other nearby air traffic
    • Recommend an escape maneuver to avoid impending mid-air collision
    • Use transponder signals from nearby aircraft to determine position relative to you
    • Aircraft fitted with TCAS cannot give advice or warnings to non-equipped aircraft
    • Non-transponder equipped aircraft are invisible to TCAS

    TCAS surveillance:

    • Passive surveillance — aircraft is not a near threat, one interrogation per minute
    • Passive surveillance — aircraft near threat in altitude or range, 0.1Hz interrogation
    • Active surveillance — aircraft near threat in altitude and range, 1Hz interrogation

    TACS symbology:

    • Open white diamond — aircraft is nearby but not a threat at the moment
    • Solid white diamond — aircraft is within 1,200ft and 6nm of you (proximity target)
    • Solid yellow circle — aircraft is within 40-45s of closest point of approach
    • Solid red square — aircraft is within 25s of closest point of approach

    TCAS 1:

    • Only gives a traffic advisory (TA) — “Traffic, Traffic” aural warning
    • Pilot needs to determine the best avoidance maneuver as no RA is given
    • Threat aircraft’s position and height will be displayed next to its aircraft symbol
    • Trend arrow also tells pilot whether threat aircraft is climbing or descending

    TCAS 2:

    • Provides both traffic advisory (TA) and resolution advisory (RA)
    • “Climb, Climb” or “Descent, Descent” aural warning
    • Descent RA is inhibited if aircraft is below 700ft AGL (above ground level)
    • RA highlights the desired rate of climb/descent on the vertical speed
      indicator (VSI) as a green arc to ensure that aircraft avoid each other

    When operating in a dense traffic area, such as an airport with multiple runways, TCAS
    may issue multiple advisories during takeoff and landing. It is advisable to de-select the
    RA function to reduce unwarranted advisories, ensuring pilot workload does not increase.

    #Automatic Dependent Surveillance - Broadcast (ADS-B)

    ADS-B:

    • Supplement traditional radar-based surveillance of aircraft
    • Aircraft uses GPS to determine its own position and automatically broadcast it
    • Does not rely on ground-based radar to send interrogation signals to aircraft
    • Broadcast precise location via Mode S transponder every half-second

    Acronym:

    • Automatic — requires no pilot input or external interrogation
    • Dependent — depends on accurate position and velocity data from GPS
    • Surveillance — provide information to facilities (ATC, hobbyist) that require it
    • Broadcast — information is continuously broadcasted for monitoring by others

    Benefits of ADS-B:

    • Easier and less expensive to deploy than ground radar
    • Position information provided by GPS is more accurate than radar
    • Allows for closer aircraft spacing, increases capacity of congested airspace
