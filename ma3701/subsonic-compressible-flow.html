    Subsonic Compressible Flow

    #Velocity Potential Equation

    The inviscid, compressible, subsonic flow over a body immersed in a uniform
    stream is irrotational. Thus, it can be defined with a velocity potential equation.

    Consider a two-dimensional, steady, irrotational, isentropic flow. Its velocity
    potential ϕ can be expressed in terms of the cartesian velocity components.

    💡
    u=∂x∂ϕ​
    💡
    v=∂y∂ϕ​

    The continuity equation for a steady, two-dimensional flow is,

    ∂x∂(ρu)​+∂y∂(ρv)​=0

    ρ∂x∂u​+u∂x∂ρ​+ρ∂y∂v​+v∂y∂ρ​=0

    For compressible flows, an additional term ρ is included since density is not a constant.

    Substituting the velocity components into the continuity equation,

    ρ∂x∂2ϕ​+∂x∂ϕ​∂x∂ρ​+ρ∂y∂2ϕ​+∂y∂ϕ​∂y∂ρ​=0

    ρ(∂x∂2ϕ​+∂y∂2ϕ​)+∂x∂ϕ​∂x∂ρ​+∂y∂ϕ​∂y∂ρ​=0

    Consider the momentum equation in terms of Euler’s equation.

    dp=−ρU dU

    dp=−2ρ​ d(U2)

    dp=−2ρ​ d(u2+v2)

    dp=−2ρ​ d[(∂x∂ϕ​)2+(∂y∂ϕ​)2]

    Since the flow is isentropic, any change in pressure dp in the flow is
    accompanied by a corresponding isentropic change in density dρ.

    dρdp​=(∂ρ∂p​)s​

    dp=a2 dρ

    Recall that the right-hand side of the equation is the square of the speed of sound.

    Equatiing the two pressure equations, we get the general equation for dρ.

    💡
    dρ=−2a2ρ​ d[(∂x∂ϕ​)2+(∂y∂ϕ​)2]

    Consider changes only in the x-direction.

    ∂x∂ρ​=−2a2ρ​ ∂x∂​[(∂x∂ϕ​)2+(∂y∂ϕ​)2]

    ∂x∂ρ​=−a2ρ​(∂x∂ϕ​∂x2∂2ϕ​+∂y∂ϕ​∂x∂y∂2ϕ​)

    Consider changes only in the y-direction.

    ∂y∂ρ​=−2a2ρ​ ∂y∂​[(∂x∂ϕ​)2+(∂y∂ϕ​)2]

    ∂y∂ρ​=−a2ρ​(∂x∂ϕ​∂x∂y∂2ϕ​+∂y∂ϕ​∂y2∂2ϕ​)

    Substituting the partial derivatives into the continuity equation,

    ρ(∂x∂2ϕ​+∂y∂2ϕ​)+∂x∂ϕ​∂x∂ρ​+∂y∂ϕ​∂y∂ρ​=0

    💡
    [1−a21​(∂x∂ϕ​)2]∂x2∂2ϕ​+[1−a21​(∂y∂ϕ​)2]∂y2∂2ϕ​−a22​(∂x∂ϕ​)(∂y∂ϕ​)∂x∂y∂2ϕ​=0

    The above equation is the velocity potential equation for compressible subsonic flows.

    #Linearized Velocity Potential

    Consider the two-dimensional, irrotational, isentropic flow over an airfoil. The airfoil is immersed in a uniform flow U∞​. At an arbitrary point P in the flow field, the velocity
    is U with components u and v.

    The x-component of velocity can be visualized as U∞​ plus an increment in velocity u^.
    Similarly, the y-component can be visualized as an increment v^, since the uniform flow
    has zero velocity in the y-direction.

    💡
    u=U∞​+u^
    💡
    v=v^

    where u^ and v^ are called perturbation velocities, which are not necessarily small

    We can define a perturbation velocity potential ϕ^​.

    💡
    ϕ=U∞​x+ϕ^​

    where u^=∂x∂ϕ^​​ and v^=∂y∂ϕ^​​

    Differentiating the velocity potential with respect to x and y,

    ∂x∂ϕ​=U∞​+∂x∂ϕ^​​

    ∂x2∂2ϕ​=∂x2∂2ϕ^​​

    ∂x∂u^​=∂x2∂2ϕ^​​

    ∂y∂ϕ​=∂y∂ϕ^​​

    ∂y2∂2ϕ​=∂y2∂2ϕ^​​

    ∂y∂v^​=∂y2∂2ϕ^​​

    ∂x∂y∂2ϕ​=∂x∂y∂2ϕ^​​

    Substituting the partial derivatives into the velocity potential equation,

    [a2−(U∞​+∂x∂ϕ^​​)2]∂x2∂2ϕ^​​+[a2−(∂y∂ϕ^​​)2]∂y2∂2ϕ^​​−2(U∞​+∂x∂ϕ^​​)(∂x∂ϕ^​​)∂x∂y∂2ϕ^​​=0

    💡
    [a2−(U∞​+u^)2]∂x∂u^​+[a2−v^2]∂y∂v^​−2v^(U∞​+u^)∂y∂u^​=0

    The above equation is the perturbation velocity potential equation.

    Recall, from normal shockwaves, that the equation for total enthalpy is,

    h1​+21​U1​2=h2​+21​U2​2

    cp​T1​+21​U1​2=cp​T2​+21​U2​2

    γ−1γRT1​​+21​U1​2=γ−1γRT2​​+21​U2​2

    γ−1a1​2​+21​U1​2=γ−1a2​2​+21​U2​2

    γ−1a∞​2​+21​U∞​2=γ−1a2​+21​[(U∞​+u^)2+v^2]

    Substituting the above into the perturbation velocity potential equation,

    💡
    (1−M∞​2)∂x∂u^​+∂y∂v^​=M∞​2[(γ+1)U∞​u^​+2γ+1​U∞​2u^2​+2γ−1​U∞​2v^2​]∂x∂u^​                                       +M∞​2[(γ+1)U∞​u^​+2γ+1​U∞​2v^2​+2γ−1​U∞​2u^2​]∂y∂v^​                                        +M∞​2[U∞​v^​(1+U∞​u^​)(∂y∂u^​+∂x∂v^​)]

    Note that the left-hand side of the equation is linear while the right-hand side is non-linear.

    Limiting to small perturbations; that is, assume a thin airfoil at a small angle of attack.
    Thus, in such a case, the perturbations u^ and v^ will be small in comparison to U∞​.

    U∞​u^​, U∞​v^​≪1

    U∞​2u^2​, U∞​2v^2​⋘1

    Comparing the terms on both sides of the equation:

    1. For 0≤M∞​≤0.8 or M∞​≥1.2,
      M∞​2[(γ+1)U∞​u^​+...]∂x∂u^​≪(1−M∞​2)∂x∂u^​
      Thus, the first term on the right-hand side can be ignored.
    2. For M∞​<5,
      M∞​2[(γ+1)U∞​u^​+...]∂y∂v^​≪∂y∂v^​
      Thus, the second term on the right-hand side can be ignored.
    3. Lastly, for the third term on the right-hand side of the equation,
      M∞​2[U∞​v^​(1+U∞​u^​)(∂y∂u^​+∂x∂v^​)]≈0

    The equation then becomes a linear partial differential equation.

    💡
    (1−M∞​2)∂x∂u^​+∂y∂v^​=0
    💡
    (1−M∞​2)∂x2∂2ϕ^​​+∂y2∂2ϕ^​​=0

    Note that the equation is applicable only to thin airfoils at small angles of attack, and
    for subsonic and supersonic Mach numbers. The equation is not valid for thick bodies
    and large angles of attack, and for transonic flow or hypersonic flow.

    #Linearized Pressure Coefficient

    Pressure coefficient is given by,

    💡
    Cp​=q∞​p−p∞​​

    where dynamic pressure q∞​=21​ρ∞​U∞​2

    The dynamic pressure can be expressed in terms of M∞​.

    q∞​=21​(γp∞​γp∞​​)ρ∞​U∞​2

    q∞​=21​γp∞​(γp∞​ρ∞​​)U∞​2

    q∞​=21​γp∞​a∞​2U∞​2​

    q∞​=21​γp∞​M∞​2

    Substituting the above into the pressure coefficient,

    💡

    Cp​=γM∞​22​(p∞​p​−1)

    The pressure ratio can be expressed as,

    p∞​p​=[1−2γ−1​M∞​2(U∞​2u^​+U∞​2u^2+v^2​)]γ−1γ​

    p∞​p​=(1−ε)γ−1γ​

    p∞​p​=1−γ−1γ​ε+...

    p∞​p​=1−γ−1γ​[2γ−1​M∞​2(U∞​2u^​+U∞​2u^2+v^2​)]+...

    p∞​p​=1−2γ​M∞​2(U∞​2u^​+U∞​2u^2+v^2​)+...

    Substituting the above into the pressure coefficient,

    Cp​=γM∞​22​[1−2γ​M∞​2(U∞​2u^​+U∞​2u^2+v^2​)+...−1]

    Cp​=−U∞​2u^​−U∞​2u^2+v^2​

    💡
    Cp​=−U∞​2u^​

    The perturbation velocities u^ and v^ are small compared to U∞​.

    #Prandtl-Glauert Compressibility Correction

    The Prandtl-Glauert method is based on the linearized perturbation velocity
    potential equation. Thus, it is limited to thin airfoils at small angles of attack.

    Consider the subsonic, compressible, inviscid flow over an airfoil. Assume that the airfoil is thin and that the angle of attack is small.

    Let β2=1−M∞​2.

    💡
    β2∂x2∂2ϕ^​​+∂y2∂2ϕ^​​=0

    Using variable transformation ζ=x and η=βy,

    ϕˉ​(ζ,η)=βϕ^​(x,y)

    ϕ^​ is the perturbation velocity potential: represents a compressible flow in (x,y) space.

    ϕˉ​ is the perturbation velocity potential: represents an incompressible flow in (ζ,η) space.

    Recall the chain rule of partial differentiation.

    ∂x∂ϕ^​​=∂ζ∂ϕ^​​∂x∂ζ​+∂η∂ϕ^​​∂x∂η​

    ∂x∂ϕ^​​=∂ζ∂ϕ^​​⋅1+∂η∂ϕ^​​⋅0

    ∂x∂ϕ^​​=∂ζ∂ϕ^​​

    ∂x∂ϕ^​​=β1​∂ζ∂ϕˉ​​

    ∂y∂ϕ^​​=∂ζ∂ϕ^​​∂y∂ζ​+∂η∂ϕ^​​∂y∂η​

    ∂y∂ϕ^​​=∂ζ∂ϕ^​​⋅0+∂η∂ϕ^​​⋅β

    ∂y∂ϕ^​​=β∂η∂ϕ^​​

    ∂y∂ϕ^​​=∂η∂ϕˉ​​

    Differentiating the above with respect to x and y,

    ∂x2∂2ϕ^​​=β1​∂ζ2∂2ϕˉ​​

    ∂y2∂2ϕ^​​=β∂η2∂2ϕˉ​​

    Substituting the above into the perturbation velocity potential,

    β2∂x2∂2ϕ^​​+∂y2∂2ϕ^​​=0

    β2(β1​∂ζ2∂2ϕˉ​​)+(β∂η2∂2ϕˉ​​)=0

    💡
    ∂ζ2∂2ϕˉ​​+∂η2∂2ϕˉ​​=0

    The above equation resembles Laplace’s equation for incompressible flow.

    Thus, the subsonic, compressible flow represented by ϕ^​(x,y), can now be related to an incompressible flow in the transformed (ζ,η) space, which is represented by ϕˉ​(ζ,η).
    The scaling factor     β transforms the compressible flow to an incompressible flow.

    #Prandtl-Glauert Rule

    The shape of the airfoil in physical space is given by y=f(x).
    The shape of the airfoil in the transformed space is η=q(ζ).

    From the diagram of the airfoil,

    tanθ=uv​=U∞​+u^v^​

    tanθ=U∞​v^​

    v^=U∞​tanθ

    v^=U∞​dxdf​

    Perturbation velocity u^ is very small compared to U∞​.

    Since v^=∂y∂ϕ^​​=∂η∂ϕˉ​​,

    ∂η∂ϕˉ​​=U∞​dxdf​

    ∂η∂ϕˉ​​=U∞​dζdq​

    💡
    dxdf​=dζdq​

    The above transformation relates the compressible flow over an airfoil in (x,y) space to the incompressible flow in (ζ,η) space over the same airfoil.

    Substituting the above into the linearized pressure coefficient,

    Cp​=−U∞​2u^​

    Cp​=−U∞​2​∂x∂ϕ^​​

    Cp​=−U∞​2​β1​∂ζ∂ϕˉ​​

    Cp​=β1​(−U∞​2uˉ​)

    Recall that ϕˉ​ is the perturbation velocity potential for an incompressible flow.
    Thus,     uˉ is then the perturbation velocity for that incompressible flow.

    Let Cp0​​ denote the incompressible pressure coefficient.

    💡
    Cp​=βCp0​​​=1−M∞​2​Cp0​​​

    where Cp​ represents the predicted pressure coefficient of a compressible flow

    Thus, if the incompressible pressure distribution over an airfoil is known, then
    the compressible pressure distribution over the same airfoil can be obtained.
    The same relation also applies to lift and moment coefficients.

    💡
    Cl​=1−M∞​2​Cl0​​​
    💡
    Cm​=1−M∞​2​Cm0​​​

    #Improved Compressibility Corrections

    The Karman-Tsien rule states,

    💡
    Cp​=1−M∞​2​+2Cp0​​​(1+1−M∞​2​M∞​2​)Cp0​​​

    Laitone’s rule states,

    💡
    Cp​=1−M∞​2​+Cp0​​⋅21−M∞​2​M∞​2[1+21​(γ−1)M∞​2]​Cp0​​​

    Note that the Prandtl-Glauert rule underpredicts the experimental data, whereas the improved compressibility corrections are more accurate. Both the Karman-Tsien and
    Laitone rules attempt to account for some of the non-linear aspects of the flow.

    #Critical Mach Number

    The linearized theory derived earlier does not apply to the transonic flow regime.
    Transonic flow is highly non-linear, and theoretical transonic aerodynamics is a
    challenging and sophisticated subject.

    Consider an airfoil in a low-speed flow. Let A represent the location on the airfoil
    surface where the pressure is a minimum and thus, where M is a maximum.

    As M∞​ increases, MA​ also increases. At some value of M∞​, the local Mach number
    at A equals to 1. When this occurs, the freestream Mach number is called the critical
    Mach number Mcr​. At values above Mcr​, the airfoil experiences a dramatic increase
    in drag as well as the formation of shock waves.

    Let p∞​ and pA​ represent the static pressures in the freestream and at A.

    p∞​pA​​=p0​pA​​⋅p∞​p0​​

    p∞​pA​​=[1+21​(γ−1)MA​21+21​(γ−1)M∞​2​]γ−1γ​

    Recall from normal shockwaves, for an isentropic flow, the total pressure p0​ is constant.

    The pressure coefficient at A can then be expressed as,

    CpA​​=γM∞​22​(p∞​pA​​−1)

    💡
    CpA​​=γM∞​22​{[1+21​(γ−1)MA​21+21​(γ−1)M∞​2​]γ−1γ​−1}

    When the freestream Mach number reaches Mcr​, the local Mach number at A equals to 1.
    Thus, the pressure coefficient at A, whereby the freestream Mach number is Mcr​, is then,

    💡
    Cpcr​​=γMcr​22​{[1+21​(γ−1)1+21​(γ−1)Mcr​2​]γ−1γ​−1}

    Note that the critical pressure coefficient is at the point A, where M∞​=Mcr​ and MA​=1.
    The equation is simply an aerodynamic relation for isentropic flow, and is not dependent on
    the shape of a given airfoil.

    Plotting Cpcr​​ with the compressibility correction equations derived earlier, we can estimate the critical Mach number for a given airfoil.

    The intersection represents the point at which sonic flow occurs at the minimum pressure location on the airfoil.

    Thus, the value of M∞​ at that intersection is
    then the critical Mach number for that airfoil.

    The thicker the airfoil, the lower its critical Mach number. A thicker airfoil produces lower local static pressures as velocity increases more.

    Note that the pressure coefficient equation does not predict the effect of thickness as it is based
    on the thin airfoil assumption.

    #Drag-Divergence Mach Number

    Consider an airfoil at a fixed angle of attack in a wind tunnel.

    • Low subsonic speeds up to Mcr​, profile drag of airfoil remains relatively constant
    • As M∞​ increases slightly above Mcr​, a small region of supersonic flow forms
    • As M∞​ increases further, it reaches a point where Cd​ suddenly starts to increase
    • At this point of sudden increase in drag, M∞​ is the drag-divergence Mach number Mdd​
    • Large increase in drag is due to an extensive region of supersonic flow over the airfoil

    Transonic flow is characterized by:

    • Sudden increase in drag
    • Severe structural vibrations due to unsteady shockwaves and compression waves
    • Thermal challenges caused by aerodynamic heating (increase in temperature of the skin)

    #Transonic Flow Behaviour

    Within the transonic regime, the section lift coefficient varies with Mach number.

    At speeds slightly below transonic speeds:

    • Flow along the upper surface decelerates from local flow velocities (supersonic) without the formation of
      a shockwave
    • Wake is small due to minimal flow separation

    At slightly transonic speeds:

    • Section lift coefficient reaches its maximum value
    • Flow is supersonic for the first 70% of the upper surface, before terminating in a shockwave, and reducing to subsonic speeds
    • Flow along the lower surface is purely subsonic
    • Wake is larger (flow separation at the shockwave)

    As M∞​ increases from 0.75 to 0.81, the airfoil’s center of pressure shifts backwards as
    the shockwave moves rearwards. This movement of the     cp is due to the region in front
    of the shockwave generating higher lift due to higher local velocities. Thus, it results in
    a nose-down pitching moment (Mach tuck).

    At transonic speeds:

    • Shockwave on upper surface shifts further rearwards
    • Supersonic flow nearly over the entire lower surface and deceleration to subsonic speeds occurs through
      a shockwave at the trailing edge
    • Lift is thus, drastically reduced
    • At the trailing edge, pressure on the upper surface is higher than that on the lower surface, resulting in a pitch-up moment

    At speeds slightly below the speed of sound:

    • Shockwaves for both the upper and lower surfaces have shifted rearwards to the trailing edge
    • Local velocities are mostly supersonic
    • Pressure imbalance, due to the different location of the shockwaves earlier, more or less disappears

    At supersonic speeds:

    • A bow shockwave forms at the leading edge, and creates a small region of subsonic flow around it
    • Flow remains supersonic nearly everywhere else
    • Shockwaves at trailing edge becomes weaker

    As M∞​ increases further in the supersonic regime, the shockwave at the leading edge becomes attached and
    the flow field becomes entirely supersonic.

    As the aircraft accelerates, the pressure distribution of the airfoil can change drastically. These sudden shifts in pressure distributions can destabilize the aircraft, resulting in unwanted vibrations, flutter, and pitching effects.

    #Supercritical Airfoil

    The thinner an airfoil, the higher its critical Mach number. However, there is a limit to how thin a practical airfoil can be — certain thickness for structural strength, room for storage
    of fuel, etc.

    The purpose of a supercritical airfoil is to improve transonic performance by increasing the value of Mdd​, although Mcr​ may also change a little. This delays the onset of significant drag growth experienced in the transonic regime.

    #Charateristics of Supercritical Airfoils

    • Relatively flat upper surface to encourage a region of supersonic flow with lower local Mach numbers, which results in a weaker shockwave and thus, less drag
    • Due to flatness of the upper surface, the front 60% of the airfoil has a negative camber, which lowers the lift generated, and thus to compensate for this, the
      rear 30% of the airfoil has an extreme positive camber (cusplike shape)
    • Does not compromise on structural strength and fuel storage

    #Area Rule

    It was well known by ballisticians that the speed of a supersonic bullet or artillery shell
    with a smooth variation of cross-sectional area was higher than projectiles with abrupt
    or discontinuous area distributions.

    Applying this concept to an aircraft, the variation of its cross-sectional area should be smooth. Thus, to compensate for the addition of the wing and tail, the fuselage cross-sectional area should decrease (coke bottle shape).

    By following this design philosophy, transonic drag could be reduced significantly by having
    a smooth variation of cross-sectional area.
