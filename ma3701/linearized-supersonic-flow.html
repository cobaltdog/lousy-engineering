    Linearized Supersonic Flow

    #Linearized Theory

    Recall the linearized perturbation velocity potential equation for subsonic, compressible flows.

    (1−M∞​2)∂x2∂2ϕ^​​+∂y2∂2ϕ^​​=0

    In the case of supersonic flow, the equation is written as,

    💡
    λ2∂x2∂2ϕ^​​−∂y2∂ϕ^​​=0

    where λ2=M∞​2−1​

    A solution to the above equation is ϕ^​=f(x−λy). From observation, the
    perturbation velocity potential is constant along lines of x−λy=const.

    The slope of the lines whereby x−λy=const is,

    dxdy​=λ1​=M∞​2−1​1​

    Recall that Mach angle μ=sin−1M1​.

    tanμ=M∞​2−1​1​

    Comparing the two equations, the line along which ϕ^​=const is a Mach line.
    All the disturbances created at the wall propagate unchanged away from the
    wall along the Mach waves, which all have the same slope.

    #Linearized Supersonic Pressure Coefficient

    Since the perturbation velocity potential ϕ^​ is a function of x−λy,

    ∂x∂ϕ^​​=f′(x−λy)⋅∂x∂​(x−λy)

    ∂x∂ϕ^​​=f′(x−λy)⋅1

    ∂x∂ϕ^​​=f′

    ∂y∂ϕ^​​=f′(x−λy)⋅∂y∂​(x−λy)

    ∂x∂ϕ^​​=f′(x−λy)(−λ)

    ∂x∂ϕ^​​=f′(−λ)

    where f′ is the differentiation of f with respect to x−λy

    Recall that the partial differentiation of ϕ with respect to x and y are,

    ∂x∂ϕ^​​=u^=f′

    ∂y∂ϕ^​​=v^=−λf′

    💡
    u^=−λv^​

    Let θ be the angle between the tangent to the surface and the freestream.

    tanθ=uv​=V∞​+u^v^​

    θ=V∞​v^​

    v^=V∞​θ

    Recall that the linearized pressure coefficient for subsonic, compressible flows is,

    Cp​=−V∞​2u^​

    Substituting u^ for supersonic flows,

    Cp​=−V∞​2(−v^/λ)​

    Cp​=V∞​λ2V∞​θ​

    Cp​=λ2θ​

    💡
    Cp​=M∞​2−1​2θ​

    Note that θ is positive when measured in
    the anticlockwise direction and vice versa.

    The linearized supersonic pressure coefficient is directly proportional to the local surface inclination with respect to the freestream. It is applicable for any slender 2-dimensional
    body where     θ is small, due to small-angle approximation.

    #Application to Supersonic Airfoils

    The linearized supersonic pressure coefficient can be used to
    estimate the lift and wave drag for thin supersonic airfoils.

    Consider points A and B near the leading edge of the airfoil. The tangent to the
    surfaces at these points form positive θA​ and θB​. Thus, CpA​​ and CpB​​ are positive.
    In contrast, when the tangent to the surface form a negative θ, at points C and D,
    then CpC​​ and CpD​​ are negative.

    Consider the simplest airfoil, a flat plate at a small angle of attack α.
    The upper surface is inclined at angle α away from the freestream.
    The lower surface is inclined at angle α into the freestream.

    Pressure coefficient for upper surface,

    Cp,u​=−M∞​2−1​2α​

    Pressure coefficient for lower surface,

    Cp,l​=M∞​2−1​2α​

    Since the inclination angle of the surfaces are constant along their entire length, the
    pressure coefficients are constant over the surfaces. Thus, the force coefficients ca​
    and cn​ can be obtained through integration.

    The axial force coefficient is given by,

    ca​=c1​∫LETE​ Cp,u​−Cp,l​ dy

    💡
    ca​=0

    Note that the flat plate has, theoretically, zero thickness. Thus, dy=0.

    The normal force coefficient is given by,

    cn​=c1​∫LETE​ Cp,l​−Cp,u​ dx

    cn​=c1​∫LETE​ M∞​2−1​2α​−(−M∞​2−1​2α​) dx

    cn​=M∞​2−1​4α​c1​∫0c​dx

    💡
    cn​=M∞​2−1​4α​

    Resolving forces in the respective x and y directions,

    Cl​=cn​cosα−ca​sinα

    Cd​=cn​sinα+ca​cosα

    Using small-angle approximation,

    Cl​=cn​−ca​α

    Cd​=cn​α+ca​

    Substituting cn​ into the above equations,

    💡
    Cl​=M∞​2−1​4α​
    💡
    Cd​=M∞​2−1​4α2​

    For a thin airfoil of arbitrary shape at a small angle of attack,

    💡
    Cl​=M∞​2−1​4α​
    💡
    Cd​=M∞​2−1​4α2​+M∞​2−1​2​∫01​[(dxdzu​​)2+(dxdzl​​)2] d(cx​)

    #Second-Order Theory

    The linearized supersonic pressure coefficient is, in essence, the first term of a Taylor series expansion of the pressure field in terms of θ. Thus, by retaining the second-order term θ2,
    a more accurate equation can be obtained.

    💡
    Cp​=M∞​2−1​2θ​+[2(M∞​2−1)2(γ+1)M∞​4−4M∞​2+4​]θ2

    Alternatively, the equation can be expressed as Cp​=C1​θ+C2​θ2.

    By integrating the pressure field over the upper and lower surfaces, expressions for normal coefficient cn​ and axial coefficient ca​ can be obtained. Then, cl​ and cd​ can be determined.

    #Wing Configurations

    At supsersonic speeds, aircraft drag is composed of:

    • Parasite drag — due to skin friction, pressure drag, interference drag
    • Compressibility/wave drag — due to formation of shockwaves
    • Induced drag — due to lift generation

    💡
    CD​=CD0​​+CDi​​+CDwave​​

    #Supersonic Airfoils

    Characteristics of a supersonic airfoil:

    • Sharp leading edge and trailing edge
    • Relatively flat upper surface to minimize wave drag and maximize lift
    • Relatively thin, mildly cambered, sections to manage formation of shockwaves

    Recall from subsonic, compressible flows, that a blunt leading edge results in formation
    of a bow shockwave at the leading edge. The shockwave creates a region of subsonic
    flow around it. Having sharp edges pins the shockwave to that edge.

    #Swept Wings

    If the wing leading edge is swept aft of the Mach cone, originating at the apex of the
    wing, the disturbances propagate along the Mach lines “warning” the approaching
    flow of the presence of the wing.

    Note that a leading edge that is swept within the Mach cone is referred to as a subsonic leading edge, and flow approaching the wing is similar to subsonic flow, even though
    the flight speed is supersonic. The sweep of the wing is akin to a concave corner.

    Consider an infinite length swept wing with sweep angle Λ, at an angle of attack α.

    Resolving the freestream Mach number in the top view:

    • Normal (chordwise) component M∞​cosΛ
    • Parallel (spanwise) component M∞​sinΛ

    Resolving the freestream Mach number in the side view:

    • Normal (chordwise) component M∞​cosΛcosα
    • Parallel (spanwise) component M∞​sinΛcosα

    Note that the normal (chordwise) component is the effective flow over the wing, which contributes to the generation of lift. Furthermore, due to the sweep angle and angle of
    attack, the velocity along the effective chord is the effective Mach number.

    The effective Mach number, along the effective chord B-B, is then,

    M∞(e)​=(M∞​sinα)2+(M∞​cosΛcosα)2​

    💡
    M∞(e)​=M∞​1−sin2Λcos2α​

    Similarly, the effective angle of attack is,

    αe​=tan−1M∞​cosΛcosαM∞​sinα​

    💡
    αe​=tan−1cosΛtanα​

    The effective thickness ratio is,

    💡
    τe​=c(cosΛ)t​

    The effective lift and drag coefficients are,

    💡
    Cl​=Cle​(1−sin2Λcos2α)
    💡
    Cd​=Cde​cosΛ(1−sin2Λcos2α)

    #Design Considerations

    Many of the theoretical aerodynamic benefits of leading edge sweepback are not attained in practice due to flow separation over the upper surface of the wing.

    Types of separation:

    • Leading edge separation due to high suction pressures
    • Separation due to spanwise flow
    • Inboard shock separation
    • Trailing edge shock separation

    Leading edge flow separation:

    • Similar to counter-rotating vortex system of delta wings
    • Flow separation occurs on the outside of the vortices (secondary separation)

    Note that for highly swept wings with subsonic leading edges, separation can occur
    quite readily even for small values of wing angle of attack, due to the effective angle
    of attack being larger.

    Spanwise flow separation:

    • Spanwise flow results in thickening of boundary layer near the wing tips
    • Thick boundary layer will separate more readily than a thin one as it is
      less resistant to a adverse pressure gradient

    Inboard shock separation:

    • Occurs at the junction between the wing and fuselage along the leading edge
    • Sufficiently strong shocks will lead to flow separations

    Trailing edge flow separation:

    • Can occur if the wing trailing edge is supersonic
    • Shockwave is required to reduce upper surface pressure back to freestream value
    • Sufficiently strong shocks will induce separation of the boundary layer

    #Swing Wings

    To widen an aircraft’s flight envelope, a variable-sweep design can be used instead, combining the benefits of efficient subsonic and supersonic flight. However, it is not
    an easy engineering problem due to the extra mechanical complexity and significant
    weight penalty.

    A tapered wing is beneficial for subsonic cruising, loitering and maneuvers.
    A swept wing is beneficial for reducing transonic drag, wave drag.
