    Incompressible Flow over Wings

    To calculate the induced drag, total lift, and lift distribution of a finite wing.

    #Downwash and Induced Drag

    Generation of lift on the wing is due to a net imbalance of the pressure distribution on the
    top and bottom surfaces of the wing. This pressure imbalance results in the flow near the
    wing tips to curl around the tips.

    On the upper surface, there is generally a spanwise component of flow from the tip toward
    the wing root, causing the streamlines over the top surface to bend toward the root. On
    the bottom surface, there is also a spanwise component of flow from the root toward
    the tip, causing the streamlines over the bottom surface to bend toward the tip.

    The tendency for the flow to “leak” around the wing tips creates a trailing vortex at each
    wing tip. These wing tip vortices induce a small downward component of air velocity in
    the neighbourhood of the wing itself — downwash, denoted by w.

    We now define α as the geometric angle of attack. The local relative wind is inclined below
    the direction of U∞​ by the angle αi​, called the induced angle of attack. The presence of downwash reduces the angle of attack as seen by the airfoil; effective angle of attack.

    💡
    αeff​=α−αi​

    Notice that the local lift vector, which is aligned perpendicular to the local relative wind, is inclined behind the vertical by the angle αi​. Thus, there is a component of local lift in the direction of U∞​, drag created by the presence of downwash — induced drag.

    The induced angle of attack is given by,

    αi​=tan−1U∞​−w​

    For small angles, the equation becomes,

    💡
    αi​=−U∞​w​

    Note that αi​ is expressed in radians.

    #The Biot-Savart Law

    Previously, we looked at a straight vortex filament extending to ±∞.

    In general, a vortex filament can be curved. The filament induces a flow field in the
    surrounding space. If the circulation is taken about any path enclosing the filament,
    a constant value Γ is obtained, which is the strength of the vortex filament.

    Consider a segment of the filament dl. The radius vector from dl to an arbitrary
    point P in space is r. The filament segment dl induces a velocity at P given by,

    💡
    dV=4πΓ​∣r∣3dl×r​

    Let us apply the Biot-Savart law to a straight vortex filament of infinite length. The strength of the filament is Γ and the velocity induced at P by the directed segment of the vortex filament dl is given by the above equation.

    Velocity induced at P by the entire vortex filament is,

    V=∫−∞∞​4πΓ​∣r∣3dl×r​

    The magnitude of the velocity V=∣V∣ is then,

    V=4πΓ​∫−∞∞​r2sinθ​ dl

    Let h be the perpendicular distance from P to the vortex filament.

    r=sinθh​

    l=tanθh​

    dl=−sin2θh​ dθ

    Substituting the above into the velocity equation,

    V=4πΓ​∫−∞∞​r2sinθ​ dl

    V=−4πΓ​∫π0​sinθ dθ

    💡
    V=2πhΓ​

    Note that the velocity induced at P is the same equation as a point vortex.

    Consider a semi-infinite vortex filament extending from A to ∞. Point A can be considered
    a boundary of the flow. Let P be a point on the same plane as A and perpendicular to the filament. Using the same method as above, the velocity induced at P is,

    💡
    V=4πhΓ​

    #Lifting Line Theory

    A vortex filament of strength Γ that is bound to a fixed location in a flow (bound vortex)
    will experience a force L′=ρ∞​U∞​Γ. This bound vortex, unlike a free vortex, does not
    move with the same fluid elements throughout a flow.

    We can replace a finite wing of span b with a bound vortex, extending between 2b​≤y≤−2b​. We then assume the vortex filament continues as two free vortices trailing downstream from the wing tips to infinity. Thus, the result is the horseshoe vortex.

    The Helmholtz’s theorem states that the strength of a vortex filament is constant along its
    length. A vortex filament cannot end in a fluid; it must extend to the boundaries of the fluid (which can be     ±∞) or form a closed path.

    Consider the downwash w induced along the bound vortex. The bound vortex induces no velocity along itself. However, the trailing vortices both contribute to the induced velocity
    along the bound vortex.

    Taking the origin as the center of the bound vortex, velocity at any point y is,

    w(y)=−4π(2b​+y)Γ​−4π(2b​−y)Γ​

    The first term is the contribution from the vortex at −2b​ and the second term from 2b​.

    The downwash velocity equation reduces to,

    💡
    w(y)=−4πΓ​(2b​)2−y2b​

    The downwash distribution due to the single horseshoe vortex does not realistically simulate that of a finite wing; the downwash approaching an infinite value at the tips. This is resolved
    by representing wing with a large number of horseshoe vortices. The bound vortex of each horseshoe vortex is coincident along a single line, called the lifting line.

    Extrapolating to an infinite number of horseshoe vortices, we get:

    • A continuous distribution of Γ(y) along the lifting line
    • Trailing vortices become a continuous vortex sheet downstream of the lifting line
    • Total strength of vortex sheet integrated across the span of the wing is zero, as it
      consists of pairs of trailing vortices of equal strength but in opposite directions

    The velocity dw induced at y0​ by the entire semi-infinite trailing vortex is,

    dw=−4π(y0​−y)dydΓ​ dy​

    The total downwash velocity w induced at y0​ by the entire trailing vortex sheet is,

    💡
    w(y0​)=−4π1​∫−b/2b/2​y0​−ydydΓ​ dy​

    w is positive in the upward direction.

    #Elliptical Lift Distribution

    Consider a circulation distribution given by,

    Γ(y)=Γ0​1−(b2y​)2​

    Differentiating the circulation distribution,

    dydΓ​=−b24Γ0​​1−b24y2​​y​

    Substituting into the velocity equation w(y0​),

    w(y0​)=πb2Γ0​​∫−b/2b/2​(y0​−y)1−b24y2​​y​ dy

    Evaluating the integral by making the appropriate substitution,

    💡
    w(θ0​)=−2bΓ0​​

    The equation states that the downwash is constant over the span of an elliptical wing.

    #Lift Coefficient

    Recall that the lift distribution from the Kutta-Joukowski theorem is,

    L′(y)=ρ∞​U∞​Γ(y)

    The total lift is then obtained by integrating the above equation,

    L=∫−b/2b/2​L′(y) dy

    L=ρ∞​U∞​∫−b/2b/2​Γ(y) dy

    Substituting the circulation distribution Γ(y) into L,

    L=ρ∞​U∞​Γ0​∫−b/2b/2​1−b24y2​​ dy

    Using the transformation y=2b​cosθ,

    L=ρ∞​U∞​Γ0​⋅2b​∫0π​sin2θ dθ

    💡
    L=ρ∞​U∞​Γ0​⋅4b​π

    The equation for lift can also be expressed in another form.

    21​ρ∞​U∞​2SCL​=ρ∞​U∞​Γ0​⋅4b​π

    💡
    CL​=2U∞​SΓ0​bπ​
    💡
    Γ0​=bπ2U∞​SCL​​

    #Induced Angle of Attack

    Recall that the induced angle of attack is given by αi​=U∞​−w​.

    Substituting the downwash velocity w(θ0​) into the equation,

    💡
    αi​=2bU∞​Γ0​​

    The equation states that αi​ is also a constant along the span for an elliptical wing.

    Substituting Γ0​ into the above equation,

    αi​=bπ2U∞​SCL​​⋅2bU∞​1​

    αi​=πb2SCL​​

    💡
    αi​=πARCL​​

    where AR is the aspect ratio of a finite wing given by AR=Sb2​

    #Induced Drag Coefficient

    With the induced angle of attack known, we can solve for induced drag coefficient.

    CDi​​=U∞​S2αi​​∫−b/2b/2​Γ(y) dy

    CDi​​=U∞​S2αi​Γ0​​⋅2b​∫0π​sin2θ dθ

    CDi​​=2U∞​Sπαi​Γ0​b​

    Substituting Γ0​ and αi​ into the above equation,

    CDi​​=2U∞​Sπb​⋅πARCL​​⋅bπ2U∞​SCL​​

    💡
    CDi​​=πARCL​2​

    The equation states that induced drag coefficient is proportional to the square of lift coefficient. Thus, induced drag is related to the production of lift on a finite wing.

    #Relation between CL​ and CD​

    The total drag of a wing is given by,

    💡
    CD​=CD0​​+CDi​​=CD0​​+πARCL​2​

    where k=πAR1​

    Plotting total drag coefficient CD​ against
    the lift coefficient CL​, we get a parabolic curve due to the squared CL​ term.

    For a cambered airfoil wing, minimum CD​ occurs at some value of CL​ as the airfoil produces lift even when α=0.

    As α decreases to zero-lift angle, drag increases due to a larger frontal area.

    For a symmetric airfoil wing, minimum drag occurs when lift is zero; when α=0.

    The lift-to-drag ratio can be expressed as,

    DL​=CD​CL​​=CD0​​+kCL​2CL​​

    To maximize DL​, differentiate the above with respect to CL​ and equate to zero.

    dCL​d(L/D)​=CD0​​+kCL​2CD0​​+kCL​2−CL​(2kCL​)​=0

    CD0​​+kCL​2CD0​​−kCL​2​=0

    💡
    CD0​​=kCL​2

    Maximum L/D occurs when zero-lift drag is equal to the induced drag. Thus,
    it is implied that induced drag accounts for approximately 50% of total drag.

    Rewriting the equation for maximum DL​,

    DL​max​=(CD0​​+kCL​2CL​​)max​

    DL​max​=2CD0​​CD0​​/k​​

    💡
    DL​max​=4kCD0​​1​​

    Note that kCL​2=CD0​​ for maximum DL​.

    #General Lift Distribution

    Consider a spanwise circulation distribution that can be represented by a Fourier sine
    series consisting of N terms.

    Γ(ϕ)=4(2b​)U∞​1∑N​An​sin(nϕ)

    The lift acting on the spanwise section
    for which the circulation is Γ(ϕ) is then,

    L′(ϕ)=ρ∞​U∞​Γ(ϕ)

    L′(ϕ)=4(2b​)ρ∞​U∞​21∑N​An​sin(nϕ)

    Typically, the series is truncated to a finite series and the coefficients are then evaluated
    by requiring the lifting-line equation to be satisfied at a number of spanwise locations
    equal to the number of terms in the series.

    The local lift coefficient per unit span is,

    CL​(ϕ)=21​ρ∞​U∞​2cL′​

    CL​(ϕ)=21​ρ∞​U∞​2cρ∞​U∞​Γ(ϕ)​

    CL​(ϕ)=U∞​c2Γ(ϕ)​

    From the definition of the lift curve,

    CL​=dαdCL​​(αeff​−αL=0​)

    Equating the two equations above,

    U∞​c2Γ(ϕ)​=dαdCL​​(αeff​−αL=0​)

    💡
    c(ϕ)a0​2Γ(ϕ)​=U∞​[α(ϕ)−αL=0​(ϕ)]−U∞​αi​(ϕ)

    where a0​=dαdCL​​; lift curve slope of the airfoil at that section

    There are five parameters in the equation that are functions of ϕ:

    • Γ the local circulation
    • c the chord length, which varies with ϕ for a tapered wing planform
    • αi​ the induced angle of attack, which depends on circulation distribution
    • αL=0​ the zero lift angle of attack, when airfoil section varies along the span
    • α the geometric angle of attack, which varies with ϕ when the wing is twisted

    Recall that the induced angle of attack is given by αi​=U∞​−w​.

    Substituting the Fourier series for Γ(ϕ) and using variable transformation,

    U∞​αi​(ϕ)=−w

    U∞​αi​(ϕ)=4π1​∫−b/2b/2​y0​−ydydΓ​ dy​

    U∞​αi​(ϕ)=U∞​sinϕ∑nAn​sin(nϕ)​

    Substituting the above into the initial equation,

    ca0​2Γ​=U∞​(α−αL=0​)−U∞​sinϕ∑nAn​sin(nϕ)​

    Since Γ=4(2b​)U∞​∑An​sin(nϕ), the equation then becomes,

    ca0​8(b/2)​∑An​sin(nϕ)=(α−αL=0​)−sinϕ∑nAn​sin(nϕ)​

    Defining μ=8(b/2)ca0​​, the resultant equation is,

    💡
    μ(α−αL=0​)sinϕ=∑An​sin(nϕ)⋅(μn+sinϕ)

    The above equation is known as the monoplane equation. If we consider only symmetrical
    lift distributions, only the odd terms of the series need to be considered. Even terms of the series gives a normal sine curve which is not symmetrical.

    #Lift Coefficient

    Recall that the lift distribution from the Kutta-Joukowski theorem is,

    L′(y)=ρ∞​U∞​Γ(y)

    The total lift on the wing can now be found,

    L=∫−b/2+b/2​ρ∞​U∞​Γ(y) dy

    L=∫0π​ρ∞​U∞​(2b​)Γ(ϕ)sinϕ dϕ

    L=4(2b​)2ρ∞​U∞​2∫0π​∑An​sin(nϕ)sinϕ dϕ

    Using trigonometric identities and solving the integration,

    L=4(2b​)2ρ∞​U∞​2{A1​[2ϕ​+4sin2ϕ​]0π​+3∑N​21​An​[n−1sin(n−1)ϕ​−n+1sin(n−1)ϕ​]0π​}

    The summation represented by the second term is zero for n=1.

    Thus, the integral for total lift becomes,

    💡
    L=4(2b​)2(21​ρ∞​U∞​2)A1​π

    The lift coefficient is then,

    (21​ρ∞​U∞​2S)CL​=4(2b​)2(21​ρ∞​U∞​2)A1​π

    💡
    CL​=A1​πAR

    The lift depends only on the magnitude of the first coefficient, no matter
    how many terms may be present in the series describing the distribution.

    #Induced Drag Coefficient

    With the lift coefficient known, we can now solve for the induced drag coefficient.

    Di​=−∫b/2b/2​ρ∞​wΓ dy

    Di​=ρ∞​∫0π​(sinϕU∞​∑An​sin(nϕ)​)(42b​U∞​∑An​sin(nϕ))sinϕ dϕ

    Di​=4(2b​)2ρ∞​U∞​2∫0π​∑nAn​sin(nϕ)∑An​sin(nϕ) dϕ

    Di​=4(2b​)2ρ∞​U∞​2⋅2π​∑nAn​2

    💡
    Di​=2π(2b​)2ρ∞​U∞​2∑nAn​2

    The induced drag coefficient is then,

    💡
    CDi​​=πAR∑nAn​2

    Since CL​=A1​πAR, the induced drag coefficient can be rewritten to,

    💡
    CDi​​=πARCL​2​∑n(A1​An​​)2

    where only odd terms in the series are considered for a symmetric lift distribution

    For a symmetric lift distribution,

    CDi​​=πARCL​2​[1+(A1​23A3​2​+A1​25A5​2​+A1​27A7​2​+...)]

    CDi​​=πARCL​2​(1+δ)

    💡
    CD1​​=πeARCL​2​

    where e=1+δ1​ with e=1 for an elliptical lift distribution

    #Effect of Aspect Ratio on CDi​​

    The total lift of a wing is given by,

    CD​=CD0​​+CDi​​

    CD​=CD0​​+πeARCL​2​

    Consider two wings with different aspect ratios AR1​ and AR2​. Assume that the wings are at the same CL​. Additionally, airfoil section is the same for both, thus CD0​​ is essentially the same.

    CD1​​=CD0​​+πeAR1​CL​2​

    CD2​​=CD0​​+πeAR2​CL​2​

    Subtracting one from the other,

    CD2​​−CD1​​=πeCL​2​(AR2​1​−AR1​1​)

    💡
    CD2​​=CD1​​+πeCL​2​(AR2​1​−AR1​1​)

    This equation can be used to scale the data of a wing with aspect
    ratio     AR2​ to correspond to the case of another aspect ratio AR1​.

    #Lift Curve Slope of a Wing

    Recall that the induced angle of attack for an elliptial wing is αi​=πARCL​​.

    Then, the geometric angle of attack for an elliptical lift distribution is,

    α=αeff​+αi​

    α=αeff​+πARCL​​

    From the lift curve of an airfoil,

    dαeff​dCL​​=d(α−αi​)dCL​​=a0​

    ∫dCL​=a0​∫d(α−αi​)

    CL​=a0​(α−αi​)+k

    CL​=a0​(α−πARCL​​)+k

    where a0​ is the lift curve slope of the airfoil

    Differentiating the lift coefficient with respect to α,

    💡
    a=dαdCL​​=1+πARa0​​a0​​

    The above equation is the lift curve slope for an elliptical wing.

    For a wing of arbitrary planform,

    💡
    a=dαdCL​​=1+πeARa0​​a0​​

    Note that both lift curve slope equations are expressed in CL​/rad.
