    Potential Flow Theory

    The study of fluid behaviour under ideal conditions — zero viscosity, steady, and 2D.

    #Irrotational and Rotational Flows

    Consider a 2D control volume undergoing deformation due to presence of velocity gradients.
    In a short time interval of dt, line segments OA and OB will rotate through the angles dα
    and dβ to the new positions OA′ and OB′.

    Angular velocity of OA is given by,

    ωOA​=dt → 0lim​ dtdα​

    For very small angles,

    tan dα≈dα=dx∂x∂v​dxdt​=∂x∂v​dt

    Substituting the above into angular velocity,

    ωOA​=dt → 0lim​ ∂x∂v​=∂x∂v​

    Similarly, the angular velocity of OB is given by,

    ωOB​=dt → 0lim​ ∂y∂u​=∂y∂u​

    Note that if ∂x∂v​ is positive, angular velocity of OA will be anticlockwise.

    Noe that if ∂y∂u​ is positive, angular velocity of OB will be clockwise.

    The rotation ωz​ of the element about the z-axis is defined as the average of
    the angular velocities ωOA​ and ωOB​ of the two mutually perpendicular lines.

    💡
    ωz​=21​(∂x∂v​−∂y∂u​)

    In fluid flow conventions, anticlockwise is positive whereas clockwise is negative.

    The rotation of the fluid element about the other two coordinate axes are,

    💡
    ωx​=21​(∂y∂w​−∂z∂v​)
    💡
    ωy​=21​(∂z∂u​−∂x∂w​)

    #Vorticity ζ

    The three components ωx​, ωy​, and ωz​ can be combined to give the rotation vector ω.

    ω=ωx​i+ωy​j+ωz​k

    ω=21​[(∂y∂w​−∂z∂v​)+(∂z∂u​−∂x∂w​)+(∂x∂v​−∂y∂u​)]

    ω=21​(∇×V)

    ∇×V=​i∂x∂​u​j∂y∂​v​k∂z∂​w​​

    ∇×V=(∂y∂w​−∂z∂v​)i+(∂z∂u​−∂x∂w​)j+(∂x∂v​−∂y∂u​)k

    The term vorticity ζ is defined as a vector that is twice the rotation vector.
    It is a measure of the rotation of a fluid element as it moves through a flow field.

    💡
    ζ=2ω=∇×V

    The above equation applies to 3-dimensional flows.

    For a 2-dimensional flow in the xy-plane,

    💡
    ζ=2ωz​=∂x∂v​−∂y∂u​

    If ζ=0, the flow is called irrotational (ideal).
    If     ζ=0, the flow is called rotational (real).

    Irrotational flow refers to the fluid element
    not being deformed by shear stresses.

    #Circulation Γ

    Circulation is defined as the line integral of
    the tangential velocity components about
    a closed curve that is fixed in a flow field.

    💡
    Γ=∮C​ V⋅ds

    It is the sum of the products of velocity and distance along a closed path around the flow
    field we are interested in. In some ways, it is a measure of the strength of the fluid flow.

    From the fluid element above, integrating in the anticlockwise direction,

    Γ=u dx+(v+∂x∂v​dx)dy−(u+∂y∂u​dy)dx−v dy

    Γ=(∂x∂v​−∂y∂u​)dxdy

    💡
    Γ=ζA

    The circulation around a closed contour is the sum of the vorticity enclosed within it.

    #Stream and Potential Functions

    #Stream Function ψ

    For 2-dimensional steady flows, a streamline is a line tangent to velocity vectors everywhere. As such, no flows across any streamlines are possible. Thus, every
    streamline in a flow can be said to have a unique function ψ associated with it.

    For a 2-dimensional incompressible flow in the xy-plane, the continuity equation is,

    💡
    ∂x∂u​+∂y∂v​=0

    The flow needs to satisfy the continuity equation to be considered a steady flow.

    The only unknown variables are u and v. Thus, we
    define a function ψ(x, y) to relate the velocities.

    💡
    u=∂y∂ψ​
    💡
    v=−∂x∂ψ​

    Substituting the relations into the continuity equation,

    ∂x∂​(∂y∂ψ​)+∂y∂​(−∂x∂ψ​)=0

    ∂x∂y∂2ψ​−∂x∂y∂2ψ​=0

    💡
    ∂x2∂2ψ​+∂y2∂2ψ​=0

    #Stream Function in Polar Coordinates

    In cylindrical coordinates, the continuity equation is,

    💡
    r1​∂r∂(rvr​)​+r1​∂θ∂vθ​​=0

    Velocities vr​ and vθ​ can be related to ψ(r, θ) through,

    💡
    vr​=r1​∂θ∂ψ​
    💡
    vθ​=−∂r∂ψ​

    #Interpreting the Stream Function

    The differential of ψ is given by,

    dψ=∂x∂ψ​dx+∂y∂ψ​dy

    dψ=−v dx+u dy

    The slope of a streamline at any point along it is,

    dxdy​=uv​ ⟶ udx​=vdy​ ⟶ u dy−v dx=0

    Thus, equating both equations, we get,

    💡
    dψ=u dy−v dx=0

    Therefore, the change in ψ is zero along a streamline
    or, lines along which     ψ is constant are streamlines.

    The product −v dx represents the volumetric flow
    rate per unit depth across AO, and the product u dy represents the volumetric flow rate across OB.

    By continuity, fluid crossing AO and OB must cross
    the curve AB. Thus, dψ is the volumetric flow rate
    per unit depth across AB.

    ψB​=ψA​+dψ

    dψ=ψB​−ψA​

    dψ is the volumetric flow rate between two streamlines. Thus, the volumetric flow rate between any two points in the flow is the difference between the values of the stream
    function at the two points of interest.

    #Velocity Potential ϕ

    For an irrotational flow, since ζ=0, the angular velocities ωx​=ωy​=ωz​=0.

    💡
    ∂y∂w​=∂z∂v​
    💡
    ∂z∂u​=∂x∂w​

    The velocity components can be expressed in terms of velocity potential ϕ(x, y, z, t).

    💡
    u=∂x∂ϕ​
    💡
    v=∂y∂ϕ​

    Substituting the relations into the continuity equation,

    💡
    ∂x2∂2ϕ​+∂y2∂2ϕ​=0​

    #Velocity Potential in Polar Coordinates

    In cylindrical coordinates, the velocity components are,

    💡
    vr​=∂r∂ϕ​
    💡
    vθ​=r1​∂θ∂ϕ​

    #Relation between Streamlines and Velocity Potential Lines

    For a potential function in two dimensions, the differential of ϕ is given by,

    dϕ=∂x∂ϕ​dx+∂y∂ϕ​dy=u dx+v dy

    For lines of constant potential dϕ=0,

    💡
    (dxdy​)ϕ=const​=−vu​

    For a streamline dψ=0,

    💡
    (dxdy​)ψ=const​=uv​

    The slope of a streamline is the negative reciprocal of the slope of a velocity potential line. Therefore, streamlines everywhere are orthogonal to velocity potential lines.

    💡
    (dxdy​)ψ=c​=−(dxdy​)ϕ=c​1​

    #Basic Plane Potential Flows

    A potential flow is characterized by incompressiblility and irrotationality ζ=0.

    #Uniform Flow

    A uniform flow is a flow whereby streamlines are all straight and parallel.

    Expressing in terms of the stream function,

    we get ∂y∂ψ​=U and ∂x∂ψ​=0

    Expressing in terms of the velocity potential,

    we get ∂x∂ϕ​=U and ∂y∂ϕ​=0

    Integrating both functions,

    💡
    ψ=Uy=Ursinθ
    💡
    ϕ=Ux=Urcosθ

    For uniform flow at some arbitrary angle,

    💡
    ψ=U(ycosα−xsinα)
    💡
    ϕ=U(xcosα+ysinα)

    #Source/Sink

    Consider a fluid flowing radially outward from a line through the origin.
    Let Q be the volume flow rate per unit length emanating from the line.

    💡
    Q=(2πr)vr​ ⟶ vr​=2πrQ​

    If Q is positive, the flow is radially outwards, and the flow is called a source flow.
    If     Q is negative, the flow is toward the origin, and the flow is called a sink flow.
    The flowrate     Q is the strength of the source or sink.

    Forming the stream functions,
    we get vr​=r1​∂θ∂ψ​=2πrQ​ and vθ​=0.

    Forming the velocity potential functions,
    we get vr​=∂r∂ϕ​=2πrQ​ and vθ​=0.

    Integrating both expressions for vr​,

    💡
    ψ=2πQ​⋅θ
    💡
    ϕ=2πQ​ ln(r)

    Streamlines (constant ψ) are radial lines. Velocity potential lines are concentric circles centered at the origin.

    #Vortex

    Consider a flow field in which the streamlines are concentric circles — vortex.

    💡
    ψ=−2πΓ​ ln(r)
    💡
    ϕ=2πΓ​ θ

    where K=2πΓ​ is a constant

    Differentiating the above equations,

    💡
    vθ​=−∂r∂ψ​=r1​∂θ∂ϕ​=2πrΓ​

    Irrotational (free) vortex:

    • Stick aligned to streamline follows a circular path and rotates anticlockwise
    • Other stick (originally vertical at A) rotates clockwise due to the nature of
      the flow field, whereby the part of the stick nearest the origin moves faster

    Rotational (forced) vortex:

    • Fluid behaves like a solid-body rotation
    • Described by vθ​=K1​⋅r where K1​ is a constant

    A combined vortex is one with a forced vortex as a central core and a velocity distribution corresponding to that of a free vortex outside the core. For a combined vortex, the velocity distribution in the central core is vθ​=rω (r≤r0​) and vθ​=rK​ (r>r0​).

    #Doublet

    Consider an equal strength source-sink pair. The combined stream function is,

    💡
    ψ=−2πQ​(θ1​−θ2​)

    Rewriting the above equation,

    tan(−Q2πψ​)=tan(θ1​−θ2​)

    tan(−Q2πψ​)=1+tanθ1​tanθ2​tanθ1​−tanθ2​​

    From the diagram on the right, we know

    tanθ1​=rcosθ−arsinθ​ and tanθ2​=rcosθ+arsinθ​

    Substituting tanθ1​ and tanθ2​,

    tan(−Q2πψ​)=r2−a22arsinθ​

    💡
    ψ=−2πQ​tan−1(r2−a22arsinθ​)

    For small values of the distance a,

    ψ=−2πQ​⋅r2−a22arsinθ​

    ψ=−π(r2−a2)Qarsinθ​

    where πQa​ is assumed to be a constant

    Let K=πQa​ to be the doublet strength.

    The doublet is formed by letting the source and sink
    approach one another     a→0, while increasing the
    strength     Q→∞ such that K is a constant.

    As a→0,

    💡
    ψ=−rKsinθ​
    💡
    ϕ=rKcosθ​

    where K=πQa​

    Doublets, like sources and sinks are not physically realistic entities. However, when combined with other basic potential flows, provides a useful representation of
    some flow fields of practical interest.

    #Superposition of Basic Flows

    #Source in Uniform Flow — Rankine Half-Body

    Consider the superposition of a uniform flow and a source.

    💡
    ψ=Ursinθ+2πQ​⋅θ
    💡
    ϕ=Urcosθ+2πQ​ ln(r)

    The components of the stream function are,

    💡
    vr​=r1​∂θ∂ψ​=Ucosθ+2πrQ​
    💡
    vθ​=−∂r∂ψ​=−Usinθ

    Recall that radial velocity of a source is vr​=2πrQ​.

    At the stagnation point, as vr​→0, we get U=2πbQ​.

    💡
    b=2πUQ​

    where b is the distance of the stagnation point from the source

    Substituting r=b and θ=π into the stream function,

    💡
    ψstagnation​=2Q​=πbU

    The streamline passing through the stagnation point is then,

    πbU=Ursinθ+bU⋅θ

    💡
    r=sinθb(π−θ)​

    where r is the radial distance from the source in
    which a point lies on the stagnation streamline

    For a given freestream velocity U, the width of the
    half-body increases with the source strength Q.

    Since y=rsinθ,

    💡
    y=b(π−θ)

    As θ→0 on the open end, the half-width becomes,

    💡
    y=±bπ

    As x tends towards infinity, the angle θ that r makes
    with respect to the horizontal tends towards zero.

    The square of the magnitude of the velocity V at any point is,

    V2=vr​2+vθ​2

    V2=U2+πrUQcosθ​+(2πrQ​)2

    Since b=2πUQ​,

    V2=U2[1+πrUQcosθ​+U21​(2πrQ​)2]

    💡
    V2=U2(1+r2bcosθ​+r2b2​)

    With the velocity at any point known, the pressure at any point can be determined
    from the Bernoulli equation, which can be written between any two points in the
    flow field since the flow is irrotational.

    #Source and Sink in Uniform Flow — Rankine Oval

    The half-body is a body that is “open” at one end. To simulate flow around a closed
    body, a source and a sink of equal strength can be combined with a uniform flow.

    💡
    ψ=Ursinθ−2πQ​(θ1​−θ2​)
    💡
    ϕ=Urcosθ−2πQ​(ln r1​−ln r2​)

    Rewriting the stream function for the source-sink pair,

    💡
    ψ=Ursinθ−2πQ​tan−1(x2+y2−a22arsinθ​)

    Note that r2=x2+y2. The plot of the streamline ψ=0 forms a closed
    body, whereby all the flow emanating from the source flows into the sink.

    Stagnation points occur at the upstream and downstream ends of the body. These
    points correspond to the points where uniform flow velocity, source velocity, and
    sink velocity all combine to give a zero velocity.

    The body half-length is given by,

    💡
    l=πUQa​+a2​

    The body half-width is the value of y where the y-axis intersects the ψ=0 streamline.
    Thus, from the stream function, we let ψ=0, x=0 and y=h.

    0=Uh−2πQ​tan−1(h2−a22ah​)

    Q2πUh​=tan−1(h2−a22ah​)

    tanQ2πUh​=h2−a22ah​

    h=2ah2−a2​ tanQ2πUh​

    💡
    ah​=21​[(ah​)2−1]tan[2(QπUa​)ah​]

    #Doublet in Uniform Flow — Cylinder

    When distance between the source-sink pair approaches zero, shape of the Rankine
    oval becomes more blunt and approaches a circular shape. Uniform flow in the
    positive x-direction combined with a doublet can be used to represent flow
    around a circular cylinder.

    💡
    ψ=Ursinθ−rKsinθ​
    💡
    ϕ=Urcosθ+rKcosθ​

    where K=Ua2

    In order for the stream function to represent flow around a circular cylinder,
    it is necessary that ψ=0 for r=a, where a is the radius of the cylinder.

    ψ=(U−r2K​)rsinθ

    0=U−a2K​

    K=Ua2

    The stream function and corresponding velocity potential function becomes,

    💡
    ψ=Ursinθ(1−r2a2​)
    💡
    ϕ=Urcosθ(1+r2a2​)

    Velocity components can be obtained from differentiation of the above functions.

    vr​=r1​∂θ∂ψ​=∂r∂ϕ​

    💡
    vr​=Ucosθ(1−r2a2​)

    vθ​=−∂r∂ψ​=r1​∂θ∂ϕ​

    💡
    vθ​=−Usinθ(1+r2a2​)

    The maximum velocity occurs at the top and bottom of the cylinder (θ=±2π​) and has
    a magnitude of twice the upstream velocity     U. As we move away from the cylinder, the decreases from Vθs​=2U to Vθ​=0.

    #Free Vortex and Cylinder — Rotating Cylinder

    Consider adding a free vortex to the flow around a cylinder.
    Free vortex and doublet in uniform flow combined to create a rotating cylinder.

    💡
    ψ=Ursinθ(1−r2a2​)−2πΓ​ ln(r)
    💡
    ϕ=Urcosθ(1+r2a2​)+2πΓ​ θ

    where Γ is the strength or circulation of the free vortex

    Differentiating the above, we get the polar velocity components,

    💡
    vr​=Ucosθ(1−r2a2​)
    💡
    vθ​=−Usinθ(1+r2a2​)+2πrΓ​

    The tangential velocity vθ​ on the surface of the cylinder whereby r=a,

    💡
    vθs​=−2Usinθ+2πaΓ​

    Stagnation points occur at locations where vθs​=0.

    Location of stagnation points,

    💡
    sinθstagnation​=4πUaΓ​

    If the RHS>1, the above equation cannot be satisfied, and the stagnation point is located away from the cylinder.
