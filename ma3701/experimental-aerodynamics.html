    Experimental Aerodynamics

    #Wind and Water Tunnels

    Wind and water tunnels provide a means for aerodynamic testing of objects by having
    fluid flow past the object, instead of moving the object through a stationary fluid,
    which may require a large area for the object to be tested at the desired speed.

    Characteristics of an effective wind/water tunnel:

    • No spanwise flow
    • Very low turbulence intensity levels
    • Uniform velocity distribution within test section

    #Open-Loop Wind Tunnel

    Description of wind tunnel:

    • Consists of a fan drawing air into the tunnel and exhausting it out
    • Flow conditioning devices located upstream of test section
      straighten the airflow to ensure low turbulence intensities
    • Diffuser located downstream of test section minimize
      disturbance caused by the fan to the airflow
    • Large fan is more efficient than a small fan

    #Closed-Loop Wind Tunnel

    Description of wind tunnel:

    • Air is recirculated within the tunnel, instead of exhausted out
    • Guide vanes at tunnel’s corners ease the airflow into the turn
    • Provides a better flow quality (controlled environment)
    • Air flow experiences less energy loss
    • Wind tunnel is quieter
    • High cost and maintenance

    #Water Tunnel

    Advantages of water tunnel:

    • Suitable for lower Re flows
    • More flow visualization options
    • Accurate velocity measurements

    Disadvantages of water tunnel:

    • Higher turbulence levels (1% - 3%)
    • Lower flow speeds (<1 m/s)

    #Flow Visualization

    Flow visualization is the art of making flow patterns visible. Most fluids
    are transparent, thus their flow patterns are invisible to the naked eye.

    Flow visualization techniques:

    • Smoke/dye visualization
    • Tufts
    • Pressure/temperature sensitive paint
    • Schlieren photography
    • Hydrogen bubble technique
    • Laser-induced fluorescence

    The use of tracers assumes that it follows the fluid flow. However, in a real-world scenario,
    the tracers tend to deviate from the flow the further away they are from the release point.
    Smoke, dye, and particles are incapable of tracking vorticity (vortices) accurately.

    #Smoke Visualization

    Description of technique:

    • Capture the instantaneous streamlines of
      the flow around the wind tunnel model
    • Indicates flow separation, reattachment
      and recirculation regions that occur
    • Dry air can be used to produce smoke

    #Dye Visualization

    Description of technique:

    • Dye is injected into the fluid through needles, nozzles, or tubes
    • Dye should be non-toxic, neutrally buoyant, and not chemically reactive
    • Flow patterns are captured using standard or high-speed cameras
    • UV lamps can enhance visibility if fluorescence dye is used

    #Tufts

    Description of technique:

    • Short lengths of string attached to the
      surface of the model using tape/glue
    • Direction of airflow is indicated by the
      direction of the tufts, while fluttering
      tufts indicate turbulent airflow
    • Relatively cheap to produce
    • Time-consuming to apply on the model
      and must be firmly secured so that they
      are not blown off the surface

    #Pressure/Temperature Sensitive Paint

    Description of technique:

    • Special paints which respond to moderate to large fluctuations in pressure or temperature
    • Capable of measuring absolute pressure if pressure-luminance relationship is properly calibrated
    • Typically used in high speed/temperatures

    Experimental process:

    • Paint is calibrated by exposing a coated surface to known pressure conditions to establish a relationship between the intensity of the emitted light and the local pressure (pressure-luminance relationship; calibration curve)
    • To measure pressure, an excitation light source illuminates coated surface with light
      of a specific wavelength that excites the paint and causes it to emit fluorescent light
    • Intensity of emitted light is proportional to the pressure at that location
    • Camera captures the light to obtain pressure distribution on the surface

    #Schlieren Photography

    Description of technique:

    • Takes advantage of the fact that changes in
      the density of a transparent medium cause
      variations in its refractive index
    • Variations in refractive index results in changes
      in light direction which is captured by a camera

    #Hydrogen Bubble Technique

    Description of technique:

    • Hydrogen bubbles, which are much finer,
      are used to visualize flow patterns in water
    • Camera captures the movement of bubbles
      as they become entrained and carried along
      by the flow

    #Laser-induced Fluorescence

    Description of technique:

    • Works based on the principle of selectively exciting molecules with laser light, causing
      them to emit fluorescent light
    • Fluorescent light is captured by a camera
    • Useful for analyzing flow cross-sections

    Measuring intensity, or lifetime of the fluorescence provides information about the concentration, distribution, and characteristics of the flow.

    #Data Measurement and Acquisition

    #Force and Moment Measurement

    Force/moments can be measured using load cells or
    force transducers. A strain gauge based load cell is typically used for its high accuracy.

    #Pressure Measurement

    Pressure measurements can be made using:

    • Pitot-static probe
    • Multi-hole pressure probe

    Description of multi-hole pressure probe:

    • Able to discern velocity direction
    • Pressure differentials between each hole provides information on relative angle
      between the probe and flow direction

    However, pitot-static probe is unable to respond fast enough to changes in pressure.
    Thus, for dynamic pressure measurements, pressure transducers are used instead.

    Types of pressure transducers:

    • Piezoelectric transducer
      • Measures pressure directly
      • Generates a voltage (electrical signal) proportional to the pressure
    • Variable resistance
      • Variation in strain gauge wire resistance in a Whitstone bridge configuration
    • Variable inductance
      • Elastic magnetic diaphragm located between two magnetic coils
      • Pressure changes causes changes in inductance between the coils
    • Capacitance
      • Elastic diaphragm that serves as one plate of a capacitor

    Difficulties with significant number of pressure measurements:

    • Pressure transducers are expensive; economically unfeasible to attach a transducer
      to every pressure tap (experiments may have up to hundreds of pressure tappings)
    • Scanivalves are used — pressure in each tap is scanned very rapidly
    • High frequency response pressure transducers have to be used

    Difficulties with PVC tubing:

    • Typically 0.5m in diameter and more than 0.5m in length
    • Air in tubing leads to resonance at certain frequencies
    • Measured pressures may deviate from actual pressures

    To minimize errors due to PVC tubing, a multistage tubing system can be used.

    • Larger diameter tubings → signal amplification
    • Smaller diameter tubings → signal attenuation
    • Combination of different-sized diameter tubings to achieve a “flat” response
    • Laborious and unreliable

    A more feasible method is the tubing frequency response correction method.

    • PVC tubing is calibrated against a short reference tubing
    • Obtain frequency response in the form of a Fourier series
    • Apply correction factors on coefficients of Fourier series
    • Perform an inverse Fourier transformation

    #Blockage Effects

    A large test model in a wind/water tunnel may lead to blockage in the flow
    which render measurements inaccurate. The projected area of the model
    should not exceed 5% of the cross-sectional area of the tunnel.

    Types of blockage:

    • Solid body blockage — due to size of the model
    • Wake blockage — due to size of the downstream wake region

    Types of correction methods:

    • Bluff body flow method
    • Quasi-streamlined flow method
    • Wall pressure signature method

    #Data Acquisition

    Description of data acqusition:

    • Process in which analog signals from instruments
      are acquired and processed into digital signals
    • The higher the sampling frequency, the better
    • High sampling frequency produces large datasets
      which are more time-consuming to process

    Fast Fourier transform:

    • Used to find the peak frequencies of fluid flows
    • Fluid flows usually have higher energy levels at lower frequencies and vice versa
    • Higher frequency components (turbulence) damp out faster

    Nyquist frequency:

    • Improper sampling frequency can result in errors (aliasing effects)
    • Frequencies higher than sampling frequency appear as a low frequency
    • Only frequencies below the Nyquist frequency can be measured correctly

    #Hot-Wire Anemometry

    Description of technique:

    • Based on the cooling effect of fluid flow on a heated wire
    • Heated wire is maintained at a constant temperature
    • Higher fluid velocity results in a higher cooling rate, which increases the
      current drawn by system to maintain the wire at a constant temperature
    • If fluid is air → hot-wire probe, if fluid is water → hot-film probe
    • Probe is calibrated by exposing it to known airflows or velocities
    • Measurements tend to drift over time due to variation in impedance,
      temperature fluctuations, or the presence of water vapor

    Advantages of hot-wire anemometry:

    • Relatively inexpensive
    • Simple working principle
    • High sensitivity and fast response
    • Ability to measure flow velocities in turbulent and unsteady flow conditions

    Disadvantages of hot-wire anemometry:

    • Wire is extremely fragile
    • Susceptible towards overheating
    • Contamination of wire due to dust
    • Sensitive towards temperature variations
    • Frequent calibration of system is required
    • Intrusive — probe needs to be physically placed in the flow

    #Laser Doppler Anemometry

    Description of technique:

    • Uses two laser beams of different frequencies to produce an intersection volume
    • Tracer particles (microspheres, oil droplets, etc) are introduced into the flow
    • When laser beams hit the tracer particles, the particles scatter the light
    • Scattering of light causes a frequency shift (Doppler effect)
    • Frequency shift is proportional to the velocity of the particles

    Advantages of laser doppler anemometry:

    • Robust
    • High frequency response
    • No calibration required (absolute measurement)
    • More accurate than hot-wire anemometry (no signal drift)
    • Non-intrusive (does not require a probe to be placed in the flow)

    Disadvantages of laser doppler anemometry:

    • Significantly more expensive than hot-wire anemometry
    • Requires more setup preparation
    • Requires tracer particles
    • Requires optical access
    • Not feasible for curved surfaces

    #Particle Image Velocimetry

    Description of technique:

    • Fluid is seeded with small, neutrally buoyant, tracer particles
    • If fluid is air → smoke/oil droplets are used, if fluid is water → microspheres are used
    • Laser light source is used to create a thin light sheet that illuminates the region of interest
    • High-speed camera records the motion of the suspended particles within this region
    • Time interval between two images can be used to determine particle velocity
    • Calculated velocity vectors are used to create a velocity field of the fluid

    Other information:

    • Laser must be capable of firing short pulses of light along the same plane
    • Laser must be sufficiently bright to illuminate the tracer particles within the flow
    • Measurement area cannot be too large as particles will look smaller and reflect less light

    Advantages of particle image velocimetry:

    • Fast experiment
    • Non-intrusive
    • Global velocity field measurement
    • Other variables can be derived from velocity measurements

    Disadvantages of particle image velocimetry:

    • Low frequency response
    • Significant post-processing time
    • Large datasets results in high storage requirement
    • Each vector is an average over a square cross-correlation window
