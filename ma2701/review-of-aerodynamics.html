    MA2701 · FLIGHT PERFORMANCE

    Review of Aerodynamics

    Aerodynamics is the study of forces acting on objects moving through air. The performance
    of an aircraft is greatly influenced by aerodynamic forces generated by the wings, horizontal
    and vertical stabilizers, and control surfaces.

    #Lift and Drag (Airfoil)

    #Airfoil Geometry

    Terms describing an airfoil:

    • Leading edge
    • Leading edge radius
    • Trailing edge
    • Trailing edge angle
    • Chord line
    • Camber line
    • Maximum camber
    • Maximum thickness

    #Pressure Distribution

    #Angle of Attack

    Angle of attack α is the angle between
    the chord line and the direction of the freestream velocity.

    Based on Bernoulli’s equation, an increase in velocity, and thus dynamic pressure, results
    in a decrease in static pressure, and vice
    versa. Hence, a net force is generated.

    #Center of Pressure

    Center of pressure cp:

    • Point at which aerodynamic forces act
    • Centroid of the pressure distribution
    • Moment about cp is zero
    • Location changes with α

    Components of resultant aerodynamic force normal to and parallel to the freestream
    velocity are the lift and drag respectively.

    #Aerodynamic Center

    Aerodynamic center ac:

    • Mac​ remains nearly constant for all α
    • L and D can be taken to act through this point with the addition of a pitch moment

    #Boundary Layer Theory

    An airfoil surface is not only subjected to pressure but also shear stresses due to
    viscous effects in the boundary layer.

    Viscous shear stresses, due to retardation of particles within the boundary layer, is called
    skin friction.

    Shear stress at any point is given by,

    💡
    τ=μdydv​ ⟶ τ=δ2μV∞​​

    Distance from the surface where velocity is equal to 99% of the freestream velocity is defined as boundary layer thickness δ.

    #Laminar vs Turbulent Boundary Layer

    The type of flow can be determined using Reynold’s number.

    💡
    Re=μρVc​

    where c is the chord length of the airfoil

    Laminar boundary layer:

    • Flow is steady, smooth and parallel
    • Velocity gradient is less steep
    • Thinner bounrday layer δl​
    • Low skin friction

    Turbulent boundary layer:

    • Flow is unsteady (contains eddies)
    • Velocity gradient is steeper
    • Thicker boundary layer δt​
    • High skin friction

    #Flow Separation of an Airfoil

    For small values of α, total drag of an airfoil is predominantly skin friction drag.
    For large values of α, total drag of an airfoil is predominantly pressure/form drag.

    Pressure drag is dependent on the size of the wake region genereated by the airfoil.

    At zero angle of attack:

    • Pressure reaches minimum value at A, and gradually rises to a value close
      to original freestream pressure at B
    • If pressure increase is gradual, turbulent mixing allows outer layers to effectively pull the inner layers along
    • Boundary layer merely thickens, leaving
      a slow-moving wake at the trailing edge

    At low angle of attack:

    • Rate of pressure increase is rapid
    • Mixing process is too slow to keep the lower part of the boundary layer moving
    • Inner layers tend to move towards the lower pressure, in the reverse direction
      to the main flow

    At high angle of attack:

    • Pressure difference between A and B increases, causing separation position
      to move forwards
    • Wake drastically increases in size

    #Lift, Drag, and Pitch Moment

    The respective formulas for lift, drag, and pitching moment are,

    💡
    L=21​ρV∞​2⋅S⋅Cl​
    💡
    D=21​ρV∞​2⋅S⋅Cd​
    💡
    Mac​=21​ρV∞​2⋅S⋅c⋅Cm​

    The pitch moment is positive if it tends to push the aircraft nose upwards and vice versa.
    Note that a lowercase subscript represents the coefficient for a 2D airfoil, whereas an uppercase subscript represents the coefficient for a 3D airfoil (wing).

    For a given Reynolds number and Mach number, the lift, drag, and pitch moment coefficients of an airfoil are purely functions of angle of attack α, as shown below.

    #Lift Curve of a Symmetrical Airfoil

    Factors affecting Clmax​​:

    • Sharp leading edge radius causes early flow separation and decreases Clmax​​
    • Thicker airfoils, up to a thickness ratio ct​=0.12, increases Clmax​​
    • Positive camber increases Clmax​​ but decreases stall angle
    • High Reynolds number increases Clmax​​

    #Lift Curve of a Cambered Airfoil

    The linear region of the lift slope can be expressed as,

    💡
    Cl​=Clα​(α−α0​)

    where α0​ is the angle at which a cambered airfoil generates zero lift

    #Drag Curve of a Cambered Airfoil

    Drag produced by a two-dimensional airfoil consists of:

    • Skin friction drag
    • Pressure/form/wake drag
    • Wave/compressibility drag (high Mach number flows)

    Drag at low angles of attack, before stall angle, is relatively constant as
    the predominant drag is skin friction drag, which is dependent on the airfoil
    surface area in contact with the air, and the surface roughness of the airfoil.

    #Drag Polar of an Airfoil

    The graph of Cd​ plotted against Cl​ is called the drag polar.

    Note that for a cambered airfoil, the minimum drag coefficient occurs at some value
    of lift coefficient as a cambered airfoil generates lift at an angle of attack of zero. For
    a symmetrical airfoil, minimum drag coefficient will occur at a lift coefficient of zero,
    and the drag polar will be symmetrical about the vertical axis.

    #Pitch Moment Curve of an Airfoil

    The pitching moment is usually calculated about the aerodynamic center, typically located at the quarter chord position. This moment remains nearly constant over
    the normal operating range of angle of attack.

    For a cambered airfoil, center of pressure typically lies behind the aerodynamic
    center, thus, when taking moments about the aerodynamic center, the lift and
    drag forces tends to create an anticlockwise moment — nose-down.

    #Effect of Mach Number on Cl​ and Cd​

    #Effect on Lift Coefficient

    Description of graph:

    • In subsonic region, Cl​ increases gradually as M∞​ increases
    • In transonic region, Cl​ decreases slightly due to flow separation
    • In supersonic region, Cl​ decreases drastically due to shockwaves

    #Effect on Drag Coefficient

    Description of graph:

    • At M∞​<Mcr​, relatively constant Cd​ (mainly due to skin friction drag)
    • At M∞​>Mcr​, slight increase in Cd​ due to transonic flow separation as
      a result of shockwave formation over the airfoil (contributes to wave drag)
    • At M∞​>Mdd​, steep rise in Cd​ due to severe flow separation

    #Lift Characteristics with High-Lift Devices

    Description of flap:

    • Deflectable device at the trailing edge of an airfoil
    • Increases effective camber, and thus effective α
    • Higher effective α allows for a lower V∞​

    Description of slot:

    • Passageway between top and bottom surface
    • Energizes airflow over the top surface to overcome the adverse pressure gradient and thus, delay the onset of flow separation

    Description of slat:

    • Movable device attached to the leading edge
    • Creates a slot when deployed

    #Lift Curve with High-Lift Devices Deployed

    Effect of deflecting flap down (same α):

    • Lift curve shifts to the left
    • Cl​ increases at the cost of drag
    • αstall​ decreases due to higher effective α

    Effect of leading edge slats:

    • Delay the onset of flow separation
    • Clmax​​ and thus αstall​ increases

    Effect of trailing edge slotted flaps:

    • Increases camber and thus effective α
    • Slots delay the onset of flow separation
    • Increases effective chord and thus wing area

    #Flap Effectiveness τ

    The use of flaps results in an increase in the lift coefficient ΔCl​.

    Cl​=Clα​(α−α0​)+ΔCl​

    Cl​=Clα​(α−α0​)+Clδf​​⋅δf​

    Cl​=Clα​[(α−α0​)+Clα​Clδf​​​⋅δf​]

    💡
    Cl​=Clα​[(α−α0​)+τδf​]

    where τ is the flap effectiveness

    Since Clα​=dαdCl​​ and Clδf​​=dδf​dCl​​,

    💡
    τ=dδf​dCl​​⋅dCl​dα​=dδf​dα​

    Flap effectiveness can be interpreted as: a δf​ degree of flap deflection
    is equivalent to     τδf​ degree of increase in angle of attack of the airfoil.

    #Lift and Drag (Wing)

    #Wing Geometry

    Nomenclature of the wing:

    • S — wing area
    • b — wing span
    • cˉ — mean chord
    • cr​ — root chord
    • ct​ — tip chord
    • cr​ct​​ — taper ratio
    • ΛLE​ — sweep angle at leading adge
    • Λc/4​ — sweep angle at quarter-chord

    #Wing Flow Field

    Wing tip vortices:

    • Consequence of a finite wing
    • Tendency for flow to “leak” around wing tips
    • Contributes to the downwash of the aircraft
      and thus induced drag of the aircraft

    Downwash:

    • Net effect of vortices
    • Induces a downward component of air velocity in the vicinity of the wing that in turn decreases the effective angle of attack of the wing

    #Induced Drag

    Recall from aerodynamics the effective angle of attack is,

    💡
    αeff​=α−αi​

    The airfoil at that section of the wing encounters a stream at an effective angle that is lower than the wing’s geometric angle of attack due to the downwash. Thus, there is a component of lift in the direction of the stream which contributes to drag.

    Thus, the drag due to lift is,

    Di​=Lcosαi​≈Lαi​

    Recall that the induced angle of attack for an elliptical lift distribution is,

    💡
    αi​=πARCL​​

    Substituting αi​ into Di​,

    Di​=L⋅πARCL​​

    Di​=21​ρ∞​V∞​2S⋅CL​⋅πARCL​​

    💡
    CDi​​=πARCL​2​

    For a wing of arbitrary planform,

    💡
    CDi​​=πeARCL​2​

    #Effect on Lift Curve Slope

    From a lift curve of an airfoil,

    dαeff​dCL​​=d(α−αi​)dCL​​=a0​

    ∫dCL​=a0​∫d(α−αi​)

    CL​=a0​(α−αi​)+k

    CL​=a0​(α−πeARCL​​)+k

    where a0​ is the slope of the airfoil

    Differentiating with respect to α,

    💡
    a=dαdCL​​=1+πeARa0​​a0​​

    Lift curve slope for an arbitrary wing.

    Recall from aerodynamics that as aspect ratio of a wing increases, lift curve slope of
    a wing approaches the theoretical maximum lift curve slope of an airfoil of 2π/rad.

    #Compressibility Correction to Lift Curve Slope

    For high aspect ratio wings AR>4, lift curve slope of a wing can be expressed in different forms depending on the regime of the flow — subsonic or supersonic.

    💡
    a=1+πeARa0​​a0​​ (straight wing, incompressible flow)
    💡
    acomp​=1−M∞​2​+πeARa0​​a0​​ (straight wing, subsonic flow where M∞​<0.7)
    💡
    acomp​=M∞​2−1​4​ (straight wing, supersonic flow)

    Similarly, for low aspect ratio wings AR<4, the different forms of the equation are,

    💡
    a=1+[πARa0​​]2​+πARa0​​a0​​ (straight wing, incompressible flow)
    💡
    acomp​=1−M∞​2+[πARa0​​]2​+πARa0​​a0​​ (straight wing, subsonic flow)
    💡
    acomp​=M∞​2−1​4​(1−2ARM∞​2−1​1​) (straight wing, supersonic flow)

    Note that for swept wings, a0​ is replaced with a0​cosΛc/2​ in the above equations.

    #Lift and Drag (Aircraft)

    #Aircraft Lift

    For the purpose of this course, lift generated by the fuselage is considered to be
    negligible compared to the lift generated by the wings. Thus, the lift curve of the
    wing is assumed to be applicable for the entire aircraft — values of CLα​, CLmax​​,
    α0​ and αstall​ for the wing will be applied to the aircraft.

    #Aircraft Drag

    Recall that for an airfoil, drag consists only of:

    • Profile drag (skin friction and pressure drag)
    • Compressibility drag (high Mach number flows)

    For an aircraft however, drag consists of:

    • Parasite drag (skin friction, pressure drag and interference drag)
    • Compressibility drag (high Mach number flows)
    • Induced drag (due to lift generation)

    Skin friction is calculated as an average, based on flow over a thin flat plate, at the flight Reynolds number, which is then multiplied by the aircraft’s wetted area. Interference drag
    is used to account for additional drag as a result of the interference of one component in
    the presence of other components.

    #Compressibility/Wave Drag

    The flow regime can be categorized into:

    RegimeSubsonicTransonicSonicSupersonicHypersonic
    Mach<0.80.8 - 1.21.01.2 - 5.05.0 - 10.0

    Recall that for an airfoil, beyond the drag-divergence Mach number Mdd​, the airfoil experiences a sharp increase in drag due to the formation of shock waves on the surfaces of the airfoil, which in turn results in early flow separation.

    Similarly, for an aircraft, at Mach numbers
    beyond Mdd​, there is a significant rise in
    (wave) drag.

    However, the Mach number at which this occurs is dependent on the planform of
    the wing — e.g. a swept wing delays the onset of shockwave formation.

    Other factors that affect Mcritical​ and thus Mdd​:

    • Thickness of wing section — the lower the thickness ratio, the higher the Mcrit​
    • Wing aspect ratio — the lower the aspect ratio, the higher the Mcrit​
    • Angle of attack — the lower the angle of attack, the higher the Mcrit​
    • Wing planform shape — a swept wing increases the Mcrit​

    A specially designed airfoil, called the supercritical airfoil, serves to increase Mcrit​.
    The airfoil has a flatter upper surface with a highly cambered aft section. It also
    has a larger leading edge radius.

    #Drag Curve of an Aircraft

    The total drag coefficient of an aircraft is,

    💡
    CD​=CD0​​+CDi​​+CDwave​​

    Note that CDi​​=kCL​2 where k=πeAR1​.

    Consider an aircraft in steady level flight where L=W and D=T.

    Total drag of the aircraft is equal to thrust required such that acceleration is zero.

    D=TR​=21​ρV2S⋅CD​

    D=TR​=21​ρV2S(CD0​​+kCL​2)

    Rearranging the lift equation,

    L=W=21​ρV2SCL​

    CL​=21​ρV2SW​

    Substituting CL​ into the drag equation,

    💡
    D=TR​=21​ρV2SCD0​​+21​ρV2SkW2​

    The first term represents parasitic drag, while the second represents induced drag.

    Let A=21​ρSCD0​​ and B=21​ρSkW2​.

    The total drag is then D=AV2+V2B​.

    Differentiating with respect to V and equating to zero for minimum drag speed,

    dVdD​=2AV−2V3B​=0

    2AV=V32B​

    V4=AB​

    💡
    VDmin​​=ρS2W​CD0​​k​​​

    Due to the effects of stall at low speeds and compressibility effects at high speeds, the drag curve deviates at these speeds.

    W=21​ρVstall​2SCLmax​​

    Vstall​=ρSCLmax​​2W​​

    From the minimum drag speed equation, as weight increases, VDmin​​ increases as well, causing the curve to shift to the right.

    Also, since CDi​​=kCL​2, an increase in weight requires an increase in lift. Thus, resulting in an increase in induced drag, causing the curve to shift upwards.

    As induced drag is dependent on lift, and hence weight, left side of the drag curve is influenced more by the increase in weight.

    As parasitic drag is independent of weight,
    the left side is less sensitive to changes in weight, such that the two curves tend to coincide at high speeds.

    An increase in altitude results in an increase in speed so as to generate the same amount of lift when density decreases. Thus, the drag curve shifts to the right.

    Since minimum drag is constant with respect to altitude,

    21​ρ0​V0​2S⋅CD​=21​ρh​Vh​2S⋅CD​

    Vh​=V0​ρh​ρ0​​​

    💡
    Vh​=V0​σ1​​

    where σ=ρ0​ρh​​ is the density ratio

    #Drag Polar of an Aircraft

    Recall from aerodynamics that the maximum DL​ is achieved when CD0​​=kCL​2.

    The maximum DL​ is given by,

    DL​max​=(CD0​​+kCL​2CL​​)max​

    DL​max​=2CD0​​CD0​​/k​​

    💡
    DL​max​=2kCD0​​​1​

    #Forward Flight Stability

    Stability is the inherent quality of an aircraft to correct for conditions that may
    disturb its equilibrium and to return to or to continue on the original flight path.

    #Static Stability

    Static stability refers to the aircraft’s initial response when disturbed from a given pitch,
    yaw, or bank. Stability can be categorized into positive, neutral and negative stability.

    • Positive static stability — returns to orignial state of equilibrium after being disturbed
    • Neutral static stability — remains in a new condition after being disturbed
    • Negative static stability — continues away from orignial state of equilibrium

    #Dynamic Stability

    Dynamics stability refers to the aircraft’s response over time when disturbed from a given pitch, yaw, or bank. Similarly, it can be categorized into positive, neutral and negative.

    • Positive dynamic stability — motion decreases in amplitude
    • Neutral dynamic stability — ocsillations neither increase or decrease
    • Negative dynamic stability — motion increases in amplitude and becomes divergent

    #Neutral Point of an Aircraft

    Recall that for an airfoil, the aerodynamic center is the location in which moments about
    the airfoil is constant regardless of angle of attack. The aerodynamic center is typically located at the quarter-chord of the airfoil.

    Similarly, for an aircraft, the neutral point is the location in which moments about the aircraft is constant regardless of angle of attack. To achieve dynamic stability, the CG
    must be located forward of the neutral point such that any disturbance will result in an anticlockwise (nose-down) restoring moment.

    Distance between CG and NP is known as the static margin. It is usually expressed as a percentage of the mean aerodynamic chord. A desirable value is between 0.05 to 0.30, whereby the CG is ahead of the NP (positive static margin).
