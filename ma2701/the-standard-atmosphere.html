    MA2701 · FLIGHT PERFORMANCE

    The Standard Atmosphere

    Precise knowledge of atmospheric conditions on the day of flight is crucial to predict
    the performance of the aircraft on that day. The ability to predict aircraft performance
    accurately is essential not only to ensure a safe flight but also to reduce fuel consumption.

    #Definition of Altitude

    Absolute altitude ha​ is the distance as measured from the center of the earth.

    If r is the radius of the earth,

    💡
    ha​=r+hG​

    where hG​ is the geometric altitude

    Let g0​ be the gravitational acceleration at mean sea level.
    Then, the local gravitational acceleration g at a given absolute altitude ha​ is,

    💡
    g=g0​(ha​r​)2=g0​(r+hG​r​)2

    where g0​=9.80665 m/s2

    #Types of Altitudes

    #Indicated Altitude

    Description of altitude:

    • Aircraft’s altimeter reading
    • Measures the altitude of an aircraft above a chosen reference level
    • Reference level is decided by the reference pressure set on altimeter

    QFE reference pressure:

    • Local airfield pressure
    • Used during landing, takeoff, or circuit flying
    • Gives the height of the aircraft above the airfield

    QNE reference pressure:

    • Standard mean sea level pressure
    • Used in cruising phase of the flight
    • Gives the altitude above 101325 Pa level curve
    • Ensures vertical seperation and collision avoidance

      Pilots only need to set the reference pressure to the standard mean sea level pressure just once. If QNH setting is used instead, pilots would need to continuously reset the reference pressure to the local mean sea level pressure.

    QNH reference pressure:

    • Local mean sea level pressure
    • Used for flights below transition altitude (TA)
    • Gives the altitude above the local mean sea level

      Upon landing, QFE gives zero altitude and QNH gives the elevation of the airport.

    #Pressure, Temperature, and Density Altitudes

    Description of pressure altitude:

    • Indicated altitude when reference pressure is set to QNE
    • Altitude corresponding to the ambient pressure outside the aircraft (ISA table)

    Description of temperature altitude:

    • Altitude corresponding to the outside air temperature (ISA table)
    • Will be the same as pressure and density altitudes on a standard day

    Description of density altitude:

    • Altitude corresponding to the ambient air density (ISA table)
    • Lower density altitude, and thus higher air density → better performance
    • Higher density altitude, and thus lower air density → lower performance

    #Standard Atmosphere

    The International Standard Atmosphere (ISA) represents a standard day and is meant for use in the calculation and testing of engine propulsion characteristics and aircraft aerodynamic characteristics at various altitudes.

    #Hydrostatic Equation

    Consider a small stationary fluid element of air with sides of unit length and an infinitesimally small height dhG​.

    On the bottom face, pressure p is felt, exerting an upward force p×1×1.

    On the top face, pressure differs by dp, which exerts a downward force p+dp.

    Volume=1⋅1⋅dhG​=dhG​

    Mass=ρ⋅dhG​

    Weight=ρ⋅g⋅dhG​

    Balancing the pressure forces on the top and bottom,

    p=p+dp+ρg dhG​

    dp=−ρg dhG​

    In the above form, g is a variable that is dependent on hG​.

    To obtain the variation of pressure with altitude p=p(hG​), we make the assumption
    that g is a constant throughout the atmosphere, equal to its value at sea level g0​.

    💡
    dp=−ρg0​ dh

    where h is slightly different from hG​ to compensate that g is slightly different from g0​

    #Geopotential and Geometric Altitudes

    Let h be called the geopotential altitude.

    −ρg dhG​=−ρg0​ dh

    1=gg0​​⋅dhG​dh​

    dh=g0​g​ dhG​

    Substituting g=g0​(r+hG​r​)2 into the above equation,

    dh=(r+hG​)2r2​ dhG​

    Integrating the above equation,

    ∫0h​dh=∫0hG​​ (r+hG​)2r2​ dhG​

    h=r2[−r+hG​1​]0hG​​

    h=r2(−r+hG​1​+r1​)

    h=r2(r(r+hG​)−r+r+hG​​)

    💡
    h=r+hG​r​⋅hG​

    #Pressure and Density Variation

    The temperature distribution of the standard atmosphere consists of vertical lines,
    called the isothermal regions, and inclined lines, called the gradient regions.

    Dividing dp=−ρg0​ dh by p=ρRT,

    pdp​=−RTg0​​ dh

    Integrating the above equation gives us functions of h for the standard atmosphere.

    #Isothermal Region (11km<h<20km)

    Let T1​, P1​, and ρ1​ be the reference point at the base of the isothermal layer.

    ∫p1​p​pdp​=−RTg0​​∫h1​h​dh

    lnp1​p​=−RTg0​​(h−h1​)

    💡
    p1​p​=e−RTg0​​(h−h1​)

    From the equation p=ρRT,

    p1​p​=ρ1​RT1​ρRT​=ρ1​ρ​

    💡
    ρ1​ρ​=e−RTg0​​(h−h1​)​

    #Gradient Region (0km<h<11km)

    Let T0​, p0​, and ρ0​ be the reference point at the base of the gradient layer.

    Consider the gradient layers, where temperature variation is linear, and is given by,

    💡
    T=T0​+a(h−h0​)

    where a=−0.0065 K/m which is the lapse rate for the gradient layers

    Rearranging the above equation,

    a=h−h0​T−T0​​=dhdT​

    dh=a1​ dT

    Substituting the above and integrating,

    pdp​=−aRg0​​ dT

    ∫p0​p​pdp​=−aRg0​​∫h0​h​ T1​ dT

    lnp0​p​=−aRg0​​ lnT0​T​

    💡
    p0​p​=(T0​T​)−aRg0​​

    From the equation p=ρRT,

    p0​p​=ρ0​RT0​ρRT​=ρ0​T0​ρT​

    ρ0​T0​ρT​=(T0​T​)−aRg0​​

    💡
    ρ0​ρ​=(T0​T​)−aRg0​​−1

    #Flight Speed and Mach Number

    Speed of sound is given by,

    💡
    a=γRT​

    where γ=1.4 and R=287 J/kg⋅K

    Mach number is given by,

    💡
    M=aV​

    where V is the aircraft speed

    #Bernoulli’s Equation

    Total pressure is constant in a steady, inviscid, incompressible flow.

    💡
    p1​+21​ρV1​2=p2​+21​ρV2​2=ptot​

    A steady flow is one in which flow properties do not change with time. An inviscid flow
    is one in which fluid viscosity is negligible. An incompressible flow is one in which fluid
    density is constant.

    #Pitot-Static Tube

    A pitot-static tube measures airspeed of an aircraft, by measuring the difference between total and static pressures.

    💡
    V∞​=ρ∞​2(ptot​−p∞​)​​

    Airspeed is the relative speed of an aircraft with respect to the surrounding air.

    #Types of Airspeeds

    #Indicated Airspeed (IAS)

    Description of airspeed Vi​:

    • Airspeed as read from the airspeed indicator
    • Has inaccuracies — instrument error, air compressibility error, air density error

      The mean sea level density ρ0​ is used for calibrating the pitot-static probe.

    #Calibrated Airspeed (CAS)

    Description of airspeed Vc​:

    • Indicated airspeed corrected for instrument error and position/installation error
    • Correction value ΔV is obtained through flight tests
    • Given by Vc​=Vi​+ΔV

    #Equivalent Airspeed (EAS)

    Description of airspeed Ve​:

    • Calibrated airspeed corrected for the compressibility of air
    • Gives the airspeed at sea level, under ISA conditions

    #True Airspeed (TAS)

    Description of airspeed V∞​:

    • Equivalent airspeed corrected for air density error
    • Given by V∞​=Ve​ρ∞​ρ0​​​=Ve​σ1​​ where σ=ρ0​ρ∞​​

    #Ground Speed

    Ground speed Vg​ of an aircraft is given by,

    💡
    Vg​=V∞​−Vheadwind​
    💡
    Vg​=V∞​+Vtailwind​

    Headwind is helpful in takeoff and landing as it helps to reduce the ground run. Tailwind
    is helpful in cruise as it helps to increase the ground speed and thus, reduce flight time.

    #Wind Triangle

    A wind triangle is constructed using:

    • Air vector
    • Wind vector
    • Ground vector

    An aircraft’s heading is the direction in which its nose is pointing towards, and is known as true heading (measured clockwise from true north).

    💡
    True Heading=360−β

    Bearing of a point is the angle between true north and the direction to that point (measured clockwise from true north).

    💡
    True Course=α

    Heading is the direction the aircraft is pointing whereas course is the direction the aircraft is
    actually moving.
