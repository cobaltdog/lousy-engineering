    #Power Required Curve

    Recall from review of aerodynamics that thrust required (drag) is,

    TR​=21​ρV2S(CD0​​+kCL​2)

    TR​=21​ρV2SCD0​​+21​ρV2Sk(21​ρV2SW​)2

    TR​=AV2+V2B​

    where A=21​ρSCD0​​ and B=21​ρSkW2​

    The power required is given by,

    PR​=TR​V

    PR​=AV3+VB​

    To determine minimum power speed, differentiate with respect to V and equate to zero.

    dVdPR​​=3AV2−V2B​

    0=3AV2−V2B​

    V4=31​AB​

    Recall that minimum drag speed VDmin​​4=AB​.

    The minimum power speed is then,

    VPmin​​=431​(ρ2S2CD0​​4kW2​)​

    💡
    VPmin​​=ρS2W​3CD0​​k​​​

    The equation shows that minimum power
    speed is lower than minimum drag speed.

    For level flight, L=W. Rearranging the lift equation, V=ρSCL​2W​​.
    Lift-drag ratio is DL​=CD​CL​​. Rearranging the equation, D=CL​/CD​W​.

    Substituting the above equations into power required,

    PR​=CL​/CD​W​ρSCL​2W​​

    PR​=CD​ρS2​(CL​W​)3​

    💡
    PRmin​​=ρS2W3​​(CL​3/2CD​​)min​

    To achieve the minimum power, the term CL​3/2CD​​ needs to be a minimum.
    Note that the other terms are constants for a given aircraft and altitude.

    CD​=CD0​​+kCL​2

    CL​3/2CD​​=CL​3/2CD0​​​+kCL​​

    Differentiating with respect to CL​ and equating to zero,

    dCL​d​(CL​3/2CD​​)=−23​CD0​​CL​−25​+21​kCL​−21​

    0=−23​CD0​​CL​−25​+21​kCL​−21​

    0=−CL​5​3CD0​​​+CL​​k​

    💡
    3CD0​​=kCL​2

    Minimum power speed is when induced drag accounts for 75% of total drag.

    Lift coefficient for which power required is minimum is then,

    💡
    CLPmin​​​=k3CD0​​​​

    #Level Flight Speeds

    For a turboprop aircraft, performance calculations will be based on the power model.

    💡
    P0​P​=ρ0​ρ​n=σn

    Power remains relatively constant with respect to airspeed. Thus,
    from the power equation, as airspeed increases, thrust decreases.

    For a turbojet aircraft, performance calculations will be based on the thrust model.

    💡
    T0​T​=ρ0​ρ​m=σm

    Thrust remains relatively constant with respect to airspeed. Thus,
    from the power equation, as airspeed increases, power increases.

    Consider a turbojet aircraft at a given altitude. Since thrust is constant, the available thrust is plotted as a horizontal line.

    Intersections of thrust required and thrust available give the minimum and maximum
    level flight speeds, Vmin​ and Vmax​.

    Note that Vmin​ does not refer to Vstall​, which is dependent on the aerodynamic characteristics
    of the aircraft.

    Thrust available of a turbojet aircraft at a given altitude is,

    💡
    TA​=T0​⋅σm

    where m≤1 (dependent on the engine)

    At the intersections, thrust required is equal to thrust available.

    TA​=TR​

    TA​=AV2+V2B​

    0=AV4−TA​V2+B

    Solving the above quadratic equation,

    💡
    Vmin/max​=2ATA​±TA​2−4AB​​​

    Note that if the discriminant is zero, the speed obtained is the minimum drag speed.

    V=2ATA​​​ ⟶ V2=2AAV2+B/V2​ ⟶ V4=AB​ ⟶ VDmin​​=ρS2W​CD0​​k​​​

    Alternatively, the equation for minimum and maximum level flight speeds is,

    💡
    Vmin/max​=ρCD0​​(WTA​​)(SW​)±SW​(WTA​​)2−4CD0​​k​​​

    Note that if Vstall​>Vmin​, then Vstall​ is the minimum permissible level flight speed.

    #Level Flight Envelope (Jet)

    For a given weight and throttle setting:

    • Thrust required (drag) curve shifts
      to the right with increasing altitude
    • Thrust available shifts downwards with increasing altitude (decreasing density)

    Recall that as altitude increases, the density decreases. Thus, to generate the same lift,
    flight speed needs to increase.

    Thus, Vmin​ and Vmax​ changes with altitude. Plotting the minimum and maximum flight speeds against altitude, we obtain the flight envelope (area under curve).

    Note that at the absolute ceiling, Vmin​=Vmax​. Thus, there is no additional thrust available for
    the aircraft to climb further.

    The flight envelope is also dependent on other factors besides thrust.

    • Stall boundary
    • Buffet boundary
    • Maximum Mach number
    • Aeroelastic boundary qmax​

    #Aircraft Ceilings

    Description of absolute ceiling:

    • Maximum steady level flight altitude (zero climb capability)
    • Due to a decrease in engine power/thrust as altitude increases
    • No additional power/thrust available to climb further (climb rate is zero)
    • Engine produces just enough thrust to overcome minimum drag of aircraft

    Description of service ceiling:

    • Maximum steady level flight altitude with reserve climb capability
    • Amount of reserve for propeller aircraft: 100 ft/min or 0.5 m/s
    • Amount of reserve for jet aircraft: 500 ft/min or 2.5 m/s

    Typical service ceiling for commercial aircraft is 42000ft or 12.8km.
    Typical service ceiling for business jets is 51000ft or 15.5km.
    Service ceiling for the Concorde is 60000ft or 18.3km.

    At the absolute ceiling, TA​=TR​=D and Vmin​=Vmax​=VDmin​​.

    TA​=Dmin​

    T0​⋅σm=Dmin​

    σ=(T0​Dmin​​)m1​

    💡
    ρ=ρ0​(T0​Dmin​​)m1​

    The density altitude, corresponding to the density, is the absolute ceiling.

    Rearranging the lift-to-drag ratio,

    DL​max​=2kCD0​​​1​

    Dmin​=2LkCD0​​​

    💡
    Dmin​=2WkCD0​​​

    For level flight, lift is equal to weight.

    #Speed Instability at V<VDmin​​

    Consider a jet-powered aircraft flying at V1​, which is lower than VDmin​​.
    Assume that engine thrust, and thus throttle, is fixed during the flight.

    If there is a small decrease in speed:

    • Drag, and thus thrust required, increases
    • Thrust produced by the engine is insufficient
    • Aircraft begins to decelerate to a lower speed (deviates from initial condition)

    If there is a small increase in speed:

    • Drag, and thus thrust required, decreases
    • Thrust produced by the engine is more than required
    • Aircraft begins to accelerate to a higher speed (deviates from initial condition)

    Regardless of a decrease or increase in airspeed, the aircraft tends to deviate from
    its initial flight conditions. Thus, to maintain a constant airspeed, the pilot needs to continuously adjust the throttle.

    #Speed Stability at V>VDmin​​

    Consider a jet-powered aircraft flying at V1​, which is higher than VDmin​​.
    Assume that engine thrust, and thus throttle, is fixed during the flight.

    If there is a small decrease in speed:

    • Drag, and thus thrust required, decreases
    • Thrust produced by the engine more than required
    • Aircraft begins to accelerate back to its original speed

    If there is a small increase in speed:

    • Drag, and thus thrust required, increases
    • Thrust produced by the engine is insufficient
    • Aircraft begins to decelerate back to its original speed

    Regardless of a decrease or increase in airspeed, the aircraft tends to return to its initial flight conditions. Thus, sustained flight can be performed without a need for pilot input.
