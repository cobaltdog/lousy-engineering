    Phase Diagrams

    #Phase Diagram of Pure Substances

    A phase is a region in a material that has:

    • Same structure or atomic arrangement throughout
    • Same composition and properties throughout
    • Distinct boundary between other phases

    Phase diagrams:

    • Representation of the phases present in a material at various temperatures and pressures
    • Phases are separated by phase boundaries
    • Liquid and vapor phases exist along the vaporization line
    • Liquid and solid phases exist along the solidification line

    #Gibbs Phase Rule

    The Gibbs phase rule is defined as,

    💡
    P+F=C+2

    where P is the number of phases that coexist in a chosen system,
    and     C is the number of components (element) in the system,
    and     F is the degrees of freedom

    When F=2, the temperature and composition of a phase are independent. A metal
    or alloy can exist at a certain phase over a range of temperatures and compositions.

    When F=1, if temperature is fixed at a certain value, the composition of the liquid
    and solid phases are then automatically fixed. Conversely, if the composition is fixed
    at a certain value, the temperature of the alloy is automatically fixed.

    When F=0, the specific composition of the liquid and solid phases can only exist
    at one specific temperature. For example, the eutectic point occurs at a specific composition and temperature.

    #Solubility Limit

    For many alloys and at some specific temperature, there is a maximum concentration of solute atoms that may dissolve in the solvent to form a solid solution — solubility limit.

    The addition of solute in excess of this solubility limit results in the formation of
    another solid solution or compound that has a distinctly different composition.

    For example, in Cu-Zn alloys containing more
    than 30% zinc, a second phase forms because
    of the limited solubility of zinc in copper.

    #Cooling Curves

    Description of cooling curve:

    • Used to determine the phase transition temperatures for both pure metals and alloys
    • Curve is obtained by recording temperature of the material over time
    • Pure metals solidify at a constant temperature (thermal arrest)
    • Alloys solidify over a range of temperature (no thermal arrest)
    • Solid-state phase transformations are detected during cooling

    Recall that a degree of undercooling is required for the formation of solid nuclei,
    which appears on the curve as a drop below the solidification temperature.

    To construct a phase diagram:

    • Obtain the cooling curves of different compositions of an alloy
    • Points of change of slope of each curve are noted
    • Values of these points are plotted on a diagram

    #Binary Isomorphous Alloy System

    Description of isomorphous system:

    • Two elements that are completely soluble in each in both liquid and solid states
    • Only a single type of crystal structure exists for all compositions of the alloy

    Liquidus and solidus lines:

    • Liquidus line is the temperature above which the material is completely liquid
    • Solidus line is the temperature below which the material is completely solid
    • Region between the lines is where the exists in both liquid and solid phases
    • Composition of the phases in this region is determined by a tie line

    Consider the phase diagram of two elements
    that are completely soluble in each other.

    Weight fraction of the liquid phase
    and solid phase must be equal to 1.

    Xl​+Xs​=1

    #Nonequilibrium Solidification of Alloy

    As an alloy is cooled through the two-phase liquid and solid region:

    • Composition of liquid and solid phases readjust continuously by solid-state diffusion
    • Very slow cooling (equilibrium cooling) allow the readjustments to take place
    • Overall alloy composition remains unchanged during cooling
    • Rapid cooling (non-equilibrium cooling) do not allow time for readjustments
    • Results in a cored structure, caused by regions with different compositions

    Degree of displacement of the non-equilibrium solidus curve from the equilibrium one depends on the rate of cooling. The slower the cooling rate, the smaller the difference.

    #Binary Eutectic Alloy System

    Description of eutectic system:

    • Two elements that have limited solubility in each other
    • Three single-phase regions — α, β, and liquid phases

    Eutectic composition:

    • Eutectic point is the composition at which freezing temperature is the lowest
    • Temperature corresponding to this point is called the eutectic temperature
    • Compositions to the right of eutectic point are called hyper-eutectic
    • Compositions to the left of eutectic point are called hypo-eutectic
    • A eutectic alloy solidifies at a constant temperature (thermal arrest)
    • Proeutectic α — solid phase that forms above eutectic temperature

    #Microstructure in Eutectic Alloys

    Depending on composition, several different types of microstructures are possible.
    Consider solidification of eutectic composition that is cooled from a temperature
    within the liquid-phase region.

    Solidification process:

    • No changes occur until the eutectic temperature is reached
    • Redistribution of the α and β phases occur via atomic diffusion
    • Resulting microstructure consists of alternating layers of α and β

    #Formation of β Precipitates

    For precipitation hardening to occur:

    • Alloy must have a terminal solid solution
    • Solid solubility decreases with temperature

    Solidification process:

    • Above the solvus line, β phase dissolves completely within the α phase, and thus
      the microstructure consists of α grains
    • Below the solvus line, α solid solubility is exceeded, which results in the formation
      of small β particles within the α grains

    To form finely dispersed precipitate particles:

    • Solution heat treatment — completely dissolve all β phase to form single-phase α
    • Quenching — prevent the formation of any of the β precipitates
    • α phase solid solution is now supersaturated with β atoms
    • Ageing — supersaturated α is heated to a temperature below solvus temperature
    • β precipitate phase begins to form as finely dispersed particles within the α phase
    • Fine precipitates are more effective in hindering dislocation movement

    A supersaturated solid solution is in a high-energy state, which is relatively unstable as
    the alloy tends to seek a lower energy state by the spontaneous decomposition of the
    solid solution into metastable phases or equilibrium phases.

    Effect of ageing on strength:

    • As ageing time increases, alloy becomes stronger and harder, but less ductile
    • A maximum strength is eventually reached if ageing temperature is sufficiently high
    • Continued ageing results in coarser precipitates and the alloy becomes weaker
