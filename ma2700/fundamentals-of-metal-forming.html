    Fundamentals of Metal Forming

    #Metal Forming

    Metal forming processes are categorized into:

    • Bulk deformation
    • Sheetmetal working

    #Bulk Deformation Process

    Description of bulk deformation process:

    • Processes stress metal sufficiently to cause plastic flow into desired shape
    • Characterized by significant deformations and massive shape changes
    • Relatively small surface area-to-volume ratio of the workpiece
    • Starting forms — cylindrical billets and rectangular bars
    • Performed as cold, warm, hot working

    In a bulk deformation process, a tool (die) apply stresses that exceed the yield strength of
    the metal. The metal deforms and take the shape determined by the geometry of the die.

    Basic operations in bulk deformation:

    • Rolling — compressive deformation process, slab or plate is squeezed between rollers
    • Forging — workpiece is compressed and shaped between two opposing dies
    • Extrusion — metal is forced through a die opening and takes the shape of the opening
    • Drawing — diameter of a round bar is reduced by pulling it through a die opening

    #Sheetmetal Working

    Description of sheetmetal working:

    • Forming and cutting operations performed on metal sheets, strips, and coils
    • High surface area-to-volume ratio of the workpiece
    • Performed as cold working with a punch and die
    • Also called press working

    Basic operations in sheetmetal working:

    • Bending — straining a sheet metal along a straight axis
    • Deep drawing — stretching a sheet metal into a hollow or concave shape
    • Shearing — cuts the workpiece using a punch and die

    #Material Behavior

    In metal forming, the plastic region is of primary interest
    as the material is plastically and permanently deformed.

    For most metals at room temperature, strength of the metal
    increases as the metal is deformed due to strain hardening.

    In the plastic region, a metal’s behavior is,

    💡
    σt​=Kεtn​

    where n is the strain hardening exponent

    Taking logarithmic of the above equation,

    💡
    logσt​=logK+nlogεt​

    #Flow Stress

    Flow stress is defined as the instantaneous value of stress required
    to continue deforming the material — to keep the metal “flowing”.

    💡
    Yf​=Kεn

    In certain cases, analysis of a process
    is based on the average flow stress
    that occur during deformation.

    💡
    Yˉf​=1+nKεn​

    Given values of K and n for the work material, the final strain for a process
    can be computed.

    #Temperatures in Forming

    For any metal, the values of K and n depend on temperature.

    • Strength and strain hardening are both reduced at higher temperatures
    • Ductility is increased at higher temperatures (greater plastic deformation)
    • Low yield strength and high ductility result in lower forces and power during forming

    Temperature ranges in metal forming:

    • Cold working — at room temperature or below 0.3Tm​
    • Warm working — above 0.3Tm​ and below recrystallization temperature
    • Hot working — above recrystallization temperature, 0.5Tm​<T<0.75Tm​

    #Cold Working

    Advantages of cold working:

    • Higher strength and hardness due to strain hardening
    • Grain flow during deformation can result in desirable directional properties
    • Better surface finish and accuracy (closer tolerances can be achieved)
    • Near net shape or net shape process
    • Minimal or no machining required
    • No heating of the work required

    Disadvantages of cold working:

    • Higher forces and power required to deform the metal
    • Surfaces of the starting workpiece must be clean (free of dirt)
    • Strain hardening limits the amount of forming that can be done

    In some operations, the metal is annealed in order to allow further deformation.
    In other cases, the metal is simply not ductile enough to be cold-worked.

    #Warm Working

    Advantages of warm working:

    • Lower forces and power required (compared to cold working)
    • More intricate work geometries are possible (higher ductility)
    • Need for annealing may be reduced or eliminated

    #Hot Working

    Hot working provides the capability to produce substantial plastic deformation of the metal. At elevated temperatures, K is substantially lower than at room temperature,
    and n≈0. Additionally, ductility of the metal is increased.

    Advantages of hot working:

    • Shape of the workpiece can be significantly altered
    • Lower forces and power required to deform the metal
    • Metals that usually fracture in cold working can be hot-formed
    • Isotropic strength properties as grain structure is not directional
    • No strengthening of the part due to strain hardening (advantageous
      in cases when the part is to be further processed by cold forming)

    Disadvantages of hot working:

    • Lower dimensional accuracy
    • Higher total energy required (thermal energy to heat the workpiece)
    • Work surface oxidation (accelerated formation of scale at higher temperatures)
    • Poorer surface finish
    • Shorter tool life

    #Bulk Deformation Processes

    #Rolling

    Rolling process:

    • Thickness of the work is reduced by compressive forces exerted by opposing rolls
    • Rolls rotate to pull (by friction) and simultaneously squeeze the work
    • Cross-section of the work is reduced

    Most rolling processes are vert capital-intensive, requiring massive pieces of equipment,
    called rolling mills, to perform them. The high investment cost requires mills to be used
    for mass production. Most rolling is carried out by hot rolling.

    #Forging

    Forging process:

    • Work is compressed between two opposing dies
    • Impact or gradual pressure is applied to form the part
    • Used to establish basic form of parts that are then machined to final shape and size

    A forging hammer applies an impact load to deform the work metal.
    A forging press applies a gradual pressure to deform the work metal.

    Cold vs hot forging:

    • Hot forging — most common due to the significant deformation required
      and the need to reduce strength and increase ductility of the work metal
    • Cold forging — increased strength that results from strain hardening

    Types of forging dies:

    • Open-die forging
      • Work is compressed between two flat dies
      • Metal is allowed to flow laterally with minimal constraint
      • Reduces the height of the work and increases its diameter
    • Impression-die forging
      • Dies contain cavity or impression of the desired shape
      • Metal flow is constrained such that a flash (excess metal) is formed
      • Excess metal flows beyond die cavity and into the small gap between the dies
    • Flashless forging
      • Workpiece is completely enclosed within the die cavity during compression
      • No flash (excess metal) is formed

    #Extrusion

    Extrusion process:

    • Work is forced to flow through a die opening to produce a desired cross-section
    • Used to produce long parts with a uniform cross-section
    • Direct (forward) and indirect (reverse) extrusion

    Direct extrusion process:

    • Ram forces the metal to flow through a die opening
    • A small portion of the billet remains that cannot be forced through
    • Extra portion, called the butt, is separated from the product by cutting it
    • Starting billet is usually round in cross-section

    Indirect extrusion process:

    • Metal is forced to flow in a direction opposite to the motion of the ram
    • Difficulty in supporting the extruded product as it exits the die
    • Lower rigidity of the hollow ram

    Advantages of extrusion:

    • Variety of shapes possible, but cross-section must be uniform throughout its length
    • Grain structure and strength is enhanced in cold and warm extrusion
    • Close tolerances possible, especially in cold extrusion
    • Little to no waste of material

    #Wire and Bar Drawing

    Drawing process:

    • Cross-section of a bar, rod, or wire is reduced by pulling it through a die opening
    • Different from extrusion in that the work is pulled through die instead of pushed
    • Metal is squeezed down as it passes through the die opening
    • Bar drawing — large diameter bar and rod stock
    • Wire drawing — small diameter stock

    Drawing products:

    • Used to produce round cross-sections
    • Wire — electrical wire, wire stock for fences, etc.
    • Rod — nails, screws, rivets, and springs
    • Bar — metals bars for machining and forging

    Continuous wire drawing process:

    • Wire stock is drawn from drums and passed through a series of draw dies
    • Each drum provides the proper force to draw wire stock through the die
    • Each die provides a small reduction in cross-section
    • Annealing may be required between dies to relieve work hardening
