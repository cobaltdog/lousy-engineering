    Failure of Metals

    Failure may be defined as the inability of a material to:

    • Perform its intended function
    • Perform safely and reliably even after deterioration
    • Meet performance criteria although it may still be operational

    #Fracture of Metals

    Fractures are classified into:

    • Ductile fracture — occurs after extensive plastic deformation, slow crack propagation
    • Brittle fracture — rapid crack propagation results in sudden, catastrophic failure
    • Crack fracture — occurs at a stress level significantly below tensile strength

    #Ductile Fracture

    Stages of a ductile fracture:

    • Specimen forms a neck, and cavities form within the necked region
    • Cavities in the neck combine into a crack in the center of the specimen and
      propagate toward the surface in a direction perpendicular to applied stress
    • When crack nears the surface, direction of crack changes to 45° relative
      to the tensile axis which results in a formation of a cup-and-cone fracture

    In practice, ductile fractures are less frequent then brittle fractures.
    Main cause for their occurrence is overloading of the component.

    Overloading could occur as a result of:

    • Improper design — selection of materials (under-designing)
    • Improper fabrication — use of incorrect fabrication processes
    • Abuse — component is used at load levels above its design load

    #Brittle Fracture

    Characteristics of a brittle fracture:

    • No significant plastic deformation before fracture
    • Fracture occurs along cleavage planes (preferred slip planes)
    • Existence of defects (voids, etc.) in the metal the main cause of fracture

    Stages of a brittle fracture:

    • Plastic deformation concentrates dislocations along slip planes at obstacles
    • Shear stresses build up in places where the dislocations are blocked
    • As a result, microcracks are nucleated at these sites
    • Further stress propagates the microcracks to fracture

    #Impact Testing

    Besides the existence of defects, low operating temperatures or high loading rates
    may also cause the brittle fracture of some moderately ductile materials, which is
    a behavior called ductile-to-brittle transition.

    Charpy impact test:

    • Normally used to determine the ductile-to-brittle transition region
    • Measures the toughness of a material (energy absorbed before fracture)
    • Shows the relative effect of temperature on the impact energy of a material

    Note that FCC metals do not undergo ductile-to-brittle transition. Thus, they are suitable
    for low-temperature applications. For such applications, selected materials should have
    a transition temperature that is significantly lower than its operating temperature.

    #Fracture Toughness

    Impact tests provide useful comparative quantitative data but do not provide the
    proper data for design purposes for material sections containing cracks or flaws.

    Fracture of a metal starts at a place where stress concentration is the highest.
    Consider a plate under tension that contains an edge crack or an internal crack.
    Stress at the tip of the sharp crack is highest, and decreases away from the crack.

    Fracture toughness of a material is,

    💡
    KIC​=Yσf​πa​

    where KIC​ is the critical stress-intensity factor, σf​ is the fracture stress,
    and     a is crack length for an edge crack (or 21​ of an internal crack length)

    #Example

    From properties table,

    KIC​=26.4 MPam​

    Largest edge crack size is,

    KIC​=Yσf​πa​

    26.4=1(207)πa​

    a=0.0051775 m=5.18 mm

    For an internal crack,

    2a=2(5.1775)=10.355 mm

    Note that for an internal crack, it’s characteristic length is half its total length.

    #Fatigue of Metals

    Metal parts subjected to repetitive or cyclic stresses will fail due to fatigue loading
    at a much lower stress than that which the part can withstand under application
    of a single static stress.

    Fatigue failure:

    • Usually originates at a point of stress concentration
    • Crack nucleates at region of stress concentration and
      propagates as the part is subjected to cyclic loading
    • Fracture occurs when remaining cross-section area
      is too small to withstand the applied load

    Low cycle fatigue (thousands of cycles):

    • Low frequency cyclic load
    • Occurs above yield stress
    • Coarse beach markings
    • Large final fracture area

    High cycle fatigue (millions of cycles):

    • High frequency cyclic load
    • Occurs below yield stress
    • Fine beach markings
    • Small final fracture area

    #Cyclic Stresses

    Applied stresses may be:

    • Axial (tension-compression)
    • Flexural (bending)
    • Torsional (twisting)

    The following are parameters used to characterize a fluctuating stress cycle.

    Mean stress is,

    💡
    σm​=2σmax​+σmin​​

    Range of stress is,

    💡
    σr​=σmax​−σmin​

    Stress amplitude is,

    💡
    σa​=2σr​​=2σmax​−σmin​​

    Stress ratio is,

    💡
    R=σmax​σmin​​

    #Fatigue Strength and Life

    Factors affecting fatigue strength:

    • Stress concentrations — minimized by careful design
    • Surface roughness — rough surfaces create stress raisers
    • Surface condition — most fatigue failures originate at the metal surface,
      employ surface-hardening treatments such as carburizing and nitriding
    • Environment — corrosive environment accelerates crack propagation

    Fatigue life of a material is determined using a rotating-beam test, which subjects
    the specimen to alternating compression and tension stresses of equal magnitude
    while being rotated.

    The stress S to cause failure is plotted against logarithm of the number of cycles N
    at which failure occurs. For certain materials, the S-N levels off, with no decrease
    in fatigue strength as the number of cycles is increased — fatigue limit.

    When fracture area is observed under a microscope:

    • Striations are visible on the surface, which represent the number of cycles
    • Spacing between striations indicates the distance crack moves in 1 cycle
    • Striation width increases with increasing stress range

    #Fatigue Crack Propagation

    Preexisting flaws or cracks reduce or may eliminate the crack initiation part
    of the fatigue life of a component. Thus, fatigue life is considerably shorter
    than a component without flaws.

    • When crack length is small, fatigue crack growth rate dNda​ is also small
    • Crack growth rate increases with increasing crack length
    • Increase in cyclic stress increases the crack growth rate

    Fatigue crack growth rate is dependent on applied cyclic stress and crack length.

    💡
    dNda​=AΔKm

    where A and m are material constants, and ΔK=Yσr​πa​

    Usually, the data is plotted as a logarithmic graph.

    💡
    logdNda​=mlogΔK+logA

    Note that the above equation produces a straight line with a slope of m.

    Fatigue life of a material can be obtained by,

    dNda​=A(Yσr​πa​)m

    dNda​=A(Ymσrm​πm/2am/2)

    ∫ai​af​​da=A(Ymσrm​πm/2am/2)∫0Nf​​dN

    A(Ymσrm​πm/2)1​∫ai​af​​am/21​ da=∫0Nf​​dN

    💡
    Nf​=AYmσrm​πm/2[−(m/2)+1]af​−(m/2)+1−a0​−(m/2)+1​

    Assuming Y is independent of crack length, and m=2.

    Application of fatigue life equation:

    • Scenario 1 — Initial and final crack lengths are known, determine fatigue life
    • Scenario 2 — Initial and final crack lengths are known, determine inspection interval
    • Scenario 3 — Initial crack length is known, obtain stress limit for required fatigue life

    From the given fatigue crack growth rate,

    m=3

    as a compression load closes the crack.

    a0​=1 mm=0.001 m

    KIC​=Yσf​πaf​​

    af​=π1​(Yσf​KIC​​)2

    #Creep of Metals

    When a metal or an alloy is under a constant load or stress:

    • May undergo progressive plastic deformation over a period of time
    • Creep — time-dependent strain; plastic strain increases with time
    • Creep is the limiting factor to how high operating temperature can be
    • Occurs when T>0.3 - 0.4Tm​ for metals, and T>0.4 - 0.5Tm​ for ceramics

    Primary creep:

    • Creep rate decreases with time
    • Metal strain-hardens to support the applied load
    • Creep rate decreases as further strain hardening becomes more difficult

    Secondary creep:

    • Creep rate is constant
    • Recovery processes counteract the strain hardening
    • Metal continues to elongate at a steady-state rate
    • Creep resistance at this stage is the highest

    Tertiary creep:

    • Creep rate increases with time
    • Necking of the specimen reduces cross-section area
    • Applied load is constant, stress increases and creep is accelerated

    At elevated temperatures, dislocations are able to bypass obstacles (solute atoms,
    precipitates, other dislocations) due to enhanced atomic diffusion. Thus, creep
    failure is of importance in high-temperature applications (turbine blades).

    Design considerations to resist creep:

    • Select a material with high-melting temperature
    • Maximize obstacles to dislocation movement through alloying
    • Select a material with the highest lattice resistance (strong bonding)
    • Large grain size to reduce grain boundary diffusion (single crystal if possible)
    • Precipitates at grain boundaries to impede grain boundary sliding at high temperatures

    #Larsen-Miller Parameter

    Creep-stress rupture data is plotted as a logarithmic graph.

    💡
    P(L.M.)=T[logtr​+C]

    where tr​ is stress-rupture time in hours, T is temperature in Kelvins, and C=20
