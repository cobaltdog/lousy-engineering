    Solidification and Imperfections

    #Solidification of Metals

    Solidification of a metal or alloy can be divided into:

    • Formation of stable nuclei in the liquid material (nucleation)
    • Growth of nuclei into crystals and the formation of a grain structure
    • Thermal gradients define the shape of each grain (direction of growth)

    #Homogeneous Nucleation

    Description of nucleation:

    • Metal itself provides the atoms needed to form nuclei
    • Usually requires a considerable amount of undercooling

      When a pure liquid metal is cooled below its melting temperature to a sufficient
      degree, many homogeneous nuclei are created by slow-moving atoms bonding
      together, which may then grow into crystals.

    During solidification, an interface is formed between the liquid and solid phases.
    Additionally, a change in volume occurs as the liquid material changes into solid.

    Two types of energy are involved during solidification:

    • Volume free energy ΔGv​ (in per unit volume)
    • Surface free energy ΔGs​ (in per unit surface area)

      ΔGv​ is the energy difference between liquid and solid phases, and
      is the driving force for solidification.       ΔGs​ is the energy required to
      form the new solid surfaces of the solidified particles.

    Total change in free energy during solidification is,

    ΔGtot​=Vol×ΔGv​+Surf.Area×ΔGs​

    💡
    ΔGtot​=34​πr3ΔGv​+4πr2γ

    where γ is specific surface free energy

    For a nucleus to be stable so that it can grow into a crystal, it must reach a critical size.

    • Embryo — cluster of atoms bonded together that is less than critical size
    • Nucleus — cluster of atoms that is larger than critical size

    Critical radius r∗ of a solid particle is when
    ΔGtot​ is maximum, which is obtained by differentiating ΔGtot​ with respect to r.

    drd(ΔGtot​)​=0

    drd​(34​πr3ΔGv​+4πr2γ)=0

    312​πr∗2ΔGv​+8πr∗γ=0

    💡
    r∗=−ΔGv​2γ​

    Critical radius r∗ and undercooling:

    • Greater the degree of undercooling ΔT below melting temperature
      of the metal, the greater the change in volume free energy ΔGv​
    • Surface free energy ΔGs​ does not change significantly
    • Critical radius decreases with increase in undercooling

    Relation between critical radius and degree of undercooling.

    💡
    r∗=ΔHf​ΔT2γTm​​

    where ΔHf​ is latent heat of fusion, and ΔT is amount of undercooling below Tm​

    #Heterogeneous Nucleation

    Description of nucleation:

    • Occurs in a liquid on the surfaces of a structural material (insoluble impurities, etc.)
    • Structural material lowers the critical free energy required to form a stable nucleus
    • Smaller amount of undercooling required

    For heterogeneous nucleation to take place:

    • Solid nucleating agent (impurity solid) must be wetted by the liquid metal
    • Liquid metal creates a low contact angle θ between solid metal and agent
    • Large radius of curvature reduces surface energy to form a stable nucleus
    • Total change in free energy required will be lower
    • Critical volume (size) of nucleus will decrease

    Number of nucleation sites available will affect the grain structure of the solid metal.
    If there are relatively few sites, a coarse grain structure will be produced. If there are
    many sites, a fine grain structure will be produced, which results in higher strength.

    #Growth Mechanisms

    The growth of the solid nuclei depends on how heat is removed from the liquid material.

    During solidification, two types of heat must be removed:

    • Specific heat of the liquid material
    • Latent heat of fusion

    Specific heat is removed first, either by radiation into the surroundings, or by conduction
    into the surrounding mold, until the liquid cools to its freezing temperature. Latent heat
    of fusion is removed from the solid-liquid interface before solidification is completed.

    #Planar Growth — Heterogeneous

    Planar growth of crystals:

    • Movement of a smooth solid-liquid interface into the liquid
    • Occurs for a liquid containing nucleating agents (heterogenous nucleation)
    • Less undercooling is required and thus, temperature of the liquid ahead of
      solidification front (solid-liquid interface) is above the freezing temperature

    Description of growth mechanism:

    • Any small protuberance begins to grow ahead of interface but stops
      when it becomes surrounded by liquid above freezing temperature
    • Growth of protuberance stops until remainder of interface catches up
    • Planar interface is maintained

    Note that any small protuberance is unable to grow further as the liquid surrounding
    it is above the freezing temperature and thus, does not change into the solid phase.
    At the interface, latent heat of fusion is removed by conduction in the solid.

    #Dendritic Growth — Homogeneous

    Dendritic growth of crystals:

    • Liquid does not contain nucleating agents (homogeneous nucleation)
    • Significant undercooling is required for a phase change to occur

    Description of growth mechanism:

    • Small, solid protuberance (dendrite), forms at the solid-liquid interface
    • Dendrite encouraged to grow since the liquid ahead of interface is undercooled
    • Latent heat of fusion is conducted into the undercooled liquid as dendrite grows
    • Temperature of undercooled liquid increases gradually to its freezing temperature
    • Dendrite growth continues until the liquid reaches its freezing temperature
    • Any remaining liquid then solidifies by planar growth

    The difference between planar and dendritic growth arises because of the different
    heat sinks for latent heat of fusion. The container or mold must absorb the heat in
    planar growth, but the undercooled liquid absorbs the heat in dendritic growth.

    Inter-dendritic shrinkage:

    • Shrinkage can occur between dendrite arms
    • Small secondary dendrite arm spacing results in
      smaller, more evenly distributed shrinkage porosity
    • Short primary dendrite arms helps to minimize shrinkage

    #Types of Grains

    Two major types of grain structures:

    • Equiaxed grains
    • Columnar grains

    Equiaxed grains:

    • Occurs when crystals can grow about equally in all directions
    • Commonly found adjacent to a cold mold wall (significant undercooling)
    • Undercooling create a high concentration of nuclei during solidification

    Columnar grains:

    • Long, thin, coarse grains
    • Grow predominantly in one direction
    • Formed at sites of slow cooling and steep temperature gradient
    • Typically grow perpendicularly to mold wall due to large thermal gradients

    Almost all engineering materials are composed of many crystals (polycrystalline).
    However, high-temperature creep-resistant gas turbine blades are single crystal.

    Single-crystal turbine blades are more creep resistant as these components typically
    fail along transverse grain boundaries, especially if they are polycrystalline.

    #Metallic Solid Solutions

    Most engineering metals are combined with other metals or non-metals to
    create an alloy that has increased strength, higher corrosion resistance, etc.

    The simplest type of alloy is a solid solution, which is a solid that consists
    of two or more elements atomically dispersed in a single-phase structure.

    #Substitutional Solid Solution

    Substitutional solid solution:

    • Solute atoms substitute for parent solvent atoms in a crystal lattice
    • Crystal structure of the parent solvent (element) is unchanged
    • Lattice may be distorted by the presence of solute atoms
    • Solute percentage varies from a fraction to 100%

    If the atomic diameters of the two elements differ, there will be a distortion of the crystal lattice. However, there is a limit in the difference in atomic diameters and still maintain
    a solid solution with the same kind of crystal structure.

    #Interstitial Solid Solution

    Interstitial solid solution:

    • Solute atoms fit into the spaces between the parent solvent atoms
    • Parent solvent atoms are much larger than the solute atoms
    • Solute atoms may cause local distortion in the lattice

    #Crystalline Imperfections

    Crystals contain various types of imperfections and defects that affect their mechanical properties, which in turn affect many important engineering properties of materials.

    Crystal imperfections are classified according to geometry and shape.

    • Zero-dimensional (point defects)
    • 1-dimensional (line defects/dislocations)
    • 2-dimensional (grain boundaries, twin boundaries)
    • 3-dimensional (precipitates, cracks, inclusions)

    #Zero-Dimensional Defects

    Vacancy:

    • Site at which an atom is missing
    • Formed during solidification as a result of local disturbances during crystal growth,
      or created by atomic rearrangements in an existing crystal due to atomic mobility
    • Additional vacancies can be introduced by plastic deformation, rapid cooling
      from higher temperatures, or by bombardment with energetic particles
    • Movement of vacancies can result in clustering of vacancies

    Self-interstitial:

    • Atom in a crystal occupies an interstitial site between surrounding atoms
    • Does not occur naturally because of the structural distortion they cause
    • Can be introduced into a structure by irradiation

    #Line Defects / Dislocations

    Dislocations cause lattice distortion centered around a line, and are created during:

    • Solidification of crystalline solids
    • Permanent (plastic) deformation
    • Vacancy condensation

    Burgers vector b:

    • Represent the magnitude and direction of lattice distortion around a dislocation
    • Determined by comparing the atom-to-atom paths taken in a perfect crystal to
      that taken in a crystal with dislocation
    • Vector required to complete the path (loop) is the Burgers vector

    #Edge Dislocations

    Description of edge dislocation:

    • Created by insertion of an extra half plane of atoms
    • Inverted “tee” ⊥ indicates a positive edge dislocation
    • Upright “tee” indicates a negative edge dislocation
    • Lowers the energy required for deformation to occur
    • Burgers vector is perpendicular to dislocation line

    #Screw Dislocations

    Description of screw dislocation:

    • Created when applying shear stresses to regions of a perfect crystal
    • Distorted lattice is in the form of a spiral ramp of distorted atoms
    • Burgers vector is parallel to the dislocation line

    #Mixed Dislocations

    Most dislocations found in crystals are the mixed type.

    #2-Dimensional Defects

    Grain boundaries:

    • Imperfections that separate grains of different orientation
    • Region of atomic mismatch between adjacent grains
    • Have lower atomic packing due to atomic mismatch
    • Restrict plastic flow by preventing dislocation movement

    Twin boundaries:

    • Region in which a mirror image of the structure exists across a boundary
    • Formed when a material is permanently deformed, or during recrystallization

    #3-Dimensional Defects

    Precipitation strengthening:

    • Tiny particles of a hard and very strong phase are introduced into a softer
      and ductile matrix of a different phase through heat treatment processes
    • Precipitate particles acts as obstacles to dislocation movements
    • Strength of the alloy is increased

    #Metallography

    Optical metallography techniques are used to study
    the features and internal makeup of materials at the
    micrometer level.

    Qualitative/quantitative information such as grain size, grain boundary, existence of various phases, internal damage, and some defects may be extracted.

    Preparation process:

    • Surface grinding — remove scratches and thin plastically deformed layers from surface
    • Polishing — remove fine scratches formed during grinding stages
    • Polished surface is then exposed to chemical etching agents
    • Grain boundary regions will be etched away by the agents
    • Etchant produces tiny grooves along grain boundaries
    • Grooves do not reflect light as intensely and appear as dark lines

    The atoms at the grain boundaries will be attacked by the etchant at a much more
    rapid rate than those atoms inside the grains as they possess a higher state of
    energy due to less efficient packing at the grain boundaries.
