    Mechanical Properties of Metals

    #Stress and Strain in Metals

    Types of deformations:

    • Elastic — metal recovers and returns to its original dimensions when force is removed
    • Plastic — irreversible deformation or change in shape when force is removed
    • Dislocation density — total length of dislocation line per cm3 of material

    During plastic deformation, the bonds between atoms are broken, and atoms are
    permanently displaced from their original positions and take up new positions.
    The ability to be extensively plastically deformed without fracture is a useful
    engineering property, especially in stamping of metals.

    #Engineering Stress and Strain

    Engineering (normal) stress is defined as,

    💡
    σ=A0​F​

    where F is the average tensile force applied,
    and     A0​ is the original cross-section area

    Engineering (normal) strain is defined as,

    💡
    ε=L0​L−L0​​=L0​ΔL​

    where L0​ is the original length of sample

    Modulus of elasticity, or Young’s modulus is,

    💡
    E=εσ​

    #Poisson’s Ratio

    A longitudinal elastic deformation of a metal produces
    an accompanying lateral dimensional change. A tensile stress σz​ produces a tensile normal strain +εz​, and
    lateral compressive strains −εx​ and −εy​.

    Poisson’s ratio is defined as,

    💡
    v=−εz​εx​​=−εz​εy​​

    #Shear Stress and Strain

    Shear stress is defined as,

    💡
    τ=AS​=Gγ

    where G is the modulus of rigidity

    Shear strain is defined as,

    💡
    γ=tanθ=ha​

    #Stress-Strain Diagram

    A tensile test is used to evaluate the strength and stiffness of metals and alloys.

    Description of tensile test:

    • Metal sample is pulled to failure in a relatively short time at a constant rate
    • Force (load) on the specimen being tested is measured by a load cell
    • Strain is obtained from extensometer attached to specimen

    #Engineering Stress-Strain Curve

    Mechanical properties that can be obtained are:

    • Modulus of elasticity E — measure of stiffness or rigidity (slope of elastic region)
    • Yield strength σy​ — point at which metal shows significant plastic deformation
    • Ultimate tensile strength σu​ — maximum strength reached in stress-strain curve
    • % elongation at fracture — measure of ductility of a metal
    • % reduction in area at fracture — alternative measure of ductility
    • Toughness — area under the full stress-strain curve

    Necking is a decrease in cross-section area after UTS is reached. The curve will begin to
    dip due to a reduction in cross-section area of the specimen producing large stresses at
    the same load. However, since engineering stress is determined based on the original
    cross-section area, the stress seems to decrease.

    #True Stress and True Strain

    The cross-section area of a specimen changes continuously during a tensile test.
    Thus, the engineering stress calculated is not precise. When necking occurs, the
    true stress is higher than the engineering stress.

    True stress is defined as,

    💡
    σt​=Ai​F​

    where Ai​ is the instantaneous minimum cross-section area

    True strain is defined as,

    💡
    εt​=∫L0​Li​​LdL​=lnL0​Li​​

    where Li​ is the instantaneous extended length during the test

    If no volume change occurs during deformation, then L0​A0​=Li​Ai​.

    💡
    εt​=lnAi​A0​​

    #Macroscopic Elastic Strain

    Description of strain:

    • Manifest as small changes in interatomic spacing and stretching of interatomic bonds
    • Magnitude of E is a measure of resistance to separation of adjacent atoms
    • Modulus is proportional to the slope of interatomic force-separation curve

    💡
    E∝ΔrΔF​

    where r is the separation distance between atoms

    #Hardness and Hardness Testing

    Hardness is a measure of the resistance of a metal to permanent (plastic) deformation.

    Description of hardness test:

    • Measured by forcing an indenter into a metal’s surface
    • Indenter is made of a material harder than the metal being tested
    • Known load is applied slowly by pressing indenter 90° into the metal surface
    • After the indentation has been made, indenter is withdrawn from the surface
    • Empirical hardness number is determined based on depth or width of indentation

    #Plastic Deformation in Single Crystals

    Slip bands, which appear as step markings on the surface, are caused by the slipping or
    shear deformation of metal atoms on specific crystallographic planes called slip planes.

    #Slip Mechanism

    • Occurs in densely or closed packed planes
    • Contact angle between atoms is smaller
    • Lower energy required for atoms to slide
    • Lower shear stress is then required for slip to occur

    If slip on closed packed planes is restricted due to local high stresses, then planes of lower atomic packing will slip instead.

    For large metal crystals to deform at low shear stresses, a high density of dislocations
    must be present. A dislocation reduces the amount of shear stress required for slip,
    since only a small group of atoms slips over each other at any instant.

    #Slip Systems

    A combination of a slip plane and a slip direction is called a slip system.

    • FCC crystal structure — slip occurs on (111) planes and [11ˉ0] directions
    • Crystal has four different types of (111)-type slip planes
    • Each slip plane has three [11ˉ0]-type slip directions
    • 4 slip planes × 3 slip directions = 12 slip systems

    #Critical Resolved Shear Stress

    Slip in a pure-metal single crystal occurs when
    shear stress on a slip plane in the slip direction
    reaches the critical resolved shear stress τcr​.

    Stress required to cause slip depends on:

    • Crystal structure of the metal
    • Atomic bonding characteristics
    • Temperature at which it is deformed
    • Orientation of active slip planes w.r.t shear stresses

    #Schmid’s Law

    Consider a tensile stress σ acting on a metal cylinder. Let A0​ be the area normal
    to the axial force F. Let A1​ be the area of the slip plane or shear area on which
    the resolved shear force Fr​ is acting.

    We define λ as the angle between force F and the slip direction.
    We define ϕ as the angle between force F and the normal of slip plane area A1​.

    Resolved shear stress is,

    τr​=A1​Fr​​

    τr​=A0​/cosϕFcosλ​

    τr​=A0​F​cosλcosϕ

    💡
    τr​=σ⋅cosλcosϕ

    where the group of cosine terms is called the Schimd’s factor

    #Example

    Angle between F and slip plane,

    cosϕ=3​aa​

    cosϕ=3​1​

    ϕ=54.74°

    Angle between F and slip direction,

    λ=45°

    Resolved shear stress of system,

    τr​=σ⋅cosλcosϕ

    τr​=13.7(cos45°cos54.74°)

    τr​=5.6 MPa
