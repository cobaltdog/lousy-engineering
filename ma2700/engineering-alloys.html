    Engineering Alloys

    #Plain-Carbon Steel

    Iron-carbon alloys contain a very small amount (about 0.03%) to about 1.2% carbon.

    Solid phases in the phase diagram:

    • α iron (ferrite) — interstitial solid solution of carbon in BCC iron crystal structure
    • δ ferrite — interstitial solid solution of carbon in BCC iron at elevated temperatures
    • γ iron (austenite) — interstitial solid solution of carbon in FCC iron crystal structure
    • Cementite (iron carbide) — intermetallic compound Fe3​C that is hard and brittle
    • Pearlite — alternating layers of α iron and cementite (lamellar structure)

    At the eutectic reaction point, with percent carbon of 4.3wt%, a single liquid phase
    forms into two solid phases,     γ iron and cementite. This reaction occurs at 1148°C.

    At the eutectoid reaction point, with percent carbon of 0.77wt%, a single solid phase
    of     γ iron forms into two solid phases, α iron and cementite. This reaction occurs at
    727°C and takes place completely in the solid state.

    #Slow Cooling of Plain-Carbon Steel

    #Eutectoid Steel

    If a sample of a 0.77% eutectoid plain-carbon steel is heated to about 750°C
    and held for a sufficient time, its structure will become homogenous austenite.
    This process is known as austenitizing.

    Microstructure of steel:

    • Slight above eutectoid temperature, remains as γ iron (austenite)
    • Slight below eutectoid temperature, lamellar structure of pearlite
    • At room temperature, no change in pearlite structure

    #Hypoeutectoid Steel

    If a sample of a 0.4% hypoeutectoid plain-carbon steel is heated to about 900°C for
    a sufficient time, its structure will become homogeneous austenite. Further cooling
    to below eutectoid temperature results in pro-eutectoid α iron and pearlite.

    Microstructure of steel:

    • At temperature b, pro-eutectoid ferrite will form at austenite grain boundaries
    • Slightly above eutectoid temperature, more pro-eutectoid ferrite will form
    • Slightly below eutectoid temperature, austenite will transform into pearlite
    • Results in pearlite with pro-eutectoid ferrite at the grain boundaries

    The α iron (ferrite) is called eutectoid ferrite to distinguish it
    from the pro-eutectoid ferrite that forms first above     727°C.

    #Hypereutectoid Steel

    If a sample of a 1.2% hypereutectoid steel is heated to about 950°C and held for a sufficient time, its structure will become homogeneous austenite. Further cooling
    to below eutectoid temperature results in pro-eutectoid cementite and pearlite.

    Microstructure of steel:

    • At temperature b, pro-eutectoid cementite will form at austenite grain boundaries
    • Slightly above eutectoid temperature, more pro-eutectoid cementite will form
    • Slightly below eutectoid temperature, austenite will transform into pearlite
    • Results in pearlite with pro-eutectoid cementite at grain boundaries

    The cementite formed by the eutectoid reaction, where austenite transforms into
    pearlite, is called eutectoid cementite to distinguish it from the pro-eutectoid
    cementite that forms first above     727°C.

    #Isothermal Transformation Diagram

    If a plain-carbon steel in the austenite phase is rapidly cooled (quenched) to
    room temperature, its structure will change from austenite to martensite. By
    controlling the cooling rate, different microstructures can be obtained.

    Isothermal transformation experiment:

    • Samples are first austenitized in a furnace above eutectoid temperature
    • Quenched in a liquid salt bath at desired temperature below eutectoid temperature
    • After various time intervals, samples are removed from the salt bath and quenched
      in water to room temperature, which produces different microstructure based on
      the holding time in the salt bath

    The S-shaped curve next to the temperature axis indicates the beginning (start) of the isothermal transformation process, and the second S-shaped curve indicates the end
    of the transformation process.

    If a eutectoid steel is held in the salt bath for a duration that falls between the two
    curves, and then quenched to room temperature, the resulting microstructure will
    be a combination of pearlite/bainite with martensite.

    #Continuous Cooling Transformation Diagram

    In industrial heat-treating processes, steel is not isothermally transformed but is continuously cooled from austenitic temperature to room temperature, which
    results in a complex final microstructure.

    Continuous cooling diagram:

    • Start and finish lines are shifted to longer times and slightly lower temperatures
    • No transformation lines below 450°C for austenite-to-bainite transformation

    Cooling curve C starts with the formation of pearlite, but there is insufficient time
    to complete the austenite-to-pearlite transformation. The remaining austenite will transform to martensite at lower temperatures. The resulting microstructure is a
    mixture of pearlite and martensite.

    Cooling at a rate faster than the critical cooling rate
    will produce a fully hardened martensitic structure.

    #Annealing and Normalizing

    Full annealing:

    • Eutectoid and hypoeutectoid steels heated to 40°C above austenite-ferrite boundary
    • Held at this elevated temperature and then slowly cooled to room temperature
    • Microstructure after full annealing consists of pro-eutectoid ferrite and pearlite
    • For hypereutectoid steel, heated to 40°C above eutectoid temperature

    Process annealing (stress-relief):

    • Softens cold-worked steels by relieving internal stresses from cold working
    • Usually applied to hypoeutectoid steel with less than 0.3% carbon content
    • Heated to a temperature below eutectoid temperature, between 550°C and 650°C

    Normalizing:

    • Steel is heated to the austenitic region and then cooled in still air
    • Increase the strength of the steel, as compared to annealed steel
    • Reduce compositional segregation in castings/forgings (more uniform grain structure)
    • More economical than full annealing since no furnace required to control cooling rate

    #Tempering

    Austenite to martensite transformation (γ iron to α′ iron):

    • Important strengthening mechanism to increase the strength of steels
    • High dislocation density and lattice distortion by interstitial carbon atoms
    • Transformation is so rapid such that carbon atoms are unable to diffuse out
    • BCC structure (α iron) is distorted into a BCT (body-centered tetragonal) structure

    Hardness and strength of martensite increases with carbon content.

    • Lath martensite, <0.6% carbon, laths of different orientations
    • Plate martensite, >1.0% carbon, fine structure of dislocations and lattice twins
    • Between 0.6% and 1.0% carbon content, mixed structure of lath and plate martensite

    Tempering process:

    • Martensite is metastable and decomposes upon reheating
    • Martensitic steel is heated to below eutectoid temperature to soften it
    • Carbon atoms diffuse out to form iron carbide precipitates, decrease in hardness
    • Resulting structure is a ferrite matrix with iron carbide precipitates (more ductile)

    Tempering temperature affects the hardness of martensitic steels. This gradual decrease
    in hardness of martensite is due to the diffusion of carbon atoms from their stressed interstitial lattice sites to form iron carbide precipitates.

    #Low-Alloy Steels

    Limitations of plain-carbon steels:

    • Cannot be strengthened beyond 690 MPa without losing ductility and impact strength
    • Not deep-hardenable (thick sections cannot be produced with uniform martensite)
    • Rapid quenching to achieve martensite may lead to distortion and cracking
    • Poor impact resistance at low temperatures
    • Low corrosion and oxidation resistance

    Note that all plain-carbon steels contain 0.3%-0.95% manganese to enhance strength.
    If strength and other engineering requirements are not too severe, plain-carbon steels
    can still be used, since they are relatively low costs.

    Classification of alloy steels:

    • First two digits — principal alloying element
    • Last two digits — percent carbon content
    • AISI 1340 steel — 1.75% manganese and 0.4% carbon

    Effect of alloying elements on eutectoid temperature:

    • Manganese and nickel both lower the eutectoid temperature and acts as austenite-stabilizing elements such that austenitic structure will form at lower temperatures
    • Tungsten, molybdenum, and titanium increase the eutectoid temperature and acts as ferrite-stabilizing elements such that ferritic structure will form at higher temperatures

    #Hardenability

    Description of hardenability:

    • Ability of a steel to form martensite upon quenching
    • Relates to how easily martensite can be formed in a thick section
    • Determines the depth and distribution of hardness induced by quenching

    Hardenability of a steel depends on:

    • Composition of the steel
    • Austenitic grain size
    • Structure of steel before quenching

    Jominy hardenability test:

    • Cylindrical bar is austenitized and one end of of the specimen is quenched
    • Hardness of the specimen is measured from the quenched to non-quenched end
    • Hardness varies along the length due to different cooling rates at various distances
    • Different cooling rates result in different microstructures with different hardness

    With a more hardenable alloy steel, martensite can be obtained at slower cooling rates. Then, it is possible obtain martensite structure in thick sections at slow cooling rates.
    Plain-carbon steels have low hardenability due to short quenching times required.

    #Aluminum Alloys

    Aluminum alloys are classified into:

    • Wrought alloys (e.g. sheet, plate, extrusions, rod, and wire)
    • Cast alloys (cast by sand casting, permanent-mold, and die casting)

    #Wrought Aluminum Alloys

    Classification of wrought alloys:

    • First digit — principal alloying element
    • Remaining digits — specific composition of the alloy

    2xxx, 6xxx, and 7xxx series can be precipitation strengthened through heat treatment.
    Remaining series can only be strengthened through cold-working and thus, are not
    suitable as structural airframe applications.

    Precipitation strengthening process:

    • Solution treatment — heated to above solvus line to obtain a homogenous phase
    • Quenching — rapidly cooled to obtain a supersaturated solid solution
    • Ageing — heated to below solvus temperature to form precipitates

    Ageing the alloy at room temperature is called natural ageing, whereas ageing at elevated temperatures is called artificial ageing. By heating an alloy to an elevated temperature, it promotes the formation of fine precipitates.

    Anisotropy of wrought alloys:

    • Wrought alloys are made by deformation processes
    • An alloy is made to flow in the direction of working
    • Grains become elongated along the working direction
    • Alloy is strongest in the working direction, where the grains are elongated

    #Temper Designations

    Basic temper designations:

    • F — as fabricated; no control over the amount of strain hardening
    • O — annealed and recrystallized; temper with lowest strength and highest ductility
    • H — strain-hardened (cold-worked)
    • T — heat-treated (age-hardened)

    #Galvanic Corrosion

    Galvanic corrosion occurs when two dissimilar metals are in electrical contact with one another. Dissimilar metals and alloys have different electrode potentials, with one acting
    as the anode and the other acting as the cathode. The electropotential difference results
    in the anode being attacked (corroded), while the cathode is protected.

    Surface corrosion prevention:

    • Alclad — pure aluminum bonded to surface of a high-strength aluminum alloy core
    • Anodizing — increases the thickness of the natural oxide layer on the surface
    • Zinc chromate — chemical compound that is used to coat the surface

    #Titanium Alloys

    Properties of titanium alloys:

    • Excellent corrosion resistance
    • Excellent mechanical properties
    • Higher fatigue strength than aluminum alloys
    • Retains strength at high temperatures

    Phases of titanium alloys:

    • α titanium — HCP crystal structure at low temperatures
    • β titanium — BCC crystal structure at temperatures above 882°C
    • α+β — two phase alloy containing a mixture of α and β phases

    Groups of alloying elements:

    • Tin — solid solution strengthening without affecting transformation temperature
    • Aluminum — increases transformation temperature; stabilizes the α phase
    • Molybdenum — decreases transformation temperature; stabilizes the β phase
    • Manganese — produces a eutectic reaction that reduces transformation temperature

    #α Titanium Alloy

    Description of alloy:

    • Best creep resistance; often used in high temperature applications
    • Less ductile as HCP α-phase has less slip systems than BCC β-phase
    • Cannot be strengthened through heat treatment due to single phase structure

    Since the titanium alloy only has a single phase, its strength cannot be increased through precipitation strengthening, a heat treatment process. It is strengthened through solid
    solution strengthening, with the addition of alloying atoms of different size.

    The most common α titanium alloy contains 5% aluminum and 2.5% tin, which provide solid solution strengthening for the HCP α-phase. The alloy is annealed in the β region. Rapid cooling of the alloy produces a needle-like α structure.

    #β Titanium Alloy

    Description of alloy:

    • Metastable β titanium is formed through rapid cooling
    • More ductile than other types due to BCC structure of β-phase
    • Can be solid solution treated and aged to achieve higher strengths
    • High density due to heavy alloying elements (molybdenum, vanadium, niobium)

    #α+β Titanium Alloy

    With proper balancing of the α and β stabilizers, a mixture of α and β can be produced
    at room temperature. The most common α+β titanium alloy contains 6% aluminum
    and 4% vanadium.

    Description of alloy:

    • Good fatigue and corrosion resistance
    • Heat-treatable due to the presence of two phases
    • Mechanical properties can be controlled using heat treatment processes

    Heat treatment process:

    • Annealing — heated to just below α+β transformation temperature
    • Slow cooling — results in equiaxed α grains, which provides good ductility
    • Fast cooling — results in needle-like α grains, has low fatigue crack growth rate

    Quenching from high temperature (low alloy content):

    • β phase transforms to titanium martensite α′ when it crosses the martensite line
    • When alloy is reheated to temper temperature, β precipitates are produced
    • Resulting microstructure is α grains with β precipitates

    Quenching from high temperature (high alloy content):

    • More highly-alloyed compositions produce supersaturated βss​ when quenched
    • When alloy is reheated to ageing temperature, α precipitates are produced
    • Resulting microstructure is β grains with α precipitates

    If higher strength is required, it is more desirable to have α grains with β precipitates, as
    HCP     α-phase has higher strength due to lesser slip systems. If ductility is required, it is
    more desirable to have     β grains with α precipitates, as the β-phase is more ductile.

    #Superplastic Forming and Diffusion Bonding

    Titanium is difficult to machine due to:

    • High strength
    • Relatively low modulus
    • Low thermal conductivity
    • High reactivity at high temperature

    Super plasticity:

    • Able to deform beyond its breaking point without localized necking and rupture
    • Requires very fine grains, and deformation process is carried out above 0.5Tm​
    • Involves grain boundary sliding and diffusion

    Advantages of superplastic forming:

    • Eliminate machining operations (product has excellent precision and surface finish)
    • Complex shapes can be formed in a single operation
    • Minimal residual stress as grains are not deformed
    • Improved formability with no springback

    Diffusion bonding:

    • Form of solid-state welding technique
    • Welding temperature is below the melting point temperature of the alloy
    • High temperature and pressure allows the diffusion of atoms across
      the interface between two materials without plastic deformation

    #Superalloys

    Properties of superalloys (nickel, iron-nickel, cobalt alloys):

    • Resistance to creep at temperatures up to 1000°C
    • High strength at elevated temperatures
    • Resistance to corrosion

    Solid-solution strengthening:

    • Addition of alloying elements with large atoms — chromium, molybdenum, tungsten
    • Smaller addition of alloying elements with small atoms — zirconium, niobium, boron
    • Alloying elements distort the lattice structure and impede dislocation movement
    • Larger size alloying elements are preferred due to their high melting points
    • Larger size alloying elements also diffuse slower

    Precipitation strengthening:

    • For superalloys containing aluminum and titanium (intermetallic compounds)
    • Precipitates are formed through solution treatment followed by ageing
    • Increased strength and creep resistance at elevated temperatures

    Most common precipitate in Ni and Fe-Ni based superalloys is FCC γ′ nickel-aluminum
    or nickel-titanium precipitates.     γ′ has about 0.1% lattice mismatch with FCC γ matrix,
    leading to lower surface energy and extraordinary long-term stability.

    Carbide strengthening:

    • Carbides impede dislocation movement and grain boundary sliding
    • Increased strength and creep resistance at elevated temperatures

    Diffusion rates are higher at grain boundaries. The presence of carbides, which are
    more stable and tend to precipitate along grain boundaries, prevent grains from
    sliding relative to each other.

    #Types of Superalloys

    #Nickel and Iron-Nickel Based

    Nickel superalloys are commonly used in high-temperature environments.
    Thus, at these elevated temperatures, high creep strength is desirable.

    Iron-nickel superalloys are commonly used in temperatures up to 600°C.
    Thus, high tensile strength and ease of processing are desirable.

    Iron-nickel superalloys have a lower operating temperature due to the low
    temperature stability of the precipitates formed (nickel-niobium vs nickel-aluminum/titanium found in nickel superalloys).

    Nickel and iron-nickel superalloys are strengthened by:

    • Precipitation strengthening
    • Carbide strengthening

    Carbides impede dislocation movement at grain boundaries and resist the sliding of grains, caused by higher atomic diffusion rates. However, at higher temperatures,
    atomic diffusion within the grains becomes a concern. Thus, precipitates reduce
    the atomic diffusion rates within the grains.

    #Cobalt Based

    Cobalt superalloys are commonly used in corrosive environments.
    Thus, high oxidation resistance is desirable to reduce corrosion.

    Pure cobalt has a HCP structure below 417°C and FCC structure above it.
    The addition of nickel serves to stabilize the FCC austenitic structure.

    Cobalt superalloys are strengthened by:

    • Solid-solution strengthening
    • Carbide strenghtening

    Additional information:

    • Good stress rupture properties above 1000°C
    • Not suitable for highly stressed components (nickel superalloys are preferred)
    • Used for low stress long life static components
    • High oxidation resistance

    #Casting vs Wrought

    Manufacturing processes:

    • Investment casting — equiaxed casting, single crystal casting
    • Wrought — power metallurgy, vacuum arc remelting, electroslag remelting

    CastWrought
    Grain SizeCoarseFine
    Tensile StrengthLowerHigh
    Fatigue ResistanceLowerGood
    Creep / Rupture StrengthGoodLower
    ApplicationTurbine bladesTurbine disks

    Investment casting:

    • Equiaxed grains — same mechanical properties in all directions but may fail as
      creep failures tend to initiate at transverse grain boundaries (width direction)
    • Directionally solidified — no grain boundaries in the transverse direction
    • Single crystal — no grain boundaries (highest creep strength)

    #Hot Corrosion (Sulfidation)

    Hot corrosion occurs due to a chemical reaction between sulfur contained in the fuel and heat in the turbines, and it is a non-recoverable degradation. Thus, a protective coating is required on turbine blades to prevent corrosion from occurring.

    The coating needs to be reapplied every
    few years to prevent hot corrosion as once the sulfidation successfully penetrates the layer, the corrosion process is irreversible.

    #Thermal Barrier Coating

    Purpose of thermal barrier coating:

    • Reduce heat flux through the turbine blade
    • Allow greater gas-metal temperature differences (higher operating temperatures)

    Properties required for coating:

    • Low thermal conductivity
    • Resistance to thermal shock
    • Contain voids for thermal insulation
