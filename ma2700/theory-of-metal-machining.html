    Theory of Metal Machining

    #Metal Machining

    Material removal process:

    • Family of shaping operations
    • Excess material is removed from a starting work until desired geometry is obtained
    • Conventional machining — sharp cutting tool is used to mechanically cut the material
    • Abrasive process — material is removed by action of hard, abrasive particles (grinding)
    • Nontraditional process — use various energy forms and tools (laser/water jet cutting)

    Machining in manufacturing:

    • Usually performed after other manufacturing processes such as casting, forging
    • Casting, forging are basic processes to create the initial shape of the work part
    • Machining provides the final geometry, dimensions, and finish

    Advantages of machining:

    • Variety of work materials — metals, plastics and plastic composites, ceramics
    • Variety of shapes and geometric features — screw threads, gear teeth
    • Good dimensional accuracy and surface finish

    Disadvantages of machining:

    • Wasteful of material — chips generated are wasted material
    • Time-consuming — takes more time to shape a part than alternative shaping processes

    #Machining Operations

    Turning operation:

    • Single point cutting tool removes material from a rotating workpiece
    • Cutting motion is provided by the rotating work part
    • Tool is fed in a direction parallel to axis of rotation of work part

    Drilling operation:

    • Used to create a round hole, using a rotating tool with two cutting edges
    • Tool is fed in a direction parallel to axis of rotation of work part

    Milling operation:

    • Rotating tool with multiple cutting edges
    • Tool is fed across the work part to produce a straight surface
    • Peripheral milling — axis of tool is parallel to the surface being machined
    • Face milling — axis of tool is perpendicular to the surface being machined

    Machine tool (lathe, milling machine):

    • Performs machining operation
    • Holds the work part securely
    • Positions cutting tool relative to work part
    • Provides power at the desired speed, feed, and depth

    #Cutting Conditions

    Relative motion is required between the tool
    and work to perform a machining operation.

    • Primary motion — cutting speed v
    • Secondary motion — feed rate f (mm/rev)
    • Penetration of cutting tool — depth of cut d

    In a turning operation, material removal rate is computed as,

    💡
    RMR​=vfd

    where RMR​ is expressed in mm3/s

    Roughing operation:

    • Used to remove large amounts of material quickly
    • High feed rate and depth of cut, low cutting speed
    • Produce a shape close to the desired form for a subsequent finishing operation

    Finishing operation:

    • Used to complete the part geometry
    • Low feed rate and depth of cut, high cutting speed
    • Achieve the final dimensions, tolerances, and surface finish

    #Theory of Chip Formation

    Cutting action in machining:

    • Shear deformation of work material to form a chip
    • At sharp edge of tool, failure of material occurs and a chip forms
    • Separation of the chip from the the work part exposes a new surface
    • Tool has a small clearance angle at the flank to avoid damage of new surface

    #Orthogonal Cutting Model

    Orthogonal cutting uses a wedge-shaped tool in which the
    cutting edge is perpendicular to direction of cutting speed.

    Mechanics of machining:

    • Tool is forced into the material
    • Chip is formed by shear deformation along the shear plane
    • Along the shear plane, the work material is plastically deformed
    • Shear plane is oriented at an angle ϕ with respect to the work surface
    • Rake angle α determines the direction the chip will flow when it is formed

    Chip thickness ratio is defined as,

    💡
    r=rc​to​​

    where to​ is thickness of the chip prior to chip formation, and tc​ is chip thickness

    Let ls​ be the length of the shear plane.

    r=tc​to​​=ls​cos(ϕ−α)ls​sinϕ​=cos(ϕ−α)sinϕ​

    Rearranging the above equation,

    💡
    tanϕ=1−rsinαrcosα​

    Area of the shear plane is expressed as,

    💡
    As​=sinϕto​w​

    A larger shear plane angle results in a smaller shear plane area. As a result,
    lower shear force, cutting forces, cutting energy, and cutting temperature.

    #Forces in Metal Cutting

    Coefficient of friction between the cutting tool and chip is expressed as,

    💡
    μ=NF​=tanβ

    Shear stress that acts along the shear plane is expressed as,

    💡
    τ=As​Fs​​

    Forces applied by cutting tool:

    • Friction force F
    • Normal force N

    Forces applied by workpiece:

    • Shear force Fs​
    • Normal force Fn​

    The forces F, N, Fs​, and Fn​ cannot be directly measured in a machining operation. However, it is possible to measure the cutting forces using a force-measuring device.

    Forces acting on cutting tool:

    • Cutting force Fc​
    • Thrust force Ft​

    Using trigonometric relations,

    💡
    F=Fc​sinα+Ft​cosα
    💡
    N=Fc​cosα−Ft​sinα
    💡
    Fs​=Fc​cosϕ−Ft​sinϕ
    💡
    Fn​=Fc​sinϕ+Ft​cosϕ

    #Merchant Equation

    Of all the possible angles at which shear deformation can occur, there is one angle
    at which the shear stress is just equal to the shear strength of the work material.
    Thus, shear deformation occurs at this angle.

    Rewriting the equation for shear stress,

    τ=As​Fs​​=(t0​w)/sinϕFc​cosϕ−Ft​sinϕ​

    Differentiating the above with respect to ϕ and equating to zero,

    💡
    ϕ=45+2α​−2β​

    Work material will select a shear plane angle ϕ that minimizes cutting energy.

    To increase shear plane angle:

    • Increase rake angle α — proper tool design
    • Reduce friction angle β or coefficient of friction μ — apply lubrication

    #Power and Energy Relations

    Cutting power required to perform a machining operation is,

    💡
    Pc​=Fc​v

    where Fc​ is cutting force and v is cutting speed

    Gross power required to operate the machine tool is,

    💡
    Pgross​=EPc​​

    where E is the mechanical efficiency of the machine tool

    It is useful to convert power into power per unit volume rate of metal cut (specific energy).

    💡
    U=Pu​=RMR​Pc​​=v(to​w)Fc​v​=to​wFc​​

    where specific energy U is expressed in J/mm3

    #Cutting Temperature

    Total energy consumed in machining:

    • Approximately 98% is converted into heat
    • Heat can cause high temperatures at the tool-chip interface
    • Remaining energy is retained as elastic-plastic energy in the chip
    • Lubricants is frequently used to reduce coefficient of friction and remove heat

    Implications of high temperatures:

    • Reduce tool life
    • Produce hot chips that pose safety hazards to operator
    • Cause inaccuracies in part dimensions due to thermal expansion of work material
