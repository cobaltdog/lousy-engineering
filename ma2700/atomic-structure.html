    Atomic Structure

    #Classification of Materials

    Materials can be grouped into four categories:
    Categories are primarily based on atomic structure.

    • Metals
    • Ceramics
    • Polymers
    • Composites

    Description of metals:

    • Composed of metallic elements and non-metallic elements in relatively small amounts
    • Atoms in metals and their alloys are arranged in a very orderly manner
    • Strong and stiff, relatively ductile, and resistant to fracture
    • Extremely good conductors of electricity and heat
    • Ferrous metals have desirable magnetic properties

    Description of ceramics:

    • Compounds of metallic and non-metallic elements (oxides, nitrides, carbides, etc.)
    • Strong and stiff, extremely brittle (lack of ductility), and susceptible to fracture
    • Extremely good insulators to electricity to heat

    Description of polymers (plastics):

    • Organic compounds chemically based on carbon, hydrogen, non-metallic elements
    • Very large molecular structures (chainlike in nature)
    • Extremely ductile and pliable, and chemically inert
    • Low electrical conductivity

    Description of composites:

    • Composed of two or more different materials (e.g. carbon fiber reinforced plastics)
    • Fibers are long and continuous (fibrous), or short and discontinuous (particulates)
    • Fibers are strong and stiff, while polymer matrix is ductile

    #Atomic Structure

    Each atom consists of a nucleus of protons and neutrons, encircled by moving electrons.

    • A chemical element is characterized by their atomic (proton) number
    • Atomic mass/weight is the sum of the masses of protons and neutrons
    • Elements with different atomic masses are called isotopes

    Avogadro’s number is defined as the number of atoms/molecules per mole of a substance.

    💡
    NA​=6.022×1023 atoms/mol

    #Electron Configuration

    The Bohr atomic model assumes that electrons revolve around the atomic nucleus in discrete orbitals, and the position of any electron is well-defined in terms of its orbital.

    The Bohr model has limitations due to its inability
    to explain several phenomena involving electrons.
    Thus, the wave-mechanical model came to be.

    Wave-mechanical model:

    • Position is the probability of an electron being at various locations (subshells)
    • Position of an electron is described by a probability distribution (electron cloud)

    • Principal quantum number denoted by n (principal energy level or shell)
    • Maximum number of electrons in each shell is given by 2n2
    • Subshells are denoted by s, p, d, f whereby s<p<d<f
    • Subshells have maximum of 2, 6, 10, 14 electrons respectively

      Subshells have energy states 1, 3, 5, 7 respectively, with
      each state accommodating a maximum of two electrons.

    Description of valence electrons:

    • Electrons that occupy the outermost shell
    • Participate in bonding between atoms
    • Electro-positive elements give electrons during chemical reactions
    • Electro-negative elements accept electrons during chemical reactions

    Elements with their outermost shell filled completely are inert. Some elements with
    unfilled valence shells assume stable configurations by gaining or losing electrons
    to form positive ions (cations) or negative ions (anions).

    Electronegativity is the degree to which an atom accepts electrons.
    It is measured on a scale of 0 to 4.1 with highest being the most active.

    Note that an electronegativity of less than 2 is considered an electro-positive element.

    #Primary Interatomic Bonds

    Types of primary (chemical) bonds in solids:

    • Ionic bonds — transfer of electrons
    • Covalent bonds — sharing of valence electrons
    • Metallic bonds — sharing of free electrons

    #Ionic Bonding

    Description of bond:

    • Formed by two unlike atoms with different electronegativity
    • Metallic element gives up valence electrons to non-metallic element
    • Transfer of electrons results in the formation of positive and negative ions
    • Ionic bonding is due to the electrostatic force between cations and anions

    Compounds with ionic bonds:

    • Poor electrical and thermal conductor
    • Entire ions must move in order for current to flow
    • Heat transfer requires transfer of energy (movement)
    • Ion movement is difficult and slow, thus low conductivity

    Far field effect (attraction) vs near field effect (repulsion):

    • At large distances, interactions are negligible as ions are too far apart
    • At small distances, electrons of each ion repel each other
    • Net force is the sum of attractive and repulsive forces

    #Covalent Bonding

    Description of bond:

    • Formed by atoms with small differences in electronegativity
    • Two covalently bonded atoms will each contribute at least one electron to the bond
    • Overlapping of electron shells in the region between the bonding atoms

    Compounds with covalent bonds:

    • High melting points but low ductility (brittle)
    • Displacement of atoms results in a repulsive force that causes crystal to crack

    #Metallic Bonding

    Description of bond:

    • Formed when atoms give up their valence electrons (sea of free electrons)
    • Free electrons are not bound to any particular atom and drift freely within the metal
    • Positive ion cores are bonded by mutual attraction to negatively charged electrons

    The free electrons shield the positively charged ion cores from the repulsive electrostatic
    forces that they would otherwise exert upon one another. They acts as a “glue” to hold
    the ion cores together.

    Metals and their alloys:

    • Good electrical and thermal conductor
    • Moderate melting point with good ductility
    • Free electrons can easily move and carry a current
    • Electrons can transfer energy to the metallic ions and other electrons
    • Rows of atoms can shear continuously without a sudden change in bonds (ductility)

    #Secondary Bonds (van der Waals)

    Description of bond:

    • Weak in comparison to the primary bonds
    • Due to the attraction of electric dipoles in atoms or molecules
    • Dipoles form due to a physical separation of positive and negative portions

    A dipole may be induced in an atom that is normally electrically symmetrical. Permanent
    dipoles exist in some atoms by virtue of an asymmetrical arrangement of positively and
    negatively charged regions.

    Description of hydrogen bonding:

    • Occurs between molecules in which hydrogen is covalently bonded another element
    • Hydrogen end of the bond is essentially a positively charged bare proton
    • Positively charged end of the molecule creates a strong attractive force
