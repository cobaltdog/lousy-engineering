    Fundamentals of Welding

    #Welding Process

    Description of welding:

    • Materials-joining process
    • Two or more parts are joined at their contacting surfaces by application of heat/pressure
    • Many welding processes are accomplished by heat alone, with no pressure applied
    • Filler material is added to facilitate the joining of parts
    • Assemblage of parts is called a weldment

    Advantages of welding:

    • Welding provides a permanent joint — welded parts become a single entity
    • Welded joint can be stronger than parent materials if filler material has superior strength
    • Most economical way to join components in terms of material usage and fabrication costs
    • Does not require alterations (drilling of holes) and additional hardware (rivets, bolts, etc.)
    • Not restricted to factory environment, can be accomplished in the field

    Limitations and drawbacks of welding:

    • Welding is performed manually, requires skilled labor — high labor cost
    • Inherently dangerous as welding involves the use of high energy
    • Welded joints do not allow for convenient disassembly
    • Welded joints can suffer from quality defects that are difficult to detect

    Safety concerns of welding:

    • High temperatures of molten metal
    • In gas welding, fuels used pose a fire hazard
    • Possible electrical shock due to the use of high voltage power
    • Ultraviolet radiation emitted in arc welding is detrimental to human vision
    • Respiratory risks due to smoke and fumes — ventilation is required to exhaust them

    Types of welding processes:

    • Fusion welding — joining is achieved by melting the base metals (with filler)
    • Solid-state welding — joining is achieved by applying heat and pressure (without filler)
    • In solid-state welding, the temperature is below melting point of metals being welded

    #Fusion Welding

    Description of fusion welding:

    • Uses heat to melt the base metals
    • Filler metal is added to the molten pool to facilitate the process
    • Filler metal provides bulk and added strength to the welded joint

    Types of fusion welding:

    • Arc welding — melting is achieved by an electric arc
    • Resistance welding — melting is achieved by heat from electrical resistance
    • Oxyfuel gas welding — melting is achieved by heat from the burning of oxyfuel gas

    #Arc Welding

    Arc welding process:

    • Heat is generated from an electric arc between an electrode and the work
    • Pool of molten metal is formed near the electrode tip
    • Electrode is moved along the joint
    • Molten metal solidifies in its wake

    Electrode used in arc welding:

    • Consumable — provide the source of filler metal, must be changed periodically
    • Non-consumable — made of tungsten, resists melting by the arc, gradually depleted
    • If non-consumable electrodes are used, the filler metal is supplied by a separate wire

    Arc shielding:

    • Due to high temperatures, metals being joined are chemically reactive to oxygen
    • Mechanical properties of weld joint can be severely degraded by these reactions
    • Shielding gases (argon) prevent the weld metal from being exposed to the air
    • Flux is a substance used to prevent the formation of oxides and contaminants

    Arc shielding can be accomplished with a blanket of gas or flux. During welding, the flux
    melts and becomes a liquid slag, covering the operation and protecting the molten weld metal. The slag hardens upon cooling and must be removed by chipping or brushing.

    #Resistance Welding

    Resistance welding process:

    • Uses a combination of heat and pressure to join parts
    • Uses no shielding gases, flux, or filler metal (compared to arc welding)
    • Electrodes that conduct electrical power to the part are non-consumable

    #Features of Welded Joint

    Zones in a fusion-welded joint:

    • Fusion zone — mixture of base metal and filler
    • Weld interface — boundary between fusion zone and heat-affected zone
    • Heat-affected zone (HAZ) — metal has experienced temperatures <Tm​
    • Unaffected base metal zone

    Heat-affected zone (HAZ):

    • Metal has undergone microstructural changes due to the welding temperatures
    • Metal in this region has been heat-treated such that its properties have been altered
    • Usually has weak mechanical properties, welding failures often occur in this region

    #Physics of Fusion Welding

    To accomplish fusion, a source of high-density heat energy must be supplied to the contacting surfaces such that the resulting temperatures cause localized melting of
    the base metal and filler.

    #Power Density

    Power density is the rate of heat energy transferred to the work per unit area.

    💡
    PD=AreaPower​

    It is desirable to melt the metal with minimum energy but high heat densities.

    Implications of power density:

    • Too low — heat is conducted into the work as fast as it is added, no melting occurs
    • Too high — high localized temperatures vaporize the metal in the affected region
    • Power density is not uniform in an area; it is distributed as a function of area

    At low power densities, a significant amount of time is required to cause melting.
    As heat density increases, melting time is reduced. For most metals, minimum
    power density required is about     10 W/mm2.

    #Heat Balance

    Quantity of heat required to melt a given volume of metal depends on:

    • Heat to raise temperature of solid metal to its melting point
    • Heat to transform metal from solid to liquid phase

    Quantity of heat required to melt a unit volume of metal is,

    💡
    Um​=KTm​2

    where Um​ is in J/mm3, constant K=3.33×10−6, and Tm​ is in Kelvins

    Net heat energy available for welding is,

    💡
    Hw​=f1​f2​H

    where Hw​ is in joules, and H is total heat generated in joules

    Heat transfer factor f1​ — ratio of actual heat received
    at work surface to total heat generated at the source.

    Melting factor f2​ — proportion of heat received
    received at work surface that is used for melting.

    Balance equation between energy input and energy needed for welding.

    💡
    Hw​=Um​V=f1​f2​H

    where V is volume of metal melted

    Most welding processes are rate processes. Net heat energy Hw​ is delivered at a
    given rate, and the weld bead is made at a certain travel velocity. Expressing the
    above as a rate balance equation,

    💡
    RHw​​=Um​RV​=Um​Aw​v=f1​f2​RH​

    where RHw​​ is rate of heat energy delivered in watts,
    and     RV​ is volume rate of metal melted in mm3/s,
    and     RH​ is rate of input energy generated in watts,
    and     Aw​ is weld cross-section area,
    and     v is travel velocity

    #Weld Quality

    The physical integrity of the structure is dependent on the quality of the weld.

    #Welding Defects

    Cracks:

    • Fracture-type interruptions in the weld or in the base metal adjacent to the weld
    • Caused by embrittlement or low ductility of the weld and/or base metal
    • Low ductility combined with high restraint during contraction

    Cavities:

    • Voids due to porosity and shrinkage
    • Porosity — small voids formed by gases entrapped during solidification
    • Shrinkage — cavities formed by shrinkage during solidification

    Solid inclusions:

    • Non-metallic solid materials trapped within the weld metal
    • Most common form is slag inclusions for AW processes that use flux
    • Metallic oxides that form during welding of metals (aluminum oxide coating)

    Incomplete fusion:

    • Fusion has not occurred throughout the entire cross-section of joint
    • Lack of penetration — fusion has not penetrated deeply enough into the joint

    Imperfect shape or profile:

    • V-groove weld — desired profile for maximum strength
    • Undercut — portion of base metal melted away
    • Underfill — depression in weld joint (smaller cross-section results in higher stresses)
    • Overlap — weld metal spills beyond joint onto part surface but no fusion occurs

    #Inspection of Welds

    Categories of inspection methods:

    • Visual inspection
    • Non-destructive testing
    • Destructive testing

    Visual inspection:

    • Inspector visually examines the weldment
    • Conformance to dimensional specifications
    • Warping (distortion of the weldment)
    • Cracks, cavities, incomplete fusion, and other surface defects
    • Only large surface defects are detectable; internal defects cannot be detected

    Non-destructive testing (NDT):

    • Methods that do not damage the specimen being inspected
    • Dye penetrant test — detect small cracks that are open to the surface
    • Fluorescent test — fluorescent penetrant that is more sensitive than dyes
    • Eddy current test — electromagnetic induction (surface and sub-surface cracks)
    • Magnetic particle test — particles concentrate in surface and sub-surface cracks
    • Ultrasonic test — cracks are detected by losses in sound transmission
    • Radiography — use of X-rays to detect internal cracks in weld metal
