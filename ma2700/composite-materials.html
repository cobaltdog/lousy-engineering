    Composite Materials

    #Classification of Composites

    A composite material is a system composed of:

    • Mixture or combination of two or more constituents
    • Constituents differ in structure and/or chemical composition
    • Essentially insoluble in each other, and have a clear interface separating them

    Advantages of composites:

    • Lightweight due to low density of the matrix material used
    • High strength and stiffness due to the reinforcement used
    • High specific modulus (high specific strength)
    • Strength and stiffness is a specific direction can be controlled
      by controlling the amount and orientation of the reinforcement

    Composite materials contain one or more phases of reinforcement and another
    phase (bulk material) that houses the reinforcement and is called the matrix.

    Properties of reinforcement phase:

    • Main load-bearing material (carries most of the load)
    • Provide strength and stiffness while being lightweight
    • Provide other properties such as low thermal expansion

    Properties of matrix:

    • Retain the shape of the structure
    • Protect and stabilize the reinforcement phase
    • Transfer loads to and between reinforcing elements

    Composites are classified based on reinforcement phase:

    • Fiber (micron scale)
      • Anisotropic (high strength and stiffness in the fiber direction)
      • Brittle and fails suddenly with little or no plastic deformation
      • Have very small diameters, and are short or long lengths
      • E.g. glass fibers, graphite fibers, or aramid fibers
    • Particulate (micron/macro scale)
      • Typically isotropic and comes in various sizes and shapes
      • E.g. gravel and sand
    • Nano reinforcement (nano scale)
      • Reinforcement can be a metal or ceramic

    A composite can be referred to as fiber-reinforced, particle-reinforced, or nanocomposite.
    It can also be classified based on the nature of the matrix, polymer matrix, metal matrix,
    or ceramic matrix composite.

    #Laminated Composites

    For fiber-reinforced composites, due to their anisotropy, multiple layers of plies are
    stacked together at different orientations to maximize its strength in all directions.

    Conventions for fibers:

    • 0° to represent the primary loading direction
    • 90° to represent transverse to primary loading direction

    #Sandwich Composites

    Description of composite:

    • Lightweight core material sandwiched between two thin outer layers
    • E.g. honeycomb sandwich and cladded sandwich structures

    Description of honeycomb sandwich:

    • Fabricated by adhesively bonding aluminum alloy face
      sheets to aluminum alloy honeycomb core sections
    • Stiff, rigid, strong, and lightweight sandwich panel

    Description of cladded sandwich:

    • Thin outer metal layers are bonded to inner core metal to form
      metallurgical bonds between the outside layers and inner core
    • High-strength aluminum alloys can be protected by a thin layer
      of soft, highly corrosion-resistant pure aluminum cladding

    #Polymer Matrix Composite

    #Common Reinforcement Fibers

    Glass fibers:

    • Good balance of mechanical properties and low cost
    • Good corrosion resistance
    • E.g. E-glass (electrical) and S-glass (structural)

    Carbon/graphite fibers:

    • Low density with excellent strength and stiffness
    • Low coefficient of thermal expansion
    • Poor electrical conductivity
    • Good fatigue resistance
    • Brittle and expensive

    Aramid fibers:

    • Lightweight and high strength
    • Good fatigue resistance
    • High impact resistance

    #Common Matrix Materials

    Polyester resin:

    • Lower in cost but not as strong as epoxy resin
    • Used in boat hulls, building panels, and structural panels in automotive and aircraft

    Epoxy resin:

    • High performance matrix
    • Higher strength and lower shrinkage after curing than polyester resin
    • Better high temperature performance — can be used at temperatures up to 135°C

    Curing of thermoset resin matrix:

    • Consist of a resin and a compatible curing agent
    • Resin and agent are mixed to form a low viscosity liquid that cures
      as a result of either internally generated or externally applied heat
    • Curing reaction forms a series of cross-links between the molecular
      chains such that a large molecular network is formed
    • Curing process is irreversible

    #Isostrain and Isostress

    #Isostrain

    Consider a fiber composite subjected to isostrain loading conditions, whereby both the fibers and matrix deform by the same amount (isostrain).

    εc​=εf​=εm​

    Load on the composite is the sum of the load on
    the fiber layers and the load on the matrix layers,

    Pc​=Pf​+Pm​

    σc​Ac​=σf​Af​+σm​Am​

    σc​Vc​=σf​Vf​+σm​Vm​

    Note that lengths of the fiber and matrix are equal.

    Volume fraction of the composite is equal to 1.

    σc​=σf​Vf​+σm​Vm​

    Dividing the above equation by the strain,

    εc​σc​​=εf​σf​Vf​​+εm​σm​Vm​​

    💡
    Ec​=Ef​Vf​+Em​Vm​

    where Vf​ and Vm​ are volume fractions of the fiber and matrix respectively

    #Isostress

    Consider a fiber composite subjected to isostress loading conditions, whereby the layers are perpendicular to the applied stress.

    σc​=σf​=σm​

    Total strain of the composite is the sum of
    the strains in the fiber and matrix layers,

    εc​=εf​+εm​

    εc​=εf​Vf​+εm​Vm​

    From Hooke’s law σ=Eε,

    Ec​σc​​=Ef​σf​Vf​​+Em​σm​Vm​​

    Ec​1​=Ef​Vf​​+Em​Vm​​

    Simplifying the above equation,

    Ec​1​=Ef​Em​Vf​Em​+Vm​Ef​​

    💡
    Ec​=Vf​Em​+Vm​Ef​Ef​Em​​

    where Vf​ and Vm​ are volume fractions of the fiber and matrix respectively

    Comparing the modulus of the composite under the different loading conditions, higher modulus values are obtained with isostrain loading at the same volume fraction of fibers.

    #Laminate Joints

    Advantages of adhesive bonding:

    • More uniform stress distribution
    • Usually lighter and less expensive
    • Smooth external surface and integrally sealed joint

    Disadvantages of adhesive bonding:

    • Permanent joint (difficult to disassemble)
    • Susceptible to environmental degradation
    • Sensitive to surface preparation

    Basic vs tapered lap joint:

    • Basic lap joint is susceptible to peeling when a tensile load is applied
    • Tapering allows for more gradual geometry discontinuity (lower interfacial stress)

    #Ceramic Matrix Composite

    Ceramic matrix composites are used in extremely high temperature applications.
    The main types of reinforcement are continuous-fiber, discontinuous-fiber, and
    particulate reinforced.

    Monolithic ceramic:

    • High strength and hardness
    • High melting temperatures
    • Chemical inertness
    • Wear resistance
    • Low density
    • Extremely low fracture toughness

    Ceramic matrix composites incorporate reinforcement to improve on the fracture
    toughness of monolithic ceramics, by relying on energy dissipation mechanisms.

    #Toughening Mechanisms

    Toughening of ceramic composites is due to fibers interfering with crack propagation.

    Crack deflection:

    • Crack is deflected when encountering the reinforcement
    • Crack propagation path follow a winding path
    • Higher stresses are required for crack growth

    Crack bridging:

    • Fibers bridge the crack and help
      keep the material together
    • Higher stresses are required
      to cause further cracking

    Fiber pullout:

    • Friction caused by fibers being pulled out
      of the cracking matrix absorbs energy
    • Higher stresses must be applied
      to cause further cracking

    A good interfacial bond is required between the fibers and matrix for higher strengths.
    Otherwise, a crack will propagate straight through the fibers due to fiber de-bonding,
    resulting in little to no energy absorption.

    #Common Reinforcement Fibers

    Discontinuous-fiber (whiskers):

    • Near-perfect single crystals
    • Strength is nearly equal to the theoretical strength of the material
    • Fibers are usually up to 1 μm in diameter and 200 μm in length

    Monofilament fibers:

    • Large fibers
    • Fibers are usually 0.14 mm in diameter

    Multifilament fibers:

    • High strength and flexible
    • Fibers are usually up to 15 μm in diameter
    • Critical bend radius of less than 1 mm (provide manufacturing flexibility)

    #Common Matrix Materials

    Carbon matrix:

    • Exceptionally stable in the absence of oxygen
    • Lightweight but brittle in the monolithic form
    • Capable of withstanding ~2200°C in vacuum and inert environment

    Glass ceramic matrix:

    • For moderate temperature applications
    • Can be melted to low viscosity for better fiber impregnation
    • Less fiber degradation due to low processing temperature

    Oxide matrix:

    • Relatively low cost
    • Rapid sintering at moderate temperatures
    • High temperature oxidation resistance
    • Poor properties at high temperatures

    Non-oxide matrix:

    • High melting point
    • Excellent mechanical properties at elevated temperatures
    • Capable of withstanding ~1480°C in air

    #Processing of PMCs

    #Starting Materials for PMCs

    In a PMC, the starting materials are a polymer and a reinforcing phase.
    They are processed separately before becoming phases in the composite.

    Nomenclature for FRPs:

    • Yarn — twisted collection of fibers
    • Cloth — fabric of woven yarns
    • Roving — collection of untwisted, continuous strands of fibers
    • Woven roving — fabric consisting of untwisted fibers rather than yarns
    • Prepreg — fibers impregnated with partially cured thermosetting resins
    • Mat — randomly oriented short fibers held loosely together with a binder

    #Open-Mold Processes

    Description of process:

    • Use of a single positive or negative mold surface to produce laminated FRP structures
    • Starting materials are applied to the mold in layers, building up to desired thickness
    • Followed by curing and part removal
    • Moldings are usually large

    #Hand Lay-Up

    Description of lay-up:

    • Successive layers of resin and reinforcement are manually applied to an open mold
    • Finished molding is usually trimmed with a power saw to size the outside edges
    • Large moldings, low in production quantity
    • Most labor-intensive process

    Lay-up process:

    • Mold is cleaned and treated with a mold release agent
    • Thin gel coat (resin) is applied to become the outside surface of the molding
    • Successive layers of resin and fiber are applied once gel coat has partially set
    • Each layer is rolled to fully impregnate the fiber with resin and remove air bubbles
    • Part is cured and the fully hardened part is removed from the mold

    #Spray-Up

    Spray-up process:

    • Liquid resin and chopped fibers are sprayed onto an open mold
    • Spray gun has chopper mechanism that feeds in continuous fibers and cuts them
    • Fibers are mixed with resin stream which result in random orientation of the fibers
    • Products not as strong as those made by lay-up due to randomly oriented fibers

    #Automated Lay-Up

    Automated lay-up process:

    • Automated machine apply a continuous unidirectional fiber prepreg
    • Permits a variety of fiber orientations to be applied between laminations

    #Curing

    Curing process:

    • Required for all thermosetting resins used in FRP
    • Curing cross-links the polymer, transforming it from liquid to a hardened state
    • Curing is done in an autoclave — enclosed chamber that apply heat and pressure

    Principal process parameters:

    • Time — curing must be sufficiently complete before a part is removed
    • Temperature — heat speeds up the curing reaction
    • Pressure — force out air bubbles trapped within

    #Closed-Mold Processes

    Description of process:

    • Molds consist of two sections that open and close during each molding cycle
    • Higher production rates but higher tooling cost
    • Good finish on all part surfaces
    • Closer control over tolerances
    • More complex geometries

    #Compression Molding

    Molding process:

    • Charge is placed in the lower mold section
    • Sections are brought together under pressure
    • Pressure causes charge to take the shape of the mold cavity
    • Mold halves are then heated to cure the thermosetting polymer
    • When molding is sufficiently cured, mold is opened and part is removed

    #Transfer and Injection Molding

    Transfer molding process:

    • Charge of thermosetting resin with short fibers is placed in a pot/chamber
    • Charge is then heated, and squeezed by a ram into the mold cavity
    • Heating of the mold cures the resin

    Injection molding process:

    • Rotating screw injects resin and fibers into the mold cavity
    • Chopped fibers are used as the screw will reduce any continuous fibers
    • Fibers tend to be aligned when exiting through the nozzle (directional properties)

    #Pultrusion and Pulforming

    Pultrusion is a process similar to extrusion, but the workpiece is pulled through the die.

    Pultrusion process:

    • Continuous fiber rovings are dipped in a resin bath and pulled through shaping die
    • Sections produced are reinforced throughout their length by continuous fibers
    • Sections have a constant cross-section (depends on shape of die opening)
    • Cured product is cut into long, straight sections

    Pultrusion is limited to straight sections of constant cross-section. Pulforming allows for forming of curved sections, and whose cross-sections may vary throughout the length.
    It is capable of altering the cross-section at one or more locations.
