    Fundamentals of Casting

    #Casting Process

    Advantages of casting:

    • Can be used to create complex geometries (including both external and internal shapes)
    • Capable of producing parts to net shape (no further manufacturing operations required)
    • Can be used to produce very large parts
    • Suited to mass production

    Disadvantages of casting:

    • Limitations on mechanical properties due to porosity
    • Poor dimensional accuracy and surface finish for some processes
    • Safety hazards to humans when processing hot molten metals
    • Environmental problems

    Casting process:

    • Metal is heated to a temperature high enough to transform it into a liquid state
    • Molten metal is then poured into the cavity of a mold
    • Molten metal cools down sufficiently for solidification to begin
    • As the molten metal changes phase, it assumes the shape of the mold cavity
    • Once the casting has cooled sufficiently, it is removed from the mold

    Depending on the casting method and metal used, further processing may be required,
    including trimming excess metal from the actual cast part, cleaning the surface,
    inspecting the product, and heat treatment to enhance properties.

    Description of the mold:

    • Contains a cavity whose geometry determines the shape of the part
    • Cavity is slightly oversized to allow for shrinkage during solidification and cooling
    • Mold cavity must be designed for the particular metal if dimensional accuracy is critical
    • Made of a variety of materials, including sand, plaster, ceramic, and metal

    #Types of Mold

    Types of mold:

    • Open mold — metal is simply poured in a container with the desired shape
    • Closed mold — more complex mold geometry and requires a gating system
      to permit the molten metal to flow from outside of the mold into the cavity

    Casting processes are categorized according to the type of mold used:

    • Expendable mold
    • Permanent mold

    Description of expendable mold:

    • Mold must be destroyed in order to remove the casting
    • Made of sand, plaster, whose shape is maintained by using binders

    Description of permanent mold:

    • Can be used over and over to produce many castings
    • Made of a metal that withstand the high temperatures of the casting operation
    • Mold consists of two or more sections that can open to permit removal of the part

    AdvantageDisadvantage
    Expendable MoldMore complex geometriesProduction limited by time required to make the mold rather than casting
    Permanent MoldHigher production ratesGeometries limited by the need to open the mold

    #Heating and Pouring

    #Heating the Metal

    Heating furnaces are used to heat the metal to a temperature sufficient for casting.

    Heat required is the sum of:

    • Heat to raise the temperature to the melting point
    • Heat of fusion to convert it from a solid to a liquid
    • Heat to raise the molten metal to desired temperature for pouring

    #Pouring the Metal

    The flow of molten metal through the gating system and into the cavity is a critical step. For
    this step to be successful, the metal must flow into all regions of the mold before solidifying.

    Factors that determine succes:

    • Pouring temperature
    • Pouring rate
    • Turbulence

    If pouring rate is excessive, turbulence can occur, which aggravates mold erosion,
    gradual wearing away of the mold surfaces due to impact of the flowing molten
    metal. Air is also introduced into the molten metal, which results in porosity.

    #Solidification and Cooling

    Solidification process differs depending on whether the metal is a pure element of an alloy.

    • Pure metal solidifies at a constant temperature equal to its freezing/melting point
    • Alloys freeze over a temperature range, which depends on the alloy system

    #Solidification Time

    Solidification time is dependent on the size and shape of the casting — Chvorinov’s rule.

    💡
    TTS​=Cm​(AV​)n

    where Cm​ is the mold constant, and n=2

    Chvorinov’s rule:

    • Casting with a higher volume-to-surface area ratio will cool and solidify more slowly
    • Riser performs the function of feeding molten metal into the main cavity
    • Metal in the riser must remain in the liquid phase longer than the casting
    • Design the riser to have a higher AV​ ratio such that the main casting will
      most likely solidify first and the effects of shrinkage will be minimized

    #Solidification Shrinkage

    Solidification shrinkage:

    • Occurs because the solid phase has a higher density than the liquid phase
    • Solidification causes a reduction in the volume per unit weight of metal
    • Pattern-makers account for thermal contraction by making oversized cavities
    • Pattern shrinkage allowance — relative size of mold to final casting size

    Shrinkage occurs in three steps:

    1. Reduction in level caused by liquid contraction during cooling
    2. Reduction in height due to formation of shrinkage cavity
    3. Thermal contraction during phase change from liquid to solid

    #Directional Solidification

    To minimize the impacts of shrinkage, it is desirable for regions of the casting
    furthest away from the liquid metal supply to freeze first for solidification to
    progress from these regions toward the riser.

    • Molten metal will continually be available from risers to prevent shrinkage voids
    • Desired directional solidification is achieved by Chvorinov’s rule
    • Locate sections with lower AV​ ratios away from the riser, such that these sections will freeze first and the supply of liquid metal for the rest of the casting will remain open
    • Chills — internal or external heat sinks that cause rapid freezing of certain sections

    Chills are usually made of metal, which is a better conductor of heat. They conduct heat away from the molten metal at a faster rate and promote directional solidification when geometry of the mold prevents it from occurring naturally.

    #Metal Casting Processes

    #Sand Casting

    Production sequence in sand casting:

    • Pattern making and mold making
    • Melting and pouring the molten metal into the mold
    • Breaking of mold after metal has solidified and cooled
    • Cleaning and inspection (removal of gating system and riser)
    • Heat treatment to improve mechanical properties of finished casting

    Mold consists of two halves:

    • Cope — upper half of the mold
    • Drag — lower half of the mold
    • Mold halves are contained in a box called a flask
    • Mold halves are separated at the parting line

    Gating system:

    • Channel, or network of channels, by which molten metal flows into the cavity
    • Downsprue, through which metal enters a runner leading to the main cavity
    • Pouring cup, at the top of the downsprue, minimize splash and turbulence
      as the metal flows into the downsprue (prevents formation of air pockets)

    Riser:

    • Reservoir that serves as a source of liquid metal to compensate for shrinkage
    • Designed to freeze after the main casting in order to satisfy its function
    • Riser is waste metal that is separated from the casting and re-melted
    • Desirable to minimize volume of metal in the riser to minimize waste

    Mold cavity is formed by a pattern, which has the shape of the part to be cast.

    • Cavity formed by packing sand around the pattern
    • When pattern is removed, remaining void has the desired shape
    • Pattern is made oversized to allow for shrinkage as metal solidifies and cools
    • Sand used for the mold is moist and contains a binder to maintain its shape

    Types of pattern:

    • Solid pattern — made of one piece (same geometry as casting)
    • Split pattern — two pieces (divide the part along the parting line of the mold)
    • Match-plate — two pieces of a split pattern are attached to opposite sides of a plate; holes in the plate allow cope and drag sections of the mold to be aligned accurately
    • Cope-and-drag — sections can be fabricated separately

    Core (model of the interior surfaces):

    • Used to define the internal geometry of a part (usually made of sand)
    • Inserted into the cavity prior to pouring
    • Molten metal will flow and solidify between mold cavity and
      the core to form the casting’s external and internal surfaces
    • May require supports (chaplets) to hold it in position in the cavity

    #Investment Casting

    Investment casting:

    • Pattern made of wax is coated with a refractory material to make the mold
    • Wax is then melted away prior to pouring the molten metal into the mold
    • Capable of producing castings of high accuracy and intricate details
    • Precision casting process

    Advantages of casting:

    • Capable of casting complex parts with intricate sections
    • Close dimensional control and good surface finish
    • Wax can usually be recovered for reuse
    • Net shape process — does not require additional machining

    Disadvantages of casting:

    • Many processing steps are required, relatively expensive process
    • New mold is required for every casting since wax is melted away

    Steps in investment casting:

    • Wax pattern is produced with a master die that is designed with proper allowances
    • Several patterns are attached to a sprue to form a pattern tree
    • Pattern tree is coated with a thin layer of refractory material
    • Final mold is formed by repeatedly coating the tree until the mold is rigid
    • Mold is held in an inverted position and heated to melt away the wax
    • Mold is preheated to a high temperature before molten metal is poured
    • Mold is broken away from the finished casting and the parts separated from the sprue

    #Basic Permanent Mold Casting

    Permanent mold casting:

    • Metal mold constructed of two sections, designed for easy opening and closing
    • Cavity, with gating system, is machined into the two halves
    • Used for casting lower melting point alloys

    Advantages of casting:

    • Good dimensional control and surface finish
    • Rapid solidification caused by the cold metal mold results in a finer grain structure
    • Castings are stronger due to their fine grain structure (more grain boundaries)

    Disadvantages of casting:

    • Limited to metals of lower melting points
    • Simpler part geometries due to the need to open the mold
    • High cost of the mold (use of metal for the mold material)

    Due to the substantial cost of the mold, this process is best suited to high volume
    production and can be automated accordingly. Typical parts include automotive
    pistons, pump bodies, and hard disks housings.

    Steps in permanent mold casting:

    • Mold is preheated and coated (coatings aid in heat dissipation and
      lubricate the mold surfaces for easier separation of the cast part)
    • Cores, if used, are inserted, and the mold is closed
    • Molten metal is poured into the mold

    #Die Casting

    Die casting:

    • Molten metal is injected into the mold cavity under high pressure
    • Use of high pressure to force the metal into the die cavity
    • Pressure is maintained during solidification

    Die casting is carried out in special die casting machines, which are designed to hold
    and accurately close two halves of the mold, and keep them closed while the liquid
    metal is forced into the cavity.

    Advantages of casting:

    • Economical for large production quantities
    • Good accuracy and surface finish
    • Able to cast thin sections
    • Rapid cooling results in small grain size and good strength

    Disadvantages of casting:

    • Limited to metals with low melting points
    • Part geometry must allow for removal from die cavity

    #Hot-Chamber Machine

    Casting process:

    • Metal is melted in a container attached to the machine
    • Piston is used to inject the liquid metal under high pressure into the die
    • Limited to low melting point metals that do not chemically attack the plunger
    • Imposes a hardship on the injection system as it is submerged in the molten metal

    Steps in hot-chamber casting:

    • Die closes and plunger is withdrawn, molten metal flows into the chamber
    • Plunger forces metal in the chamber to flow into the die (reduce porosity)
    • Plunger is withdrawn, die is opened, and solidified part is ejected

    #Cold-Chamber Machine

    Casting process:

    • Molten metal is poured into an unheated chamber from external container
    • Piston is used to inject the metal under high pressure into the die cavity
    • Used for casting aluminum, brass, and magnesium alloys

    Steps in cold-chamber casting:

    • Die closes and ram is withdrawn, molten metal is poured into the chamber
    • Ram forces metal to flow into the die, maintains pressure during cooling
    • Ram is withdrawn, die is opened, and part is ejected

    #Casting Quality

    #Common Casting Defects

    Common casting defects:

    • Misrun — casting solidifies before completely filling mold cavity
      • Cross section of mold cavity is too thin
      • Insufficient fluidity of the molten metal
      • Low pouring temperature
      • Slow pouring rate
    • Cold shuts — two portions of metal flow together but there is
      a lack of fusion due to premature freezing (similar to misrun)
    • Cold shots — splattering of metal during pouring, causing solid
      globules of metal to form that become entrapped in the casting
      • Proper pouring procedures and gating system design
    • Shrinkage cavity — depression in the surface or internal void
    • Microporosity — small voids distributed throughout the casting
    • Hot cracking — unyielding mold does not allow casting to contract
      during solidification, which results in high tensile stress in the part

    #Sand Casting Defects

    Sand casting defects:

    • Penetration — Fluidity of metal is high, resulting in the metal penetrating into the mold
    • Mold shift — Step in cast part at parting line due to displacement of cope and drag

    #Product Design Considerations

    Guidelines and considerations for casting:

    • Geometric simplicity — Simplifying part design will improve its castability
    • Avoid unnecessary complexities — simplify mold making, reduce the need for cores
    • Avoid sharp corners and angles — sources of stress concentration
