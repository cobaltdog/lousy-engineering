    #History of Engineering

    Importance of history:

    • Learning from mistakes of past events
    • Gives meaning to the relationship between engineering and different aspects of society

    Description of technology:

    • Form of human cultural activity for practical ends and purposes
    • Forming and transforming the material world through ideas and thoughts
    • Solving problems and meeting the needs of the public in the real world

    #Early Technological Developments

    The origin of technology dates back to the Stone Age (2.5 million years ago).

    Description of Stone Age:

    • Survival through hunting and foraging
    • Made tools from wood and stone for survival purposes
    • Developed controlled use of fire, clothing and utensils for domestic use

      The ability to cook food with fire increased the range of food sources available, which allowed for the development of cognitive brain functions.

    Fertile river valleys encouraged the transition to an agricultural society.

    Description of agriculture society:

    • Domesticated animals to do work
    • Primitive sled for crop transportation
    • Development of irrigation systems
    • Smelting and working of metals

    The Greeks laid the first philosophical and scientific basis for knowledge (600 BC).

    Description of Ancient Greece (Classical Antiquity):

    • Philosophers attempted to explain the world on the basis of rationality than myth
    • Practical craft skills considered unworthy by scholars
    • Technological advancements not recognized

    The Romans inherited Greek philosophy and mathematics.

    Description of Roman Empire:

    • Engineering practice became more recognized
    • Competent engineers developed systems for both military and society
    • Became technologically stagnant due to widespread use of slaves
    • Use of slaves prevented the development labor-saving technology

      On the other hand, the Romans made engineering advances in the military and transport areas, driven by their endeavors to invade and fortify cities.

    Chaos ensued after the collapse of the Roman Empire and feudalism emerged.

    Description of feudalism:

    • Powerful nobles gave their followers land grants in exchange for military services
    • Peasants became serfs to work the lands in exchange for a place to live
    • Fragmented political sovereignty lead to growth of free cities
    • Growth lead to commodity production and formation of medieval craft guilds
    • Use of serfs and craftsmen drove the development of labor-saving technology

    Outbreak of plague led to a labor shortage and the move away from feudalism.

    Description of capitalism:

    • Power based on amount of land owned was challenged by merchants
    • Power was centralized in monarchies, which were suspicious of feudal barons
    • Emergence of medieval technologies in military helped strengthen the monarchs
    • Monarchs introduced law, taxation, and unified markets

    The rediscovery of Greek and Roman culture, and the discovery of America brought new ideas and knowledge to Europe.

    Description of Renaissance period:

    • Artists embraced new humanistic perspectives
    • Centers were established for learning of law and medicine
    • Leonardo da Vinci was the best example of a Renaissance man

    #The Scientific Revolution

    At the end of the 16th Century, observation and experiment paved the way to a new view of the natural world. Galileo developed the telescope and concluded that the Earth resolved around the Sun.

    Francis Bacon, an enthusiast for industrial science, used inductive approach to draw conclusions from data. Rene Descartes emphasized deductive approach through mathematics.

    The main difference between inductive and deductive reasoning is that inductive reasoning aims at developing a theory while deductive reasoning aims at testing an existing theory.

    Description of revolution:

    • Renewed interest in the practical orientation of knowledge
    • Knowledge is accessible by all and less controlled by the church
    • Knowledge became a means of controlling nature (Newton’s Laws)
    • Development of ships fitted with navigational aids and firearms
    • Ships allowed for the exploration and conquering of foreign lands

    #The Industrial Revolution

    By the early 18th Century, merchants gained control of commodity production.

    Description of revolution:

    • Emphasis on individual freedom to invest and trade as one sees fit
    • Plunder from colonies helped finance the European industrial takeoff
    • Continuous exploitation of colonial resources sustained industrial growth
    • Smelting of metals for raw materials to make machines and structures
    • Use of machines to replace human labor in production processes

    Description of the machine age:

    • Mechanization of tasks led to widespread use of machines
    • Laborers reduced to machine minders at factories and mills
    • Population growth provided labor and consumers for new industries and markets

    The steam engine formed the basis for the mechanical engineering profession.

    Description of steam engine:

    • Thomas Savery invented and patented the primitive steam engine
    • James Watt improved on the efficiency of the engine to produce rotary motion
    • Richard Trevithick built the first steam-powered locomotive (known as “Rocket”)
    • Railway enable the quick, reliable, and cheap movement of people and goods
    • Railway opened up broader markets for goods and services

    The Crystal Palace Exhibition in London was a celebration of British engineers and engineering, as it marked the high point of British industrial ascendency.

    Settlers in the US were extremely utilitarian — an ethical theory that determines right from wrong by focusing on outcomes.

    Description of US industrialization:

    • Saw knowledge as a commodity to satisfy needs
    • Designed equipment that reduced their independence on labor
    • Developed effective grinding and milling machines for production processes
    • Manufacture of highly standardized products made from interchangeable parts
    • Recognized the need to encourage domestic manufacture — military equipment

    Tensions between slave-owning South and industrialized North led to the American Civil War, with both sides fighting with “modern” weapons. After the civil war, the railroads united the nations to move ahead with greater momentum.

    Britain’s loss of industrial leadership to the US reflected complacency in society. Pressures to adopt values and interests of industrialization were resisted by the ruling elite, hence Britain did not change radically.

    Description of Britain’s loss:

    • Emphasized on pursuit of scientific knowledge
    • Neglected and undervalued commercial application
    • Dominance of steam engine stifled development of other forms of power
    • France and Germany developed internal combustion engine without competition
    • US capitalized on the opportunity for mass production of standard products

      The innovative development of tools and their use to produce standardized interchangeable parts paved the way for Henry Ford to use the moving assembly line technique to produce automobiles.

    Manufacturing replaced agriculture as the leading source of economic growth in US. The manufacturing industry shifted to producing products for consumers to producing products for producers.

    #Engineering Ethics

    Description of profession:

    • Claims special knowledge or expertise
    • Warrants special privileges and trust from the public
    • Enjoy a high social status, regard and esteem conferred upon them by society

    Description of engineers:

    • Build things and make processes in order to improve life
    • Reflect constantly on the products and processes that we make
    • Need to be socially responsible — taking into consideration the needs of society
    • Social responsibility requires professional responsibility

    Ethical responsibilities are placed on engineers because they provide services or make judgements that are not easily understood by the general public. Clients must trust an engineer’s professional judgement.

    #Rules of Behavior

    Ethical problems are open-ended and there is no formula for a solution. These problems rarely have a common correct answer that everyone shares.

    Etiquette:

    • Rules of acceptable personal behavior and courtesy when interacting with others
    • Violation does result in legal consequences but can hurt professionalism
    • E.g. table manners, dress codes, seating arrangements

    Law:

    • System of rules and punishments defined and established by society
    • Maintains a safe and orderly social environment
    • Violations carry consequences

    Morality:

    • Personal rules of right and wrong behavior
    • Derived from a person’s upbringing, religious beliefs, and societal influences

    Ethics:

    • The study of the morality of human actions
    • System of rules defining moral behavior for a particular society
    • Requires strength of character to behave in an ethical manner
    • Professional ethics guide the conduct of a professional

    #Singapore Professional Engineers Board

    A professional engineer (PE), is governed by the PE Act, the PE Rules, as well as the Code of Professional Conduct and Ethics.

    Professional Engineers Act:

    • Provide for the registration of professional engineers
    • Regulate the qualifications and conduct of professional engineers
    • Regulate the corporations which supply professional engineering services

      One must be a registered PE to engage in PE work, and supply or offer to supply PE services. Else, only licensed corporations can supply or offer to supply PE services.

    #National Society of Professional Engineers (US)

    As members of the engineering profession, engineers are expected to exhibit the highest standards of honesty and integrity.

    #Code of Ethics

    Code of ethics:

    • Hold paramount the safety, health and welfare of the public
    • Issue statements in an objective and truthful manner
    • Perform services in one’s area of competency
    • Act as a faithful agent and trustee
    • Avoid deceptive acts
    • Conduct themselves honorably, responsibly, ethically, and lawfully

    The code of ethics serves to protect each professional from certain pressures by making it reasonably likely that most other members of the profession will resist those pressures too. The code prevents the engineer from acting to serve their interests or employer’s interest.

    Area of competence:

    • Offer services, advice or undertake assignments only in their areas of competence
    • Exercise care and communicate clearly when accepting or interpreting assignments
    • Responsible in calling for and obtaining the services of a specialist if required

    A PE should therefore not sign documents unless he has actually prepared them, or they are prepared under his direct supervision. This ensures that a PE, acting as a qualified person, can be relied upon for professional integrity and commitment to protect the public interest and safety.

    E.g. The architect’s plans for the Hotel New World building were drawn up by an unqualified draftsman. Collapse of the building killed 33 people.

    Faithful agent and trustee:

    • Disclose all known and potential conflicts of interest that could influence or appear to influence their judgement or the quality of their services
    • Shall reject bribery in all its forms

    Avoid deceptive acts:

    • Shall not falsify qualifications or misrepresent their associates’ qualifications
    • Shall not offer, give or receive any contribution to influence award of a contract
    • Never attempt to deliberately deceive the client

    E.g. Volkswagen installed software to falsify their car’s emission, lying to both the US government and to consumers.

    #Whistleblowing

    Whistleblowing is the act of revealing information about activity within an organization that is deemed illegal, immoral, fraudulent, or unsafe. It is always the last resort.

    Problems that warrant whistleblowing:

    • Threat to public safety or environment
    • Injustices to employees
    • Unethical policies
    • Criminal behavior
    • Incompetence

    Internal whistleblowing is when an individual makes disclosures within the organization.

    Protected disclosures:

    • Qualifying disclosure made to employer or through internal procedures
    • Qualifying disclosure about an exceptionally serious failure
    • Disclosure about health and safety matters

    It is morally permissible to engage in external whistleblowing concerning safety.

    Safety concerns:

    • Harm done to public is serious and considerable
    • Superiors do not take action even after raising concern
    • Must have documented evidence to convince a reasonable, impartial observer

    #Case Study: Ford Pinto

    In 1978, a Ford Pinto was hit from behind on a highway. The impact of the collision caused the fuel tank to rupture and burst into flames, killing the passengers.

    Description of accident:

    • Ford was charged for the death of the passengers and found to be negligent
    • Gas tank design was flawed and not inline with accepted standards
    • Engineers were aware of the dangers with the design
    • Management concerned with getting the car to market at a competitive price
    • Memo indicated that cost of projected lawsuits was less than making the car safe

      Projected lawsuits would cost them 49.5mil whereas the suggested changes would cost about $11 per car, or 137mil in total.

    Ethical dilemma of engineers:

    • Produce the car at a competitive price and bring it to market quickly
    • Attempt to balance their duty to their employer

    #Case Study: Therac-25

    The Therac-25 was a computer-controlled radiation therapy machine.

    Description of accident:

    • Programming errors led to patients being given overdoses of radiation
    • Accident highlighted the dangers of software control of safety-critical systems
    • Overconfidence and lack of proper due diligence to resolve reported software bugs

      Previous models had hardware interlocks to prevent overdosing, but the Therac-25 had removed them, and instead relied on software checks for safety.

    #Socratic Method

    Description of method:

    • Form of cooperative argumentative dialogue between individuals
    • Based on asking and answering questions to stimulate critical thinking
    • Also draws out ideas and underlying presumptions

    The Socratic method works to clarify a person’s own beliefs by evaluating their worth and clarifying the concepts of good and justice. A person can find the answer to any problem by breaking it down into a series of questions and then finding the answers.

    An engineer can use the Socratic method to analyze the situation to derive ethical judgement based on the inner self.

By communicating with one another do we understand each other’s perspective on the issue. If all else fails, external whistleblowing should be used as a last resort.

    #Case Study: Bhopal Disaster

    Background information:

    • Located in the city center of Bhopal
    • Plant classified as non-hazardous by government
    • Manufactured pesticide that increases crop yields
    • Brought economic growth to the population in the surrounding areas

    Geographic advantages:

    • Central location in India
    • Reliable source of water from lake
    • Sufficient electricity and labor to sustain the plant operations
    • Access to railway system makes transportation of pesticides efficient

    #Breakdown of Disaster

    Timeline of disaster:

    • Pesticide storage tank developed a water leak due to improper closing of a valve
    • Reaction of the water and pesticide mixture is a exothermic reaction
    • Temperature of mixture increases and causes pesticide to vaporize
    • Vaporization of pesticide leads to build-up of pressure in the tank
    • Pressure relief valve opened and allowed vapor pesticide to escape

    Swiss-Cheese model:

    • Malfunction of refrigeration unit months before accident allowed overheating
    • Overtemperature alarm was improperly set and provided no early warnings
    • Malfunction of flare tower allowed escape of toxic vapors into the atmosphere
    • Scrubber used to neutralize toxic vapors not activated until vapors had escaped

    In summary, the disaster occurred as the decisions made by the company were profit driven. More importance was placed on cost saving — not fixing the refrigeration unit, flare tower, and scrubber, rather than safety of the staff and public.

    #Responsibility of Parties

    Parties to be blamed:

    • Management of the plant was negligent in maintenance
    • Inadequate plan for notifying and evacuating surrounding population
    • Lax safety standards and lack of policies set by the Indian government
    • Company failed to adequately train and supervise its Indian employers in the maintenance and safety procedures that are taken for granted

    Consequences of disaster:

    • Death of over 10000 people and many more injured
    • Compensation to the victims of the accident
    • Long-term health effects from exposure to toxic vapors

    #Case Study: Challenger Disaster

    The space shuttle was designed to be a reusable launch vehicle. The boosters were made by joining multiple individual cylinders together and the joints were sealed with O-rings.

    #Breakdown of Disaster

    Timeline of disaster:

    • Cold weather before the launch affected sealing of the booster joints
    • Failure of O-rings allowed hot gases from combustion to escape out the joint
    • Leak expanded and created a stream of flame that broke the booster struts
    • Booster detached from the strut and and crashed into the external fuel tank
    • Fuel tank exploded and destroyed the launch vehicle

    Swiss-Cheese model:

    • Congress was rushing NASA to speed up the launch of the shuttle
    • US wanted to be the first to announce the first teacher in space
    • Higher management disregarded engineers’ opposition to the launch

      The engineers tried convincing higher management of the consequences of improper sealing by the O-rings. Before the accident, the engineers had informed their managers of the inadequate design of the O-rings but were ignored.

    #Responsibility of Parties

    The engineers had no power, no resources, and no management support in their proposal of redesigning the O-rings. The lead engineer had little influence over the decisions of management, and was unable to convince them to delay the launch.

    The engineers could have chosen to carry out external whistleblowing.

    #Case Study: Nicoll Highway Collapse

    The “cut and cover” method was used to construct the underground tunnels between the Nicoll Highway and Boulevard stations. This method involves excavating a large hole. which is then covered by a concrete deck.

    #Breakdown of Disaster

    Timeline of disaster:

    • Large cavity is progressively excavated from ground level to tunnel depth
    • Steel struts attached to bars on the walls were used to reinforce the tunnel wall
    • One of the strut in the lower levels failed and allowed the tunnel wall to cave in
    • Cave in led to the collapse of the highway

    Swiss-Cheese model:

    • Underestimation of strut loads in the lower levels
    • Overestimation of wall bending moments in the lower levels
    • Lack of proper soil analysis led to buckling of the tunnel walls when strut failed

    In summary, the eventual failure of a strut in the lower level, together with the inability of the tunnel wall to redistribute the load resulted in the cave in and the collapse.

    #Responsibility of Parties

    Parties to be blamed:

    • Engineer that carried out soil analysis did not have experience in that field
    • Lack of experience resulted in inaccurate date that was used in the construction
    • Company also had not provided critical instrument readings to the authorities

    The structural safety of temporary works is as important as that of permanent works. One must not be complacent in monitoring the stability of a work site no matter how unlikely the event of a collapse is.

    #Engineering Practices (Singapore)

    #Workplace Safety and Health Act (WSHA)

    Goals of WHSA (with effect from 1 Mar 2006):

    • Reducing risk at source by requiring all stakeholders to eliminate or minimize risks
    • Instilling greater industry ownership of OSH — employers responsible for safety
    • Prevent accidents through higher fines for poor safety and health management

      The WHSA focuses on the outcomes of safety and health systems, rather than whether a company complies with the laws. It facilitates effective enforcement through the issuance of remedial orders.

    Changes between WHSA and the Factories Act:

    • WHSA specifies liabilities for a range of persons at the workplace (being proactive)
    • WHSA focuses on effective management of workplace safety and health
    • WHSA stipulates greater penalties for compromising safety and health

      The Factories Act only imposed responsibility on the occupier of the factory, whereby higher management without offices in the factory was free from those responsibilities.

    The WHSA covers all workplaces except the following:

    • Singapore Armed Forces (SAF)
    • Singapore Police Force (SPF)
    • Singapore Prison Service
    • Internal Security Department
    • Central Narcotics Bureau (CNB)
    • Singapore Civil Defense Force (SCDF)
    • Immigration and Checkpoint Authority (ICA)

    #Occupational Safety

    Occupational Safety and Health standards (Mar 2005):

    • Key reforms to improve OSH standards was announced
    • Reforms based on the premise that accidents can only be prevented if all involved in the workplace take personal responsibility for achieving higher safety standards

      The new OSH standards aimed at reducing deaths at workplaces by one-third in 5 years, and by half within a decade or sooner.

    Risk management regulations (with effect from 1 Sep 2006):

    • Employers/self-employed responsible for identifying safety and health hazards
    • To take appropriate actions to eliminate or reduce the risks associated with hazards
    • First-time offender liable to a fine not more than $10000
    • Repeat offender liable to a fine up to $20000, 6 months imprisonment, or both

    #Reduce Risk at Source

    Creating a flexible and practical regulatory framework by:

    • Focusing on reinforcing the concept of individual ownership
    • Placing responsibility for workplace safety on all stakeholders
    • Incident reporting regulations (enacted on 1 Mar 2006)
    • Work Injury Compensation Act (with effect from 1 Apr 2006)
    • Risk management regulations (with effect from 1 Sep 2006)

    Delivering practical assistance by:

    • Approved Code of Practice (ACOP) — set out preferred work practices or standards that industry practitioners are expected to adopt when managing workplace risks
    • Guidelines — provide guidance on issues and non-technical advice on issues
    • Technical advisories — provide detailed guidance on issues that are technical in nature or where engineering and scientific methods are required

    Reducing risk by implementing a robust inspection framework.

    #Instilling Greater Ownership

    Creating a progressive and pervasive WSH culture:

    • Reactive — Top down management and direction of WSH initiatives
    • Participative — Stakeholder participation and engagement in WSH assurance
    • Proactive — Routine overview of WSH to address safety and health issues
    • Progressive — Stakeholders motivated to take ownership and spread WSH culture
    • Excellence — Setting WSH as one of the company’s values

    Elevating awareness of WSH among new sectors by:

    • Engagement program to engage employers and employees of new sectors
    • Campaigns to grasp the attention of the public at a more personal level
    • Performances in companies to promote WSH initiatives

    The WHSA requires every person at the workplace to take reasonably practicable steps to ensure the safety and health of every workplace and worker. An action is considered to be practicable when it is capable of being done.

    Factors in determining reasonable steps:

    • Severity of any injury or harm to health that may occur
    • Possibility of that injury or harm occurring
    • How much is known about the hazard and the ways to eliminate it
    • Availability, suitability, and cost of the safeguards

    #Challenges in Safety and Health

    The WSH Institute was set up as a leading institute for WSH knowledge and innovations with the goal of enhancing WSH through knowledge, innovations and solutions.

    Creating and enhancing knowledge of stakeholders:

    • MOM — to make informed policies and strategies based on evidence
    • Companies — to equip them with solutions and information to address issues faced
    • Leaders — to be effective in managing and influencing workplace safety and health

    #Risk Assessment

    A risk is any uncertainty about future events that impact an organization’s ability to achieve its objectives. Risk is measured in terms of its impact and the likelihood that it will happen.

    Risk assessment is divided into three sub-tasks:

    • Risk determination — probability of risky events occurring and the likely consequences
    • Risk evaluation — consists of risk avoidance and risk outcome
    • Risk measurement — involves complex quantitative measures of risk

    #Risk Categories

    #Total Risk

    Total risk is the sum of identified and unidentified risks with identified risks making up a larger proportion of the sum.

    Identified risk:

    • Risk that has been determined to exist using analytical tools
    • Amount of risk is affected by the time and costs of analysis efforts, the quality of the risk management program, and the state of the technology involved

    Unidentified risk:

    • Risk that has not yet been identified
    • Investigations may reveal previously unidentified risks

    #Residual Risk

    Residual risk is the portion of total risk that remains after efforts to reduce risk are done.

    Acceptable risk:

    • Part of identified risk that is allowed to persist after employing efforts to reduce risk
    • Risk is determined acceptable when further efforts to reduce it would reduce the efficiency of an operation, or when the point of diminishing returns is reached

    Unacceptable risk:

    • Portion of unidentified risk that cannot be tolerated
    • Must either be eliminated or controlled

    #Hidden Risk

    Organizational factors associated with human resource:

    • Staff morale
    • Inadequate manpower
    • Insufficient training leading to incompetence
    • Industrial culture not conducive to promoting safety
    • Reporting culture
    • Just culture

    Just culture is where employees are not blamed for honest errors, but are held accountable for violations and negligence. It creates an atmosphere of trust in which people are encouraged, and even rewarded, for reporting essential safety-related issues.

    #Types of Risk

    #Societal Risk

    • Real risk to an individual — determined on the basis of future circumstances
    • Statistical risk — based on past events
    • Predicted risk — from simulated models
    • Perceived risk — risk felt by an individual (e.g. fear of flying)

    #Voluntary and Involuntary Risk

    • Risk of injury/death when travelling by air represents voluntary risk
    • Living near a nuclear power plant represents involuntary risk

    #Risk Assessment and Mitigation Processes

    Risk analysis to assess potential harm or damage:

    • Probability — probability of the hazard causing adverse consequences
    • Severity — severity of the potential adverse consequences
    • Exposure — rate of exposure to the hazard

    Risk assessment and mitigation processes analyze and eliminate/mitigate to an acceptable level of risk to minimize effect of risk on the capability of an organization.

    #Risk Management

    Description of risk management:

    • Eradication/minimization of adverse effects of risks the organization is exposed to
    • Provides a reasoned and repeatable process that reduces reliance on intuition
    • Includes control and monitoring of risks, and communicating those risks
    • Requires a clear understanding of what constitutes unnecessary risk
    • Benefits are realized through prudent risk-taking

      Accepting risk is a function of both risk assessment and management, and is often complex with many variables to consider, some of which has high uncertainties.

    Stages of risk management:

    • Identifying hazards
    • Assessing their implications
    • Deciding on a course of action
    • Evaluating the results

    #Identification of Hazards

    Hazards can only be controlled if they are identified.

    Areas to look out for hazards:

    • Equipment — equipment can malfunction and cause harm
    • Procedures — improper procedures may result in harm
    • Chemicals — improper handling of chemicals may result in harm
    • People — not focused on their work which may result in mistakes
    • Environment — dangerous work environment introduces hazards

    Identifying risks:

    • Stop and look
    • Think through the task
    • Identify hazards
    • Control and communicate
    • Do the activity safely

    #Assessment of Implications

    Severity of injury:

    • First aid treatment
    • Hospitalization
    • Permanent damage
    • Acute or chronic effects
    • Lab acquired infection

    Property damage:

    • Broken glassware
    • Damaged equipment/furniture
    • Damaged building

    Environmental damage:

    • Release of toxic chemicals into the air
    • Discharge of waste materials illegally
    • Contamination of water

    #Evaluation of Risk

    Minor risk level:

    • No injury or light injury requiring only first aid treatment (human impact)
    • May not cause infections, but if it does, is unlikely to spread (biological impact)
    • Reversible environmental damage
    • Property damage up to $5000

    Moderate risk level:

    • Injury leading to hospitalization or temporary disability (human impact)
    • Can cause severe health issues and is infectious but treatable (biological impact)
    • Reversible environmental damage but may take years
    • Property damage up to $50k

    Major risk level:

    • Death, permanent disability or life threatening disease (human impact)
    • Lethal infection with no treatment available (biological impact)
    • Irreversible environmental damage
    • Property damage more than $50k

    Probabilities of risk occurring:

    • Remote — may occur but unlikely (once in 5 years)
    • Possible — probable (once in a year)
    • Frequent — most likely will occur (once or more in a month)

    Risk score of less than 3:

    • Low risk, and acceptable
    • No additional risk control measures required
    • Continue to monitor and ensure risk does not increase to a higher level

    Risk score of 3-4:

    • Medium risk, and moderately acceptable
    • Acceptable to carry out work activity
    • Tasks need to be reviewed to reduce risk level as low as reasonably practicable

    Risk score of more than 4:

    • High risk, and not acceptable
    • Tasks must not be carried out until risk level is reduced to at least medium
    • Measures should focus on elimination, substitution and engineering controls
    • Immediate management intervention required to ensure risk is reduced to medium

    #Risk Control

    Types of control measures:

    • Elimination — eliminate the hazard from the workplace
    • Substitution — substitute a hazardous substance or process with a less hazardous one
    • Engineering controls
    • Administrative controls

    Engineering controls:

    • Consists of substitution, isolation, ventilation, and equipment modification
    • Focuses on the source of the hazard

      The concept behind the controls is as much as possible, the work environment and the job itself should be designed to eliminate the hazards or reduce exposure to hazards.

    Administrative controls:

    • Changing of work habits, improving sanitation and hygiene practices
    • Changing the way a task is carried out by an employee

    Additional controls for risk rated at more than 3:

    • Reduce the risk to as low as reasonably practicable
    • Responsible person to implement the identified control measures
    • Update the risk assessment upon completion of the additional control measures

    #Sustainable Issues and Human Factors

    Sustainability is defined as the ability to maintain at a certain rate or level of consumption to avoid the depletion of natural resources in order to maintain an ecological balance.

    Sustainability encompasses both the economic, environment, and social elements.

    Description of mental model:

    • An explanation of the thinking process about how something works in the real world
    • Can help shape behavior and set an approach to solving problems and doing tasks

    Example of a mental model:

    • Thoughts — thinking about going to a class
    • Actions — attending the class
    • Results — finding the class useful/not useful

      If class is not useful, you don’t attend the next class.

    #Ladder of Inference

    By using the Ladder of Inference, we can learn to get back to the facts and use our beliefs and experiences to positively effect outcomes, rather than allowing them to narrow our field of judgement.

    With a new sense of reasoning and perhaps a wider field of data and more associated assumptions, one can go forward step-by-step, up the rungs of the ladder again.

    • Recreate ourselves
    • Be able to do things we were never able to do before
    • Re-perceive the world and our relationship to it
    • Extend our capacity to create; to be part of the generative process of life

    Shared Vision:

    • A vison that many people are committed to and reflects their personal vision
    • Helps to establish primary goals and guides the team when stresses develop
    • About building a practice of unearthing shared visions of the future that foster genuine commitment and enrollment rather than compliance

    Personal Mastery:

    • Organizations learn through only individuals who learn
    • Organization learning cannot occur without individual learning

    #Iceberg Analogy

    Problems are best not solved on the level where they appear to occur but from a higher viewpoint.

    The causes of a problem and the problem itself are usually hidden below the surface. What we perceive of the problem may be us reacting to a symptom of the problem, which does not solve the problem.

    The basis of systems thinking is the holistic approach of seeing the system as a whole, rather than the individual events.

    Iceberg analogy:

    • An issue is presented to use in the form of events or patterns (symptoms)
    • Get to root of issue by uncovering the underlying structures which cause the events

    Events:

    • An occurrence at some moment in time
    • We are usually concerned with the event rather than their causes
    • Instead of reacting to events, we must anticipate and shape them
    • Solutions that address events are short-lived as they do not address the fundamental structure that caused the event

    Patterns:

    • Changes in events over time
    • Allows us to understand the systematic structure that drives the pattern
    • Allows us to see how a series of events are inter-related and what caused them

    Structure:

    • The way system components are inter-related (organization of a system)
    • A system’s structure gives rise to events and patterns (symptoms)
    • Holds the key to lasting change as actions taken influence the future

    #The Blame Game

    Description of blame game:

    • OK — Beginning or neutral state
    • Injured — Contributes to our stress
    • Blaming — Finds someone to blame
    • Vengeful — Revengeful actions

    • Causes known — Identify causes
    • Loss mitigated — Take preventions

    An underlying problem generates symptoms that demand attention. However, the underlying problem is obscure or costly to confront. Thus, people shift the burden to
    other solutions that address the symptoms instead of the root cause of the problem.

    #Case Study: MRT Accident

    On the 22 Mar 2016, two maintenance staff was killed on the tracks near Pasir Ris station.

    Swiss-Cheese Model:

    • Failure to set the speed limit on the affected track sector to 0km/h
    • Failure to deploy lookouts for approaching trains and provide early warnings
    • Track access management did not ensure that the proper procedures were carried out

    In conclusion, while existing safety protection mechanisms are adequate, and current operating procedures continue to be relevant and applicable, these can be improved for greater clarity and ease of ground implementation.

    #Human Factors

    Human factors is the study of human capabilities and limitations in the workplace.

    Purpose of human factor training:

    • Enhance individual awareness of individual and organizational human factor issues
    • Acquire skills, such as communication, task management, situational awareness, etc.
    • Create a positive impact on the safety and efficiency of maintenance operations
    • Encourage a positive attitude towards safety and discourage unsafe practices

    Types of human factors:

    • Physical limitations of the human body
    • Psychology (state of the human mind)
    • Workplace design
    • Environmental conditions
    • Human-machine interface

    #Error Chain

    An accident could be avoided if things were done differently. This involves a series of human factor problems which form an error chain. By breaking one link of the chain, the accident does not happen.

    For example, aircraft maintenance crew could identify where the vulnerable areas might be within the maintenance link, which should prevent the error chain from reaching a catastrophic conclusion.

    #Human Error

    Human failures can be categorized into intended and unintended actions.

    Description of mistakes:

    • A specific type of error brought about by a faulty plan
    • Person did what he intended to do but it did not work

    Description of lapses:

    • Missed actions or omissions (memory failure)
    • Omitted items in a checklist, forgetting to do something
    • E.g. Missing out critical steps in emergency procedures when under stress

    Description of slips:

    • Actions not carried out as intended or planned
    • E.g. flight crew too fixated on warning light and not realize they’re going to crash

    #Economic Development Strategies (Singapore)

    The economic history of Singapore consists of 5 phases.

    #Take-Off Phase (1965 - 1984)

    Singapore relies on external help to grow its own economy.

    List of strategies:

    • Economic fundamentals of prudent public finances
    • Cooperative industrial relations
    • Sound monetary policies
    • Market-based strategies
    • Outward orientation

    Description of phase:

    • Shift away from import-substitution in favor of export-led industrialization
    • Attract global multinational corporations to achieve industrial growth

      Import-substitution is the blocking of imported goods to increase the demand for domestically produced goods to help the economy.

    #Transition from 3rd World to 1st World Phase (1985 - 2010)

    Singapore looks towards growing its own economy through local companies.

    Rise of modern services:

    • Enhance wage flexibility
    • Promote innovation, enterprise and entrepreneurship
    • Tap into regional markets for trade and outward investment
    • Lessening of government regulations and restrictions in various service sectors

    #Transition Towards Productivity-Led Phase (2011 - 2025)

    Singapore strives towards increasing productivity to grow its economy.

    Description of phase:

    • Overcoming resource constraints through a restructuring of economy
    • Significant step-down in real GDP growth to 3.6% per annum
    • Economy matures by 2025 where economic growth is due to productivity growth

    #Regional Integration and Offshore Economy Phase (2026 - 2040)

    Setting up of economic zones outside of Singapore to grow.

    Description of phase:

    • High-end manufacturing and modern services benefit from trade with others
    • Setting up of Iskandar-Singapore Economic Zone (ISEZ) in 2028
    • Setting up of ASEAN Free Economic Zone in 2020

    #Climate Change Mitigation and Adaptation Phase (2040 - 2065)

    Singapore strives towards a green industry to grow.

    Description of phase:

    • Adapting to climate change as important as mitigating it
    • Introduction of carbon tax to reduce carbon intensity in economic activities
    • Green industry to become the largest contributor to gross national income

    #Research, Development and Innovation

    Singapore’s future depends on research, innovation and technological developments.

    Importance of R&D:

    • Increase capacity to undertake R&D so as to attract high value-added activities
    • Attract multinational corporations and encourage them to set up shop here
    • Enhance competitiveness of local enterprises to allow them to compete globally
    • Capabilities rely on superior technology, innovation and human resources
    • R&D support the upgrading and restructuring of existing industry and business clusters
    • Prominent industry and business clusters to serve as anchors for economic growth

    #Challenges in Research and Development

    Conducive industry to undertake R&D:

    • Cost of doing R&D have increase along with general costs of doing business
    • Conducive environment must be created to encourage more R&D projects
    • Co-investment in corporate R&D centers through industry R&D grants
    • Development and funding of manpower related grants
    • Fostering of economically relevant R&D in universities
    • Promoting a culture of innovation and entrepreneurship
    • Establishment of a network of international collaborations

    Strengthening technological capabilities:

    • Singapore’s technological capability lags behind other countries (Taiwan and Korea)
    • Bridge this gap by continually building up its capability via government partnership
    • Strategic development and funding of 15 research institutes and centers
    • Biomedical and chemical sciences, electronics, infocomm, manufacturing, etc.
    • R&D grant to promote and coordinate university and industry R&D projects
    • Acquiring relevant technologies from other countries to upgrade own industry
    • Technology capability planning — focus finite resources to avoid costly overlaps

    Fostering innovation and commercialization:

    • Commercialization is the barrier technology development has to overcome
    • Ensure investments in R&D will reap the corresponding economic benefits
    • Integrated one-stop innovation and commercialization centers
    • Environment for high-tech industries and start-ups to flourish

    Meeting demand for manpower:

    • Critical factor in successfully developing and sustaining high level of R&D
    • Challenging due to size of Singapore and our small population base
    • Groom more local researchers to increase the pool of skilled manpower
    • Attract foreign manpower through scholarships and special programs
    • Set up overseas R&D programs and centers to augment our talent base
    • Encourage prominent R&D centers to set up branches in Singapore

    #Population and Economy

    Singapore is located in a fast growing region.

    Concerns related to population:

    • Increase in economic opportunities result in increase in competition
    • Calibrate policies carefully in order to maintain competitiveness
    • Grow at a sustainable rate so everyone can enjoy improving quality of life
    • Increasingly difficult to find sufficient manpower for low-skill jobs

    Possible solutions:

    • Raise productivity through restructuring and workforce training
    • Encourage residents to enter and stay in the workforce longer
    • Control the amount of immigrants and foreign workers

    #Knowledge-Based Economy

    Knowledge and ingenuity will enable Singapore to overcome the limitations of its geography and population size.

    Description of economy:

    • Strengthen and broaden overall education of its people
    • Nurture the spirit of entrepreneurship
    • Learn to think outside of the box
    • Life-long learning to remain relevant
