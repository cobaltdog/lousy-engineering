    Physical Quantities and Vectors

    Physical quantities are based on experimental observations and measurements.

    #Standards and Units

    Criteria for measurement standards:

    • Readily accessible
    • Can be measured reliably
    • Must not change with time
    • Must yield the same results no matter who or where it is measured

    Measurement is a process where a physical quantity is compared with a calibrated scale.

    Accuracy refers to how close the measured quantity is to the “true” value.

    Precision of an instrument refers to the limit of its sensitivity.

    #Derived Quantities

    • Derived quantities are products or quotients of base quantities
    • Derived quantity has a derived unit which is the product or quotient of base units
    • Dimension of a derived quantity is the product or quotient of base quantity dimensions

    #Dimensionless Quantities

    Dimensionless quantities are ratios of physical quantities with the same dimensions. Examples include, relative density, relative humidity, efficiency, angle in radians, etc.

    #Dimension Analysis

    Dimension analysis cannot be used to find missing or unknown dimensionless factors.

    Description of analysis:

    • Checks if a relationship or equation is correct
    • Determines the unit of a physical quantity
    • Predict relationships between physical quantities

    When an equation is homogenous, the left side and right side have the same dimension.
    A dimensionally correct equation may be physically incorrect. Quantities of different dimensions cannot be added or subtracted.

    #Scalars and Vectors

    #Scalars

    A scalar is a physical quantity that is completely described by its magnitude.

    #Vectors

    A vector is a physical quantity that is described by its magnitude and direction. Two vectors are equal if they have the same magnitude, are parallel, and in the same direction.

    #Addition and Subtraction

    The resultant of adding two vectors can be obtained by placing the tail of the second vector at the head of the first vector, and drawing a line joining the tail of the first vector to the head of the second vector.

    #Resolution of Vectors

    A vector in 2D can be resolved into two mutually perpendicular components. The components of a vector can be either positive or negative, depending on which quadrant of the circle it is in.

    A vector in 3D can be resolved into three mutually perpendicular components.

    A vector can also be represented as a column matrix containing its x, y, and z components. The sum of two vectors can be found by adding their respective components and then adding the resultants of its x, y, and z components.

    #Unit Vectors

    A unit vector has a magnitude of 1. It is used to indicate the direction of a vector.

    Any vector can be written as A=Ax​i^+Ay​j^​+Az​k^.

    #Dot (Scalar) Product

    The dot product of two vectors is defined as C=A⋅B=∣A∣∣B∣cosθ.

    A⋅B can also be expressed as Ax​Bx​+Ay​By​+Az​Bz​.

    A(Bcosθ) can be described as the magnitude of A multiplied by the component of B in the direction of A and vice versa, whereby A⋅B=B⋅A.

    If a constant force F causes an object to undergo a displacement s, the work done, which is a scalar, is given by W=F⋅scosθ. Work can be zero, positive, or negative depending on the value of θ.

    #Cross (Vector) Product

    The cross product of two vectors is defined as C=A×B. The direction of C is perpendicular to both A and B, and can be obtained using the right-hand-rule.

    The magnitude of C is given by C=ABsinθ, 0°≤θ≤180°. The vector product of two parallel or antiparallel (opposite directions) vectors is always zero.

    The vector τ=r×F is known as torque where r is the radius vector and F is the force vector. The magnitude of τ is known as the moment of the force and the direction is given by the right hand grip rule.
