    Motion in Two or Three Dimensions

    Kinematics is the part of mechanics that describe the motion of objects.

    #Acceleration Vector

    Since we treat velocity as a vector, acceleration will describe changes in the velocity magnitude and changes in the direction of velocity.

    💡
    ax​=dtdvx​​     ay​=dtdvy​​     az​=dtdvz​​    (Components of instantaneous acceleration)

    The parallel component a∥​ tells us about changes in the object’s speed, while the perpendicular component a⊥​ tells us about about changes in the object’s direction of motion.

    If there is only a∥​, the velocity change Δv during a small time interval Δt is in the same direction as a.

    If there is only a⊥​, the velocity change Δv is very nearly perpendicular to v1​, and so v1​ and v2​ will have different directions.

    Generally, the acceleration a has components both parallel and perpendicular to
    the velocity v. Then, the object’s velocity will change and its direction of motion
    will change so that it follows a curved path.

    #Example

    At point B:

    • Skier is moving in a straight line with increasing speed
    • Acceleration points downwards in the same direction as her velocity

    At point D:

    • Has parallel and perpendicular components of acceleration
    • Parallel acceleration vector points ahead of the normal as skier is speeding up

    At point E:

    • Skier’s instantaneous velocity is constant (velocity is maximum at this point)
    • Has only perpendicular component of acceleration

    At point F:

    • Has parallel and perpendicular components of acceleration
    • Parallel acceleration vector points behind the normal as skier is slowing down

    #Projectile Motion

    A projectile is any body that is given an initial velocity and then follows a path determined entirely by the effects of gravitational acceleration and air resistance. The path followed by a projectile is called its trajectory.

    Projectile motion is always confined to a vertical plane determined by the direction of the initial velocity. This is because acceleration due to gravity is purely vertical; gravity can’t accelerate the projectile sideways. Thus projectile motion is two-dimensional.

    vx​=v0x​

    x=x0​+v0x​t

    vy​=v0y​−gt

    y=y0​+v0y​t−21​gt2

    It’s usually simplest to take the
    initial position as the origin.

    #Motion in a Circle

    When an object moves along a curved path, the direction of its velocity changes. This means that the object must have a component of acceleration perpendicular to the path, even if its speed is constant.

    #Uniform Circular Motion

    A car rounding a curve with constant radius at constant speed, a satellite moving in a circular orbit, and an ice skater skating in a circle with constant speed are examples of uniform circular motion.

    B=(−rsinθ)i^+(−rcosθ)j^​

    B′=(rsinθ)i^+(rcosθ)j^​

    vB​=(vcosθ)i^+(−vsinθ)j^​

    vB′​=(vcosθ)i^+(vsinθ)j^​

    arad​=t(vB′​−vB​)​=t(2vsinθ)j^​​

    arad​=limθ→0​ rθv2sinθj^​​

    arad​=rv2​

    Acceleration in uniform circular motion is always directed towards the center of the circle, and is always perpendicular to the velocity vector v. It is sometimes called centripetal acceleration.

    #Relative Velocity

    In general, when two observers measure the velocity of a moving body, they get different results if one observer is moving relative to the other. The velocity seen by a particular
    observer is called the velocity relative to that observer.

    #Relative Velocity in One Dimension

    Each observer forms a frame of reference.

    💡
    vP/A​=vP/B​+vB/A​
    💡
    vA/B​=−vB/A​

    #Relative Velocity in Two Dimension

    rP/A​=rP/B​+vB/A​t

    💡
    vP/A​=vP/B​+vB/A​

    The acceleration of an object measured by an observer in one frame of reference is the same as that measured by any other observer moving with constant velocity relative to the first frame.

    dtdvP/A​​=dtdvP/B​​+dtdvB/A​​

    dtdvP/A​​=dtdvP/B​​+0

    aP/A​=aP/B​
