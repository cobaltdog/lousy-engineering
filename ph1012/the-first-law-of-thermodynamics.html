    The First Law of Thermodynamics

    The first law of thermodynamics is an extension of the principle of conservation of energy.

    #Thermodynamic Systems

    In general, a thermodynamic system is any collection of objects that is convenient to regard as a unit, and that may have the potential to exchange energy with its surroundings.

    #Signs for Heat and Work

    Energy relationships in any thermodynamic process can be described in terms of the quantity of heat Q added to the system and the work W done by the system.

    Sign convention for heat:

    • Positive Q — heat flow into the system
    • Negative Q — heat flow out of the system

    Sign convention for work:

    • Positive W — work done by the system against
      its surroundings (energy leaving the system)
    • Negative W — work done on the system by its surroundings (energy entering the system)

    Work done by an expanding gas is positive work as it corresponds to energy leaving the system. Work done during compression of a gas, in which work is done on the gas by its surroundings, is negative as it corresponds to energy entering the system.

    #Thermodynamic Processes

    #Work Done during Volume Changes

    A simple thermodynamic system is a quantity of gas enclosed by a movable piston.

    Consider the work done by the system during a volume change. When a gas expands, it pushes
    the piston outwards — positive work.

    If the cylinder has cross-sectional area A and the pressure exerted by the system at the piston face
    is P. The total force exerted by the system on the piston is F=PA.

    dW=F dx=PA dx=P dV

    💡
    W=∫Vi​Vf​​P dV

    If the pressure P remains constant while volume changes, the work done by the system is,

    💡
    W=P(Vf​−Vi​)

    #Internal Energy and the First Law

    Internal energy U of a system is tentatively defined as the sum of the kinetic energies of all of its constituent particles, plus the sum of all the potential energies of interaction among these particles.

    During a change of state in a system, the internal energy may change from an initial value Ui​ to a
    final value Uf​.

    ΔU=Uf​−Ui​

    When heat Q is added to a system, some of this added energy remains within the system, changing its internal energy by an amount ΔU; the remainder leaves the system again as the system does work W.

    💡
    ΔU=Q−W

    As Q and W may be positive, negative or zero, ΔU can be positive, negative or zero for different types of processes.

    While Q and W depend on the path, ΔU is independent of path. The change in internal energy of a system during any thermodynamic process depends only on the initial and final states, not on the path leading from one to another.

    #Example 1

    ΔUab​=Qab​−Wab​

    ΔUab​=150−0=150 J

    Wbd​=P(V2​−V1​)

    Wbd​=(8×104)[(5−2)×10−3]

    Wbd​=240 J

    Wabd​=0+240=240 J

    Qabd​=150+600=750 J

    ΔUabd​=Qabd​−Wabd​

    ΔUabd​=750−240=510 J

    ΔUacd​=ΔUabd​=510 J

    Wacd​=(3×104)[(5−2)×10−3]=90 J

    Qacd​=510+90=600 J

    #Example 2

    W=P(V2​−V1​)

    W=(1.013×105)[(1671−1)×10−6]

    W=169 J

    Q=mLV​=ρVLV​

    Q=1000(1×10−6)(2.256×106)

    Q=2256 J

    ΔU=Q−W

    ΔU=2256−169=2087 J

    #Internal Energy of an Ideal Gas

    When the partition is removed, the gas expands to fill the container. The gas does no work and there is no heat flow through the insulation.

    Both Q and W are zero and U is constant. If T is constant during a free expansion, even though both P and V change, U depends only on T.

    💡
    The internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on its temperature.

    #Types of Thermodynamic Processes

    #Adiabatic Process

    An adiabatic process is defined as one with no heat transfer into or out of the system.

    💡
    ΔU=−W

    When a system expands adiabatically, W is positive. Thus ΔU is negative and internal energy decreases. When a system is compressed adiabatically, W is negative and internal energy increases.

    #Isochoric Process

    An isochoric process is a constant-volume process. Thus, work done is zero.

    Q=ΔU

    💡
    Q=23​nRΔT=nCV​ΔT

    In an isochoric process, all the energy added as heat remains in the system as an increase in internal energy — heating a gas in a closed constant-volume container.

    #Isobaric Process

    An isobaric process is a constant-pressure process.

    W=P(Vf​−Vi​)=nR(Tf​−Ti​)

    ΔU=23​nR(Tf​−Ti​)

    Q=ΔU+W

    Q=nR(Tf​−Ti​)(23​+1)

    💡
    Q=25​nRΔT

    The above equation applies to monatomic gases.

    #Isothermal Process

    An isothermal process is a constant-temperature process.

    For a process to be isothermal, any heat flow into or out of the system must occur slowly enough that thermal equilibrium is maintained.

    #Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas

    The molar heat capacity of a gas under constant-volume conditions is CV​.

    The molar heat capacity for solids and liquids under constant-pressure conditions is CP​.

    For a given temperature increase, ΔU of an ideal gas has the same value no matter the process. Thus, the heat input for a isobaric process must be greater than that for a isochoric process as additional energy must be supplied to account for the work done.

    💡
    CP​>CV​

    #Relation between CP​ and CV​

    Consider the constant-volume process. We place n moles of ideal gas at temperature T
    in a constant-volume container and add heat.

    dQ=nCV​dT

    dU=nCV​dT

    Pressure increases but the gas does not work.

    Consider the constant-pressure process. We place the same gas in a cylinder with a piston that is allowed to move just enough to maintain constant pressure.

    dQ=nCP​dT

    dW=P dV=nR dT

    nCP​dT=dU+nRdT

    The internal energy change for the constant-pressure process is given by dU=nCV​dT as internal energy depends only on temperature. Thus, dU is the same for every kind of process with the same dT.

    nCP​dT=nCV​dT+nRdT

    💡
    CP​=CV​+R

    The relation between CP​ and CV​ is known as Mayer’s relation.

    #Ratio of Heat Capacities

    The dimensionless ratio of heat capacities γ is given by,

    💡
    γ=CV​CP​​

    #Example

    γ=CV​CP​​=CV​CV​+R​=1+CV​R​

    1.4=1+CV​8.314​

    CV​=20.785 J/mol⋅K

    As internal energy is dependent only on temperature,

    ΔU=nCV​ΔT

    ΔU=2500(20.785)(26−35)

    ΔU=−4.68×105 J

    #Applications of the First Law

    #Adiabatic Process for an Ideal Gas

    A process is approximately adiabatic if the system is well insulated or if the process takes place so quickly that there is not enough time for appreciable heat flow to occur.

    ΔU=−W

    nCV​dT=−P dV

    nCV​dT=−VnRT​ dV

    CV​dT+VRT​ dV=0

    TdT​+(γ−1)VdV​=0

    #Isothermal Expansion of an Ideal Gas

    The ideal gas law with T constant indicates that the equation of the curve is PV=Constant.

    W=∫Vi​Vf​​P dV=∫Vi​Vf​​VnRT​ dV

    W=nRT[lnV]Vi​Vf​​

    W=nRT[lnVf​−lnVi​]

    💡
    W=nRT ln(Vi​Vf​​)

    For an ideal gas, the change in internal energy is proportional to the change in temperature. Since there is no change in temperature, there is no change in internal energy. All the heat added into the system is used to do work.
