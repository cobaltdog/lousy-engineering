    Current and Resistance

    #Electric Current

    If a constant, steady electric field E is established inside a conductor, a charged particle inside the conducting material is subjected to a steady F.

    E causes a very slow net motion or drift of the moving charged particle in the direction of F. This motion is described in terms of the drift velocity vd​.

    Current is the rate at which a net charge flows through a cross-sectional area A.

    💡
    I=dtdQ​

    #Drift Velocity and Current Density

    Suppose there are n moving charged particles per unit volume — concentration of particles. The particles move with the same magnitude vd​.

    In a time interval dt, each particles moves a distance vd​ dt. The volume of the cylinder is Avd​ dt, and the number of particles within it is nAvd​ dt.

    If each particle has a charge q, the charge dQ that flows out of the end of the cylinder during dt is,

    dQ=nqvd​A dt

    I=nqvd​A

    The current per unit area is the current density J.

    J=AI​=nqvd​

    💡
    I=dtdQ​=n∣q∣vd​A
    💡
    J=AI​=nqvd​

    Current density J describes how charges flow at a certain point, and the vector’s direction tells us about the direction of the flow at that point. The magnitude of J can vary around a circuit. Current I describes how charges flow through an extended object such as a wire.

    #Example

    J=AI​

    J=π(0.00051)21.67​=2.04×106 A/m2

    I=n∣q∣vd​A

    1.67=(8.5×1028)(1.602×10−19)vd​π(0.00051)2

    vd​=0.000150 m/s=0.150 mm/s

    #Resistivity and Resistance

    #Resistivity

    The current density J in a conductor depends on the electric field E and on the properties of the material. For some materials, especially metals, at a given temperature, J is nearly directly proportional to E and the ratio of the magnitudes of E and J are constant.

    💡
    ρ=JE​

    The reciprocal of resistivity ρ is conductivity σ.

    💡
    σ=ρ1​
    💡
    J=σE

    #Resistivity and Temperature

    The resistivity of a metallic conductor nearly always increases with increasing temperature.

    💡
    ρ=ρ0​[1+α(T−T0​)]

    As temperature increases, the ions of the conductor vibrate with greater amplitude, making it more likely that a moving electron will collide with an ion. This impedes the drift of electrons through the conductor and hence reduces current.

    #Resistance

    Let V be the potential difference between the higher-potential and lower-potential ends. As current flows through the potential difference, electric potential energy is lost as this energy is transferred to the ions of the material during collisions.

    V=EL

    LV​=E=ρJ=AρI​

    The ratio of V to I for a particular conductor is its resistance R.

    💡
    R=IV​=AρL​

    If ρ is constant, then so is R.

    💡
    V=IR

    Since the resistivity of a material varies with temperature, the resistance of a specific conductor also varies with temperature. For temperature ranges that are not too great,
    this variation is approximately a linear relationship.

    💡
    R=R0​[1+α(T−T0​)]

    For a resistor that obeys Ohm’s law, a graph of current as a function of potential difference is a straight line. If the sign of V changes, so does the sign of the current produced.

    #Example 1

    ρCu​=1.72×10−8 Ωm

    J=8.2×10−71.67​=2.0366×106 A/m2

    ρ=JE​

    E=(1.72×10−8)(2.0366×106)=0.0350 V/m

    V=0.035029(50)=1.75 V

    R=AρL​

    R=8.2×10−7(1.72×10−8)(50)​=1.05 Ω

    #Example 2

    αCu​=0.00393 /C°

    R0​=1.05[1+0.00393(−20)]=0.967 Ω

    R100​=1.05[1+0.00393(100−20)]=1.38 Ω

    #Electromotive Force and Circuits

    #Electromotive Force

    There is always a decrease in potential energy when charges move through a conducting material with resistance. Thus, there must be some part of the circuit in which potential energy increases. The influence that makes current flow from lower to higher potential is called electromotive force.

    A charge q experiences an electric force Fe​ due to the electric field E. The source of emf provides an additional influence, a non-electrostatic force Fn​.

    Fn​ pushes charge from b to a in an “uphill” direction against Fe​. Thus, Fn​ maintains the potential difference between the terminals.

    For the ideal source of emf, Fe​ and Fn​ are equal in magnitude but opposite in direction. Thus, work done on q is zero and there is only an increase in potential energy.

    💡
    Vab​=ε

    #Internal Resistance

    Real sources of emf have internal resistance r. Thus, the potential difference across a real source in a circuit is not equal to the emf.

    💡
    Vab​=ε−Ir

    The potential Vab​ called the terminal voltage, is less than the emf ε due to a potential drop across the internal resistance r.

    #Example 1

    I=R+rε​

    I=4+212​=2 A

    Vab​=ε−Ir

    Vab​=12−2(2)=8 V

    #Example 2

    As charges move through a resistor, there is a decrease in electric potential energy, but there is no change in current. Thus, the ammeter for part (a) would read I=2 A.

    An ideal voltmeter would have an infinitely large resistance, thus there would be no current at all in the circuit the ammeter for part (b) would read I=0.

    The voltmeter measure the potential difference Vbb′​. Since there is no current, the potential difference across the resistor and ideal ammeter is zero. Thus, Vbb′​=Vab​. Since there is no current, the terminal voltage equals the emf and Vab​=12 V.

    #Energy and Power in Electric Circuits

    As an amount of charge passes through the circuit element, there is a change in electric potential energy equal to qVab​.

    If q>0 and Vab​ is positive, potential energy decreases as the charge “falls” from potential Va​ to lower potential Vb​. The quantity qVab​ represents energy transferred into the circuit element.

    If Va​ is lower than Vb​, then Vab​ is negative and there is a net transfer of energy out of the circuit element. The element acts as a source, delivering electrical energy into the circuit.

    💡
    P=Vab​I

    #Power Input to a Resistor

    💡
    P=Vab​I=I2R=RV2​

    The moving charges collide with atoms in the resistor and transfer some of their energy to these atoms, increasing the internal energy of the material. Either the temperature of the resistor increases or there is a flow of heat out of it, or both. The energy is dissipated in the resistor at a rate of I2R.

    #Power Output of a Source

    Va​ is higher than Vb​, and thus, Vab​ is positive. The current is leaving the higher-potential terminal and energy is being delivered to the external circuit.

    Vab​=ε−Ir

    💡
    P=Vab​I=εI−I2r

    The term εI represents the rate of conversion of non-electrical energy to electrical energy within the source. The term I2r is the rate at which electrical energy is dissipated in the internal resistance of the source.

    #Power Input to a Source

    The source with the higher emf is pushing current backwards through the source with the lower emf.

    Vab​=ε+Ir

    💡
    P=Vab​I=εI+I2r

    Work is being done on the battery, and there is a conversion of electrical energy into non-electrical energy at a rate of εI.

    #Example 1

    Rate of Energy Conversion=εI

    εI=12(2)=24 W

    Dissipation (Battery)=I2r

    I2r=(2)2(2)=8 W

    Dissipation (Resistor)=I2R

    I2R=(2)2(4)=16 W

    #Example 2

    Rate of Energy Conversion=εI

    εI=12(6)=72 W

    Dissipation (Battery)=I2r

    I2r=(6)2(2)=72 W

    With ideal wires and an ideal ammeter, such that R=0, all of the converted energy from the source is dissipated within the source. This is why a short-circuited battery is quickly ruined and may explode.
