    Newton’s Laws of Motion

    Dynamics is the relationship of motion to the forces that cause it.

    #Force and Interactions

    A force is an interaction between two bodies or between a body and its environment.

    #Types of Forces

    Types of contact forces:

    • Normal force
    • Friction force
    • Tension force

    Types of non-contact forces:

    • Magnetic force
    • Force of gravity

    #Superposition of Forces

    Any number of forces applied at a point on a body have the same effect as a single force equal to the vector sum of the forces. Thus, any force can be replaced by its component vectors, acting at the same point.

    The vector sum (resultant) of all the forces acting on a body is called the net force.

    Rx​=∑Fx​

    Ry​=∑Fy​

    R=(Rx​)2+(Ry​)2​

    For three-dimensional problems,

    R=(Rx​)2+(Ry​)2+(Rz​)2​

    #Example

    4500sin48°=FB​sin32°

    FB​=6310 N

    ∣FA​+FB​∣=FA​cos48°+FB​cos32°=8362 N

    #Newton’s First Law

    When no net force acts on a body, the body either remains at rest or moves with
    constant velocity in a straight line. Once a body has been set in motion, no net
    force is required to keep it moving. The tendency of a body to keep moving
    once it is set in motion results from inertia.

    When net force is zero, a body is in equilibrium.

    💡
    ∑F=0         ∑Fx​=0         ∑Fy​=0

    #Inertial Frames of Reference

    A frame of reference in which Newton’s first law is valid is called the inertial frame
    of reference. Thus, Newton’s first law is sometimes called the law of inertia.

    Even though the passenger’s velocity relative to the vehicle changes, the net force
    acting on the passenger is zero. The vehicle’s frame of reference is not an inertial
    frame, thus Newton’s first law is invalid.

    #Newton’s Second Law

    When the net force acting on an object is not zero, during the time the force is acting, the velocity of the object changes at a constant rate and it moves with constant acceleration,
    where the acceleration a is in the same direction as v and ∑F.

    💡
    ∑F=ma

    Newton’s second law refers to external forces — forces exerted on a body by other bodies.

    Newton’s second law also apply to a body moving along a curved path. In this case, the net force and acceleration are both constant in magnitude and directed towards the center of the circle.

    💡
    ∑F=rmv2​
    💡
    ∑F=mrω2

    The principle superposition of forces also apply when net force acts at an angle.

    💡
    ∑Fx​=max​          ∑Fy​=may​          ∑Fz​=maz​

    The above equations are valid only when the mass of an object is constant.

    #Applying Newton’s Laws

    #Kinetic and Static Friction

    When a body rests or slides on a surface, the surface exerts a single contact force on the body, with force components perpendicular and parallel to the surface.

    The direction of the friction force is always such as to oppose relative motion of the two surfaces.

    The friction that acts when a body slides over a surface is called kinetic friction force.

    💡
    Fk​=μk​Fn​

    The more slippery a surface is, the smaller the coefficient of kinetic friction.

    Friction forces also act when there is no relative motion — static friction force.

    💡
    Fs​≤μs​Fn​

    As soon as the box starts to slide, the friction force usually decreases; it’s easier to keep the box moving than to start it moving. Hence, the coefficient of kinetic friction is usually less than the coefficient of static friction for any given pair of surfaces.

    #Example 1

    #Fluid Resistance and Terminal Velocity

    Fluid resistance is the force that a fluid exerts on a body moving though it. The
    moving body exerts a force on the fluid to push it out of the way. By Newton’s
    third law, the fluid pushes back on the body with an equal and opposite force.

    For small objects moving at very low speeds, fluid resistance is defined by,

    💡
    F=kv

    k is a constant that depends on the shape and size of the body and the properties of the
    fluid. The equation is appropriate for dust particles falling in air or a ball falling in oil.

    For larger object moving at higher speeds, fluid resistance (air drag) is defined by,

    💡
    F=Dv2

    D is a constant that depends on the shape and size of the body and the air density.

    Due to the effects of fluid resistance, an object falling in a fluid does not have a constant acceleration.

    ∑Fy​=mg−kvy​

    may​=mg−kvy​

    As velocity increases, resisting force also increases until it equals object’s weight.

    0=mg−kvt​

    At this point, acceleration becomes zero, and the object reaches its terminal velocity.

    💡
    vt​=kmg​

    To determine the relationship between velocity and time during the interval
    before the terminal velocity is reached, we can rewrite using ay​=dtdvy​​.

    mdtdvy​​=mg−kvy​

    km​⋅dtdvy​​=kmg​−vy​

    km​⋅dtdvy​​=vt​−vy​

    vt​−vy​1​⋅dtdvy​​=mk​

    ∫0v​vy​−vt​1​ dvy​=−mk​∫0v​ dt

    lnvt​vt​−vy​​=−mk​t

    💡
    vy​=vt​[1−e−(k/m)t]

    vy​=vt​ as t→∞; the ball cannot attain terminal velocity in any finite length of time.

    For an object falling through the air at high speeds, its fluid resistance is equal to Dv2.

    💡
    vt​=Dmg​​
