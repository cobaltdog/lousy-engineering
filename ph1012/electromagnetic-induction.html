    Electromagnetic Induction

    #Faraday’s Law

    The magnetic flux through an area is given by Gauss’s Law.

    💡
    ΦB​=∫B⋅dA=BAcosθ

    Faraday’s law of induction states that the induced emf in a closed loop equals the negative of the time rate of change of magnetic flux through the loop.

    💡
    ε=−dtdΦB​​

    For a coil with N identical turns and if the flux varies at the same rate through each turn, the total rate of change through all the turns is N times larger than for a single turn.

    💡
    ε=−N dtdΦB​​

    #Direction of Induced emf

    The direction can be determined using the right-hand-rule. If the induced emf or current in the circuit is positive, it is in the same direction as your curled fingers; if the induced emf or current is negative, it is in the opposite direction.

    #Example 1

    ε=−dtdΦB​​=−dtdB​ Acosθ

    ε=−(0.02)(0.012)

    ε=−0.24 mV

    I=5−0.24​=−0.048 mA

    #Example 2

    ε=−N dtdΦB​​=−N dtdB​ Acosθ

    ε=−500(−0.2)π(0.04)2cos30°

    ε=0.435 V

    The direction of the induced emf follows the direction of your curled fingers.

    #Lenz’s Law

    Lenz’s law states that the induced current in a loop is in the direction that creates a magnetic field that opposes the change in magnetic flux through the area enclosed by the loop.

    #Motional emf

    Motional emf is the emf induced in a conductor moving through a constant magnetic field.

    Suppose a conductor of length l is moving in a direction perpendicular to B with a constant v.

    Under the influence of FB​, charges move to the upper end of the conductor and accumulate there, leaving a net negative charge at the lower end.

    An E field is produced inside the conductor.
    The charges accumulate at both ends until the upward FB​ is balanced by the downward Fe​.

    Fe​=FB​

    qE=qvB

    E=vB

    Vab​=El=Blv

    💡
    ε=Blv

    The general formula for motional emf for a conductor with any shape is given by,

    💡
    ε=∮(v×B)⋅dl

    When we have stationary conductors in changing magnetic fields, the above equation cannot be used. In such cases, ε=−dtdΦB​​ should be used instead.

    #Example 1

    ε=Blv

    ε=0.6(0.1)(2.5)=0.15 V

    I=Rε​

    I=0.030.15​=5 A

    FB​=IlB

    FB​=5(0.1)(0.6)=0.3 N

    The magnetic force is directed opposite to the rod’s motion.

    #Example 2

    ε=Blv

    I=R∣ε∣​=RBlv​

    Pdissipated​=I2R

    Pdissipated​=(RBlv​)2R=RB2l2v2​

    #Induced Electric Fields

    A solenoid with cross-sectional area A and n turns per unit length is surrounded at the center by a circular conducting loop.

    A current I in the windings of the solenoid sets up a magnetic field B along the solenoid axis, whereby magnetic flux passes through the loop.

    B=μ0​nI

    ΦB​=BA=μ0​nIA

    When the solenoid current changes with time, the magnetic flux also changes and induces an emf in the loop.

    ε=−dtdΦB​​=−μ0​nA dtdI​

    dtdI​=0 → ε=0

    An induced electric field in the conductor caused by the changing magnetic flux makes charges move around the wire loop. When a charge q goes once around the loop, the
    total work done on it by the electric field must be equal to q multiplied by ε.

    💡
    ∮E⋅dl=ε=−dtdΦB​​

    #Example

    ε=−dtdΦB​​=−μ0​nA dtdI​

    ε=−(4π×10−7)(500)(0.0004)(100)=−25.133 μV

    ε=∮E⋅dl

    ε=E(2πr)

    E=2πr∣ε∣​

    E=2π(0.02)25.133×10−6​=0.002 V/m

    #Generators and Motors

    Electric generators take in energy by work and transfer it out by electrical transmission.

    As a loop rotates in a magnetic field, the magnetic flux through the area enclosed by the loop changes with time, and this change induces an emf and a current in the loop according to Faraday’s law.

    Consider a coil with N turns and an area A, that rotates in a magnetic field with a constant angular velocity ω.

    ΦB​=BAcosθ

    ΦB​=BAcosωt

    ε=−N dtdΦB​​=−NBA dtd​(cosωt)

    ε=−NBA(−sinωt)

    💡
    ε=NBAsinωt
