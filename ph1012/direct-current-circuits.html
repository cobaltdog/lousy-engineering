    Direct Current Circuits

    #Kirchhoff’s Rules

    A junction in a circuit is a point where three or more conductors meet. A loop is any closed conducting path.

    💡
    ∑I=0          (junction rule)

    The junction rule is based on conservation of electric charge. No charge can accumulate at a junction, so the total charge entering the junction per unit time must equal the total charge leaving the unit per unit time.

    💡
    ∑V=0         (loop rule)

    The loop rule is a statement that the electromotive force is conservative.

    #Sign Conventions for Loop Rule

    When we travel through a source in the direction from + to −, the emf is considered to be positive; when we travel from − to +, the emf is considered to be negative.

    When we travel through a resistor in the same direction as the assumed current, voltage drop across the resistor is −IR because the current goes in the direction of decreasing potential. When we travel in the direction opposite to the assumed current, the voltage drop is +IR.

    #R-C Circuits

    To distinguish between quantities that vary with time and those that are constant, we will use lowercase letters v, i and q for time-varying voltages, currents and charges, respectively.

    #Charging a Capacitor

    Let q represent the charge on the capacitor and i the current in the circuit at some time t after the switch has been closed.

    vab​=iR

    vbc​=Cq​

    Using Kirchhoff’s loop rule,

    0=ε−iR−Cq​

    💡
    i=Rε​−RCq​

    At t=0, when the switch is first closed, the capacitor is uncharged, and so q=0.

    The initial current is given by,

    💡
    I0​=Rε​

    As q increases, vbc​ becomes larger and the capacitor charge approaches its final value Qf​. The current decreases and eventually becomes zero (I=0).

    0=Rε​−RCQf​​

    💡
    Qf​=Cε

    We can derive expressions for the charge q and current i as functions of time.

    i=dtdq​=Rε​−RCq​=−RC1​(q−Cε)

    q−Cε1​ dq=−RC1​ dt

    ∫0q​q−Cε1​ dq=−∫0t​RC1​ dt

    ln(−Cεq−Cε​)=−RCt​

    Taking exponential of both sides,

    💡
    q=Cε(1−e−t/RC)=Qf​(1−e−t/RC)

    The instantaneous current i can be found as the derivative.

    💡
    i=Rε​e−t/RC=I0​e−t/RC

    After a time equal to RC, the current in the circuit has decreased to 1/e of its initial value. At this time, the capacitor charge has reached (1−1/e)=0.632 of its final value Qf​.

    The product RC is a measure of how quickly a capacitor charges — the time constant.

    💡
    τ=RC

    If R is in ohms and C in farads, τ is in seconds.

    #Example

    RC=(10×106)(1×10−6)=10 s

    q=Qf​(1−e−t/RC)

    Qf​q​=1−e−46/10

    Qf​q​=0.98995=99.0%

    i=I0​e−t/RC

    Io​i​=e−46/10

    Io​i​=0.010052=1.00%

    #Discharging a Capacitor

    Suppose the capacitor has acquired a charge Q0​, we remove the battery from the circuit and connect points a and c to an open switch.

    Using Kirchhoff’s loop rule,

    0=−iR−Cq​

    💡
    i=−RCq​

    The current is negative as positive charge is leaving the left-hand capacitor plate and current is flowing
    in the opposite direction.

    At t=0 and q=Q0​, the initial current is given by,

    💡
    I0​=−RCQ0​​

    i=dtdq​=−RCq​

    ∫Q0​q​q1​ dq=−∫0t​RC1​ dt

    lnQ0​q​=−RCt​

    💡
    q=Q0​e−t/RC
    💡
    i=−RCQ0​​e−t/RC=−I0​e−t/RC
