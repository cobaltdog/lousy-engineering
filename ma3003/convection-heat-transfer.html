    Convection Heat Transfer

    #Convection Mechanism

    Convection can be classified into:

    • Natural convection — fluid motion due to buoyancy effect
    • Forced convection — fluid is forced to flow over a surface by external means

    Forced convection can be further classified into:

    • Internal flow — fluid is completely bounded by solid surfaces (e.g. flow in a pipe)
    • External flow — flow of an unbounded fluid over a surface, such as a plate

    Rate of convection heat transfer is expressed by Newton’s law of cooling,

    💡
    q˙​x​=hx​(Ts​−T∞​)

    where hx​ is the local convection coefficient

    Factors affecting local convection coefficient:

    • Flow conditions
    • Surface geometry
    • Density of fluid ρ
    • Dynamic viscosity of fluid μ
    • Coefficient of pressure Cp​
    • Thermal conductivity of fluid k

    At the surface that is in contact with the fluid,

    💡
    q˙​conv​=q˙​cond​=−kfluid​∂y∂T​​y=0​

    Note that the fluid in direct contact with a solid “sticks” to the surface due to viscous effects,
    which is known as the no-slip condition. Thus, heat transfer from the solid’s surface to the
    fluid layer adjacent to the surface is by pure conduction, since the layer is motionless.

    Total heat transfer rate from a surface is then,

    Q˙​=∫As​​ q˙​ dAs​

    Q˙​=∫As​​ hx​(Ts​−T∞​) dAs​

    Q˙​=[As​1​∫As​​ hx​ dAs​]As​(Ts​−T∞​)

    💡
    Q˙​=hˉAs​(Ts​−T∞​)

    where hˉ is the average convection coefficient

    For flow over a flat plate with a uniform width,

    💡
    hˉ=L1​∫0L​ hx​ dx

    #Nusselt Number

    Nusselt number is used to nondimensionalize the convection coefficient.

    💡
    Nu=kf​hLc​​

    where kf​ is the thermal conductivity of the fluid

    Consider a fluid layer of thickness L and a temperature difference ΔT=T2​−T1​. Heat transfer through the layer convection; fluid is moving, or by conduction
    when the fluid is motionless.

    The Nusselt number represents the enhancement
    of heat transfer through a fluid layer as a result
    of convection relative to conduction.

    The larger the Nusselt number, the more effective the convection. If Nu=1, it implies that heat transfer
    across a fluid layer is by pure conduction.

    #Velocity Boundary Layer

    Consider the parallel flow of a fluid over a flat plate.
    x-coordinate is measured in the direction of the flow.
    y-coordinate is measured from the surface in the normal direction.

    The fluid approaches the plate in the x-direction with a uniform velocity V.
    The fluid layer adjacent to the plate’s surface is zero due to no-slip condition.
    The velocity boundary layer is the distance y from the surface at which u=0.99V.

    Hypothetical line of u=0.99V divides the flow over the plate into two regions.
    Boundary layer region is where viscous effects and velocity changes are significant.
    Inviscid region is where frictional effects are negligible and velocity is essentially constant.

    #Surface Shear Stress

    Shear stress exerted by the fluid layer in contact with the surface is,

    💡
    τs​=μ∂y∂u​​y=0​

    Fluids that obey the above linear relationship are Newtonian fluids.

    Variation of shear stress along surface:

    • Velocity gradient decreases in the flow direction as the boundary layer grows
    • Shear stress then decreases based on
      the equation above

    Local friction coefficient is defined as,

    💡
    Cf,x​=21​ρV2τs,x​​

    Average friction coefficient is then,

    💡
    Cˉf​=21​ρV2τˉs​​=∫As​​ Cf,x​ dAs​

    Once Cˉf​ over a given surface is known, the skin friction drag force can be found.

    💡
    Ff​=τˉs​As​=21​ρV2As​Cˉf​

    #Laminar and Turbulent Flows

    Surface friction and convection heat transfer depend strongly on the type of fluid flow.

    Laminar flow is characterized by smooth streamlines and highly ordered motion.
    Turbulent flow is characterized by velocity fluctuations and disordered motion.

    Transition from laminar to turbulent flow depends on:

    • Surface geometry
    • Surface roughness
    • Surface temperature
    • Flow velocity of fluid
    • Type of fluid

    Reynolds number, a dimensionless quantity, is defined as,

    💡
    Re=μρVL​=νVL​

    For a flat plate, the characteristic length is the distance x from the leading edge.
    For a flat plate, flow transition occurs at critical Reynolds number     Recr​=5×105.

    #Thermal Boundary Layer

    Thermal boundary layer:

    • Develops when there is a temperature difference between freestream and surface
    • At the surface, where y=0, fluid temperature is equal to surface temperature
    • Fluid temperature varies along the y-direction

    Consider the flow of a fluid at a uniform temperature T∞​ over an isothermal flat plate
    at temperature Ts​. The fluid layer adjacent to the surface reaches thermal equilibrium
    with the plate such that T∞​=Ts​.

    Thickness δt​ of the boundary layer at any location is
    the distance y at which the ratio of temperatures is,

    💡
    Ts​−T∞​Ts​−T(x,y)​=0.99

    Convection heat transfer rate:

    • Directly related to the temperature gradient at that location
    • Shape of temperature profile dictates the convection heat transfer rate
    • Thickness of boundary layer increases in the flow direction
    • Temperature gradient at y=0 decreases in the flow direction

    Due to the no-slip condition, heat transfer from the surface to the fluid layer adjacent to the surface is purely by conduction.

    q˙​conv​=q˙​cond​

    hx​(Ts​−T∞​)=−kfluid​∂y∂T​​y=0​

    💡
    hx​=Ts​−T∞​−kf​∂y∂T​​y=0​​

    whereby the partial derivative is the
    temperature gradient at the surface

    #Prandtl Number

    Relative thickness of the velocity and thermal boundary layers
    is described by the dimensionless parameter Prandtl number.

    💡
    Pr=kμcp​​=αν​

    Ratio of the molecular diffusivity of momentum to the molecular diffusivity of heat.

    For gases, Pr≈1, momentum and heat dissipate through the fluid at the same rate.
    For liquid metals, Pr≪1, heat diffuses very quickly through the fluid.
    For oils, Pr≫1, heat diffuses very slowly through the fluid.

    #Differential Convection Equations

    Assumptions for fluid flow:

    • Flow is steady and 2-dimensional
    • Fluid properties are constant
    • Fluid is a Newtonian fluid

    Consider the parallel flow of a fluid over a surface.
    Fluid flows with a uniform freestream velocity V,
    which is resolved into u(x,y) and v(x,y).

    #Conservation of Mass

    In steady flow, the mass inflow must be equal to mass outflow.

    m˙in​=m˙out​

    ∑in​ρAV=∑out​ρAV

    ρu(dy⋅1)+ρv(dx⋅1)=ρ(u+∂x∂u​dx)(dy⋅1)+ρ(v+∂y∂v​dy)(dx⋅1)

    Simplifying the above equation,

    💡
    ∂x∂u​+∂y∂v​=0

    #Momentum Equation

    The net force on a control volume is equal to the product of mass and acceleration of
    the fluid element within the control volume, which is also equal to the net change in momentum of the control volume.

    Using Newton’s 2nd law on the control volume,

    dm⋅ax​=Fsurface,x​+Fbody,x​

    ρ(dx⋅dy⋅1)ax​=Fsurface,x​+Fbody,x​

    Acceleration of the fluid element in the x-direction is,

    ax​=dtdu​=∂x∂u​dtdx​+∂y∂u​dtdy​=u∂x∂u​+v∂y∂u​

    Note that u is a function of both x and y.

    Forces acting on the fluid element are due to pressure and viscous effects.
    In a 2-dimensional flow, viscous stress can be resolved into normal stress
    and shear stress. However, normal stress can be considered negligible.

    Net surface force acting in the x-direction is,

    Fsurface,x​=(∂y∂τ​dy)(dx⋅1)−(∂x∂P​dx)(dy⋅1)

    Fsurface,x​=(∂y∂τ​−∂x∂P​)(dx⋅dy⋅1)

    Fsurface,x​=(μ∂y2∂2u​−∂x∂P​)(dx⋅dy⋅1)

    Note that shear stress τ=μ∂y∂u​.

    Substituting into Newton’s 2nd law, we get the momentum equation in the x-direction.

    💡
    ρ(u∂x∂u​+v∂y∂u​)=μ∂y2∂2u​−∂x∂P​+X

    where X is any body force (gravity, electric, magnetic, etc.) expressed in per unit volume

    Momentum equation in the y-direction is,

    💡
    ρ(u∂x∂v​+v∂y∂v​)=μ∂y2∂2v​−∂y∂P​+Y

    where Y is any body force (gravity, electric, magnetic, etc.) expressed in per unit volume

    #Energy Equation

    Energy balance for a steady flow process is expressed as,

    E˙in​−E˙out​=0

    Since energy can be transferred by heat, work and mass only,

    (E˙in​−E˙out​)heat​+(E˙in​−E˙out​)work​+(E˙in​−E˙out​)mass​=0

    Total energy of a flowing fluid
    expressed in per unit mass is,

    e=h+KE+PE

    e=h+2V2​+gz

    e=h+2u2+v2​+gz

    e=h=cp​T

    Note that the kinetic and potential energies
    are usually very small relative to enthalpy.

    Net rate of energy transfer by mass to the control volume is,

    (E˙in​−E˙out​)mass​=−ρcp​(u∂x∂T​+T∂x∂u​)dxdy−ρcp​(v∂y∂T​+T∂y∂v​)dxdy

    (E˙in​−E˙out​)mass​=−ρcp​(u∂x∂T​+v∂y∂T​)dxdy

    Note that ∂x∂u​+∂y∂v​=0 from the continuity equation.

    Net rate of heat transfer to the control volume is,

    (E˙in​−E˙out​)heat​=k∂x2∂2T​dxdy+k∂y2∂2T​dxdy

    (E˙in​−E˙out​)heat​=k(∂x2∂2T​+∂y2∂2T​)dxdy

    Energy equation when viscous shear stresses are not negligible is,

    💡
    ρcp​(u∂x∂T​+v∂y∂T​)=k(∂x2∂2T​+∂y2∂2T​)+μΦ+e˙gen​

    where Φ is the viscous dissipation function

    Viscous dissipation function is defined as,

    💡
    Φ=(∂y∂u​+∂x∂v​)2+2[(∂x∂u​)2+(∂y∂v​)2]

    Viscous dissipation is the rate at which mechanical work (kinetic energy)
    is converted into heat (thermal energy) due to viscous effects in the fluid.

    For a stationary fluid, where u=v=0, the energy equation
    reduces to the steady 2-dimensional heat conduction equation.

    💡
    ∂x2∂2T​+∂y2∂2T​=0

    #Simplifications

    Assuming small boundary layer thickness:

    • u≫v
    • ∂y∂u​≫∂x∂u​,∂x∂v​,∂y∂v​
    • ∂y∂T​≫∂x∂T​
    • P=P(x) where pressure varies only along the flow direction

    Other simplifications include:

    • Negligible body forces X and Y on the element
    • No heat generation within the control volume

    Momentum equation in the x-direction reduces to,

    💡
    u∂x∂u​+v∂y∂u​=ν∂y2∂2u​−ρ1​∂x∂P​

    RHS of the equation represents viscous and pressure forces.
    LHS of the equation represents inertia forces.
    For a flat plate,     ∂x∂P​=0.

    Momentum equation in the y-direction reduces to,

    💡
    ∂y∂P​=0

    Energy equation for the control volume reduces to,

    💡
    u∂x∂T​+v∂y∂T​=α∂y2∂2T​+cp​ν​(∂y∂u​)2

    RHS of the equation represents conduction and viscous forces.
    LHS of the equation represents advection (energy transfer through bulk motion of fluid).

    #Example

    Assumptions:

    • Steady operating conditions
    • Oil is an incompressible fluid
    • Body forces such as gravity is negligible
    • Plates are large, no variation in z-direction

    From Table A13,

    koil​=0.145 W/m⋅K

    μ=0.8374 kg/m⋅s

    #Part (a)

    Velocity distribution in the oil,

    u(y)=LV​y

    Temperature distribution in the oil,

    T(y)=T0​+2kμV2​(Ly​−L2y2​)

    Conservation of mass

    Parallel flow between the plates, v=0.

    Continuity equation reduces to,

    ∂x∂u​+∂y∂v​=0

    ∂x∂u​=0

    Momentum equation

    Since u=u(y), v=0, and ∂x∂P​=0 for a flat plate,

    u∂x∂u​+v∂y∂u​=ν∂y2∂2u​−ρ1​∂x∂P​

    dy2d2u​=0

    Integrating the x-momentum equation twice,

    u(y)=C1​y+C2​

    Boundary conditions:

    • u(0)=0
    • u(L)=V

    Substituting the boundary conditions,

    u(y)=LV​y

    Energy equation

    Plates are isothermal, thus T=T(y).
    Additionally, u=u(y) and v=0.

    u∂x∂T​+v∂y∂T​=α∂y2∂2T​+cp​ν​(∂y∂u​)2

    0=ρcp​k​∂y2∂2T​+cp​μ/ρ​(∂y∂u​)2

    0=k∂y2∂2T​+μ(∂y∂u​)2

    ∂y2∂2T​=−kμ​(LV​)2

    From momentum equation,

    u(y)=LV​y

    ∂y∂u​=LV​

    Integrating the energy equation twice,

    T(y)=−2kμ​(LV​y)2+C3​y+C4​

    Boundary conditions:

    • T(0)=T0​
    • T(L)=T0​

    Substituting the boundary conditions,

    T(y)=T0​+2kμV2​(Ly​−L2y2​)

    #Part (b)

    Gradient of temperature distribution,

    dydT​=2kLμV2​(1−L2y​)

    Maximum temperature is obtained by equating the above to zero.

    0=2kLμV2​(1−L2y​)

    2kLμV2​=kL2μV2​y

    y=2L​

    Maximum temperature occurs at the midpoint between both plates.

    Tmax​=T0​+2kμV2​(LL/2​−L2L2/4​)

    Tmax​=T0​+8kμV2​

    Tmax​=20+8(0.145)0.8374(12)​

    Tmax​=124°C

    Heat flux through bottom plate,

    q˙​0​=q˙​cond​=−koil​dydT​​y=0​

    q˙​0​=−k⋅2kLμV2​(1−0)

    q˙​0​=−2LμV2​

    q˙​0​=−2(0.002)0.8374(12)2​

    q˙​0​=−30.1 kW/m2

    Heat flux through top plate,

    q˙​L​=q˙​cond​=−koil​dydT​​y=L​

    q˙​L​=−k⋅2kLμV2​(1−2)

    q˙​L​=2LμV2​

    q˙​L​=2(0.002)0.8374(12)2​

    q˙​L​=30.1 kW/m2

    Note that the heat fluxes at the two plates are equal in magnitude but opposite in sign. Also, the heat flux is equivalent to the rate of mechanical energy dissipation. Thus, mechanical energy is being converted to heat at a rate of 60.2 kW/m2 to overcome friction in the oil.

    Note that calculations are done using oil properties at 20°C, but the actual oil temperature is much higher at 124°C. Thus, the calculations should be done
    using properties at the average temperature of     72°C.

    #Nondimensionalized Convection Equations

    The convection equations are nondimensionalized by dividing all dependent
    and independent variables by relevant and meaningful constant properties.

    All lengths are divided by a characteristic length L.

    x∗=Lx​

    y∗=Ly​

    All velocities are divided by a reference velocity V (freestream velocity for a plate).

    u∗=Vu​

    v∗=Vv​

    Pressure is divided by ρV2, and temperature by a suitable temperature difference.

    P∗=ρV2P​

    T∗=T∞​−Ts​T−Ts​​

    Continuity equation becomes,

    💡
    ∂x∗∂u∗​+∂y∗∂v∗​=0

    Momentum equation in the x-direction, assuming no body forces, becomes,

    💡
    u∗∂x∗∂u∗​+v∗∂y∗∂u∗​=VLν​∂y∗2∂2u∗​−dx∗dP∗​
    💡
    u∗∂x∗∂u∗​+v∗∂y∗∂u∗​=ReL​1​∂y∗2∂2u∗​−dx∗dP∗​

    where Reynolds number ReL​=μρVL​=νVL​.

    Energy equation, assuming no viscous dissipation, becomes,

    💡
    u∗∂x∗∂T∗​+v∗∂y∗∂T∗​=VLα​∂y∗2∂2T∗​
    💡
    u∗∂x∗∂T∗​+v∗∂y∗∂T∗​=ReL​Pr1​∂y∗2∂2T∗​

    where Prandtl number Pr=kμcp​​=αν​

    #Friction and Convection Coefficients

    For a given geometry, the solution for u∗ is expressed as,

    u∗=f1​(x∗, y∗, REL​)

    Shear stress at the surface becomes,

    τs​=μ∂y∂u​​y=0​

    τs​=LμV​∂y∗∂u∗​​y∗=0​

    τs​=LμV​ f2​(x∗, ReL​)

    Substituting the above into the local friction coefficient,

    Cf,x​=21​ρV2τs,x​​

    Cf,x​=ρV2/2μV/L​ f2​(x∗, ReL​)

    💡
    Cf,x​=ReL​2​ f2​(x∗, ReL​)

    Friction coefficient is a function of only x∗ and Reynolds number.

    For a given geometry, the solution for T∗ is expressed as,

    T∗=g1​(x∗, y∗, ReL​, Pr)

    From the convection heat transfer coefficient equation,

    hx​=Ts​−T∞​−kf​∂y∂T​​y=0​​

    hx​=L(Ts​−T∞​)−kf​(T∞​−Ts​)​∂y∗∂T∗​​y∗=0​

    hx​=Lkf​​∂y∗∂T∗​​y∗=0​

    Substituting the above into the Nusselt number,

    Nux​=kf​hx​L​

    Nux​=∂y∗∂T∗​​y∗=0​

    💡
    Nux​=g2​(x∗, ReL​, Pr)

    Average Nusselt number is then,

    💡
    Nuˉ=g3​(ReL​, Pr)

    #Laminar Flow over a Flat Plate

    Consider the nondimensionalized momentum and energy equations for steady, incompressible, laminar flow of a fluid with constant properties and negligible
    viscous dissipation.

    When Pr=1 and ∂x∗∂P∗​=0 for a flat plate,

    u∗∂x∗∂u∗​+v∗∂y∗∂u∗​=ReL​1​∂y∗2∂2u∗​ (Momentum equation)

    u∗∂x∗∂T∗​+v∗∂y∗∂T∗​=ReL​1​∂y∗2∂2T∗​ (Energy equation)

    Notice that the equations are in the same form. Thus, the
    first derivatives of u∗ and T∗ at the surface must be equal.

    ∂y∗∂u∗​​y∗=0​=∂y∗∂T∗​​y∗=0​

    Substituting the Nusselt number into the friction coefficient,

    Cf,x​=ReL​2​ f2​(x∗, ReL​)

    Cf,x​=ReL​2​∂y∗∂u∗​​y∗=0​

    💡
    Cf,x​=ReL​2​Nux​

    which is known as the Reynolds analogy and is only applicable when Pr=1

    Alternatively, the Reynolds analogy can be expressed as,

    💡
    Stx​=2Cf,x​​=ReL​PrNux​​=ρcp​Vhx​​

    where St is the Stanton number

    The Reynolds analogy is of limited use because of the restriction Pr=1. It is desirable
    to have an analogy that is applicable over a wide range of Pr, which is done by adding
    a Prandtl number correction.

    Friction coefficient and Nusselt number for a flat plate are,

    Cf,x​=0.664ReL​−1/2

    Nux​=0.332ReL​1/2Pr1/3

    Taking the ratio of the above and rearranging, we get the Chilton-Colburn analogy.

    💡
    Cf,x​2ReL​​=Nux​Pr−1/3
    💡
    2Cf,x​​=Stx​Pr2/3=jH​

    The above equations are only applicable for 0.6<Pr<60.
    jH​ is called the Colburn j-factor.

    #Example

    Assumptions:

    • Steady operating conditions exist
    • Edge effects are negligible
    • Local atmospheric pressure is 1 atm

    From Table A15, at 20°C,

    ρair​=1.204 kg/m3

    cp​=1.007 kJ/kg⋅K

    Pr=0.7309

    Airflow is along the length of the plate,
    thus characteristic length is L=3 m.

    Total surface area exposed to airflow,

    As​=2(2)(3)=12 m2

    From the drag force equation,

    Ff​=21​ρV2As​Cf​

    0.86=21​(1.204)(72)(12)Cf​

    Cf​=0.00243

    Average convection coefficient,

    2Cf​​=StPr2/3

    2Cf​​=ρcp​Vh​Pr2/3

    20.00243​=1.204(1007)(7)h​(0.7309)2/3

    h=12.7 W/m2⋅K
