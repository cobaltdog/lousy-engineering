    Pure Bending

    #Pure Bending (Centric)

    Pure bending occurs when prismatic members are subjected to equal and opposite couples acting in the same longitudinal plane. A prismatic member can be defined as the type of beam which has the same cross section throughout the length of the beam.

    Torque is bending/twisting about a member’s longitudinal axis.
    Bending moment is about an axis perpendicular to the member’s longitudinal axis.

    #Bending Deformation

    Under pure bending of a beam with a plane of symmetry:

    • Member remains symmetric when subject to bending moment
    • Member bends uniformly to form a circular arc between the end faces
    • Cross section planes passes through arc’s center and remains planar

    The length of the top surface decreases while the length of the bottom surface increases. A neutral surface must exist, that is parallel to both surfaces, for which the its length does not change. Stresses and strains above and below the neutral surface are negative and positive respectively (compression and tension).

    #Bending Strain

    Understanding Stresses in BeamsIn this video we explore bending and shear stresses in beams. A bending moment is the resultant of bending stresses, which are normal stresses acting perpendicular to the beam cross-section. We can easily derive an equation for these bending stresses by observing how a beam deforms for a case of pure bending.https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f08Y39UiC-o

    Consider a beam segment of length L with its centroid at O.

    The length of the neutral surface remains the same at L (solid line).

    Let L′ be the length of a surface above the neutral surface (dotted line).

    Arc Length=rθ

    L=ρθ

    L′=(ρ−y)θ

    ΔL=L′−L=−yθ

    💡
    εx​=ρθ−yθ​=−ρy​

    As y ⟶ c, maximum strain occurs at either the top or bottom surface.

    💡
    ∣εmax​∣=ρc​ ⟶ ρ=∣εmax​∣c​
    💡
    εx​=−cy​εmax​
    💡
    σx​=−cy​σmax​

    #Bending Stress

    A moment causes different sides of the neutral axis to experience different stresses. The neutral axis is where there is tension and compression on both sides.

    💡
    σmax​=IMc​

    where M is the bending moment,
    and c is the maximum distance from neutral axis (NA),
    and I is the second moment of area (moment of inertia).

    Substituting σmax​ into σx​=−cy​σmax​, we get the following equation.

    💡
    σx​=−IMy​

    where M is the bending moment,
    and y is the distance from neutral axis (NA),
    and I is the second moment of area (moment of inertia).

    #Second Moment of Area

    By finding the centroid of an object, we can determine whether forces acting on the surface of an object will result in centric or eccentric loadings.

    For complex geometry, use the area method to determine the location of the centroid.

    💡
    ∑i=1n​ Ai​yi​=AT​yˉ​

    Second moment of area (moment of inertia):

    • Axis for moment of inertia has to be the same axis where the moment was taken
    • Can only add/subtract moment of inertia if they are all about the same axis

      If the moments of inertia are not about the same axis, use parallel axis theorem to shift the moment of inertia from each section’s NA to the object’s NA.

    💡
    INA​=12bh3​ for rectangular cross section with respect to the centroid
    💡
    INA​=2J​=4πr4​ for solid circular cross section
    💡
    INA​=4π(r2 4​−r1 4​)​ for hollow circular cross section
    Understanding the Area Moment of InertiaThe area moment of inertia (also called the second moment of area) defines the resistance of a cross-section to bending, due to the shape of the cross-section. It is a key parameter for the analysis of beams and columns. In this video I take a detailed look at the area moment of inertia, how's it calculated, and how it's used.https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Bls5KnQOWkY

    #Parallel Axis Theorem

    💡
    Parallel Axis Theorem:  I+Ad2
    where I is the moment of inertia of a section, and A is the area of that section,
    and d is distance between the NA of the section and NA of the object

    To find the moment of inertia about the horizontal axis, we need to shift the moment of inertia of each section to the neutral axis to be able to add/subtract them.

    Ixx​=(I1​+A1​d1 2​)+(I2​+A2​d2 2​)

    Ixx​=[12b1​h1 3​​+(b1​h1​)(y1​−yNA​)2]+[12b2​h2 3​​+(b2​h2​)(yNA​−y2​)2]

    Iyy​=12b1​h1 3​​+12b2​h2 3​​

    Bending about the X-X axis, where the axis is the NA, is denoted by Ixx​.

    Bending about the Y-Y axis, where the axis is the NA, is denoted by Iyy​.

    #Elastic Section Modulus

    The elastic section modulus, S, is defined as the ratio of the second moment of area, I,
    and the distance from the neutral axis to the furthest surface, c.

    💡
    S=cI​
    💡
    σmax​=SM​

    where M is the bending moment,
    and S is the elastic section modulus.

    Consider two rectangular beams with the same cross section area.

    S=cINA​​

    S=h/2bh3/12​=61​bh2=61​Ah

    From the equation, the beam with a greater height will be more effective in resisting bending. Thus, structural steel beams are designed to have a large section modulus.

    #Pure Bending (Eccentric)

    The distribution of stresses in the cross section of a member under axial loading can be assumed to be uniform only if the line of action of the loads passes through the centroid.
    Such a loading is said to be centric.

    When the line of action of the loads do not pass through the centroid, the loading is eccentric.

    ∑FC​=0

    F−P=0

    F=P

    ∑MC​=0

    M−Pd=0

    M=Pd

    From the FBD, we can see that the stresses in section DE can be represented by the same force and a moment if it was detached from from AB, and subjected to both centric loads — P and P’, and to the bending moments — M and M’.

    💡
    σx​=σx(centric)​+σx(bending)​
    💡
    σx​=AF​+(−IMy​)=AF​−IMy​

    Depending on the cross section shape and the eccentricity of the load, the combined stresses may all have the same sign, or some may be positive and others negative. The neutral axis of the member does not align with the neutral axis of the individual loading.

    #Example (Tension + Bending)

    ∑Fy​=0

    F−P=0

    F=700 N

    ∑MC​=0

    P(0.016)−M=0

    M=11.2 Nm

    σcentric​=π(0.0062)700​=6.19 MPa

    I=4π(0.0064)​=1.0179×10−9 m4

    σeccentric​=1.0179×10−911.2(0.006)​=±66.0 MPa

    σt​=6.19+66.0=72.2 MPa

    σc​=6.19−66.0=−59.8 MPa

    At the neutral axis, there is not net stress (tensile stress cancels out compressive stress).

    0=AF​−IMy​

    0=π(0.0062)700​−1.0179×10−911.2yNA​​

    yNA​=0.000563 m=0.563 mm

    #Example (Compression + Bending)

    The link experiences eccentric loading as the forces do not act through the centroid.

    yNA​=38 mm

    I=868×10−9 m4

    d=0.038−0.01=0.028 m

    P=Fcentric​

    ∑MC​=0

    Pd−M=0

    M=0.028P

    σcentric​=0.003−P​=−333P

    σeccentric​=868×10−90.028P(−0.038)​=−1226P

    σeccentric​=868×10−90.028P(0.022)​=710P

    σA​=−333P+710P=377P

    σB​=−333P−1226P=−1559P

    30×106=377P

    P=79.6 kN

    120×106=1559P

    P=77.0 kN

    The maximum load that can be applied is 77.0kN.

    #Generalized Eccentric Axial Loading

    Consider a straight member subjected to equal and opposite eccentric forces.

    The eccentric force is equivalent to the system of a centric force and two moments about the y- and z-axis.

    P=Fcentric​

    My​=Pa

    Mz​=Pb

    💡
    σ=σcentric​±σMx​±σMy​
    💡
    σx​=AF​±Ixx​Mx​y​±Iyy​My​x​

    The above is just a general formula. The signs are to be determined through visualization.
