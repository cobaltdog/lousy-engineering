    Analysis of Beams

    #Bending in Beams

    Generally, loads are perpendicular to the axis of the beam, resulting in only bending and shear forces. When the loads are not perpendicular, the beam will experience axial forces as well.

    The transverse loading of a beam may consist of concentrated loads and/or distributed loads. When a load is constant over part of the beam, it is uniformly distributed over that part.

    Beams are categorized according to the way in which they are supported.

    A statically indeterminate beam is stable and all unknown forces can be determined from the equations of equilibrium alone. A statically indeterminate beam is one that is stable but contains more unknown forces than available equations of equilibrium.

    Consider a simply supported beam AB subjected to two concentrated loads and a uniformly distributed load. To determine the internal forces at C, we first draw a FBD of the entire beam to obtain the reactions at the supports.

    The bending moment M creates normal stresses in the cross section, while the shear force V creates shearing stresses in that section.

    In most cases, the main criteria is the maximum value of the normal stress in the beam.

    #Shear and Bending Moment Diagrams

    The SFD and BMD are obtained by determining the values of V and M at selected points of the beam. The values can be found by passing a section through the point where they are to be determined and using equations of equilibrium on either side of the section.

    To determine the direction of internal forces, we must define a sign convention.

    Description of sign convention:

    • Shear at any given point is positive if the shear forces create a clockwise rotation
    • Bending moment at any given point is positive if the moments causes the beam to sag

      The sign convention only applies to beams and not elements.

    #Load, Shear, and Bending Moment Relations

    Consider a simply supported beam AB carrying a distributed load w per unit length.

    Let C and C’ be two points of the beam at a distance Δx from each other.

    The distributed load is assumed to be acting on the centroid of the section.

    The shear and bending moment at both points are assumed to be positive.

    Shear at C=V

    Bending Moment at C=M

    Shear at C′=V+ΔV

    Bending Moment at C′=M+ΔM

    ∑Fy​=0

    V=(V+ΔV)−wΔx

    ΔV=−wΔx

    ΔxΔV​=−w

    💡
    dxdV​=−w

    The slope of the shear curve is negative, and its numerical value of the slope at any point is equal to the load per unit length at that point.

    ∑MC′​=0

    (M+ΔM)+wΔx(2Δx​)=M+VΔx

    ΔM=(V−21​wΔx)Δx

    ΔxΔM​=V−21​wΔx

    💡
    dxdM​=V

    The slope of the bending moment curve is equal to the value of the shear. This is true at any point where no concentrated load is applied.

    #Shearing in Beams

    A transverse load applied to a beam will result in normal and shear stresses in any given transverse section of the beam. The normal stresses are created by the bending moment and the shear stresses by the shear force.

    When shear stresses are exerted on the vertical faces of an element, equal stresses must be exerted on the horizontal faces of the same element. Thus, horizontal shear stresses must
    exist in any member subjected to a transverse loading.

    Understanding Stresses in BeamsIn this video we explore bending and shear stresses in beams. A bending moment is the resultant of bending stresses, which are normal stresses acting perpendicular to the beam cross-section. We can easily derive an equation for these bending stresses by observing how a beam deforms for a case of pure bending.https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f08Y39UiC-o

    #Horizontal Shear Stress

    Consider a cantilever beam made of separate planks clamped together at one end.

    When a load is applied to the free end of the beam, the planks are observed to slide with respect to each other.

    If a bending moment is applied instead, the planks will bend into concentric arcs of circle and will not slide with respect to each other.

    #Determination of Shear Stress

    Consider a constant cross section beam AB with a vertical plane of symmetry.

    At a distance x from end A, we detach an element CDD’C’ of length Δx extending across the width of the beam from the upper surface of the beam to a horizontal plane located at a distance y1​ from the neutral axis.

    The forces on the element consist of vertical shearing forces VC′​ and VD′​, a horizontal shear force ΔH exerted on the lower face of the element, and normal forces σC​dA and σD​dA.

    ∑Fx​=0

    ΔH+∫a​(σC​−σD​)dA=0

    Substituting σ=IMy​,

    ΔH=IMD​−MC​​∫a​ y dA

    ΔH=IΔM​∫a​ y dA

    Substituting ΔM=dxdM​⋅Δx=VΔx,

    ΔH=IVΔx​∫a​ y dA

    ΔH=IVΔx​⋅Q

    ΔH=IVQ​Δx

    Q is the first moment of area.

    The horizontal shear per unit length q can be obtained, or also known as shear flow.

    💡
    q=ΔxΔH​=IVQ​

    Observing that ΔA=tΔx where t is the thickness of the shear plane (width).

    💡
    τavg​=ΔAΔH​=ItVQ​

    Shear stress is zero on the upper and lower faces of the beam, since no forces are exerted on these faces. Shear stress is also zero at the end faces of the beam.

    The first moment of area of a beam is the area above the point of interest.

    💡
    Q=Ai​yˉ​i​

    #Shear in Narrow Rectangular Beams

    As long as the width of a rectangular beam remains small compared to its height, the shearing stress varies only slightly along its width, and is assumed to be constant.

    Let yˉ​ be the distance from the neutral axis to the centroid.

    yˉ​=21​(c−y)+y

    yˉ​=21​(c+y)

    Q=Ayˉ​

    Q=b(c−y)⋅21​(c+y)

    Q=21​b(c2−y2)

    I=12bh3​=12b(2c)3​=32​bc3

    Substitute Q and I into τavg​.

    For a rectangular beam, t=b.

    τ=ItVQ​=IbVQ​

    τ=2Vb(c2−y2)​×2b2c33​

    τ=2bc33V(c2−y2)​=2Ac23V(c2−y2)​

    Maximum shear stress occurs when y=0.

    💡
    τmax​=2A3V​

    The above formula only applies to narrow rectangular beams.
