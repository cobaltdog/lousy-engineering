    Stress Transformations

    The general state of stress at a given point may be represented by six components. Three of these components σx​, σy​, and σz​, define the normal stresses exerted on the faces of an element at the given point. The other three τxy​, τyz​, and τzx​, define the shearing stresses on the same element.

    #Transformation of Plane Stress

    Understanding Stress Transformation and Mohr's CircleIn this video, we're going to take a look at stress transformation and Mohr's circle. Stress transformation is a way of determining the normal and shear stress components acting at a specific location within a body, as we rotate the coordinate system we are using to observe the stresses.https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_DH3546mSCM&list=PLEYqyyrm-hQ3wtF34smyJSAOqUJqnf1ch&index=7

    #Stress Equations

    Let us assume a state of plane stress exists at point Q, which is defined by the stress components σx​, σy​, and τxy​. We would now like to determine the stress components
    σx′​, σy′​ and τxy′​ after the element has been rotated through an angle of θ about the z-axis.

    ∑Fx′​=0

    0=σx′​A−σx​Acosθcosθ−σy​Asinθsinθ−τxy​Acosθsinθ−τxy​Asinθcosθ

    0=σx′​−σx​(cosθ)2−σy​(sinθ)2−2τxy​sinθcosθ

    σx′​=σx​cos2θ−σy​sin2θ−2τxy​sinθcosθ

    σx′​=σx​(21+cos2θ​)+σy​(21−cos2θ​)+τxy​sin2θ

    σx′​=2σx​+σy​+(σx​−σy​)cos2θ​+τxy​sin2θ

    💡
    σx′​=2σx​+σy​​+2σx​−σy​​cos2θ+τxy​sin2θ

    The expression for σy′​ can be obtained by replacing θ with the angle θ+90°.

    cos(2θ+180°)=−cos2θ

    sin(2θ+180°)=−sin2θ

    💡
    σy′​=2σx​+σy​​−2σx​−σy​​cos2θ−τxy​sin2θ
    💡
    σx′​+σy′​=σx​+σy​

    The above equation verifies that the sum of normal stresses is independent of orientation.

    ∑Fy′​=0

    0=τx′y′​A+σx​Acosθsinθ−σy​Asinθcosθ−τxy​Acosθcosθ+τxy​Asinθsinθ

    0=τx′y′​+σx​(cosθ)sinθ−σy​(sinθ)cosθ−τxy​(cosθ)2+τxy​(sinθ)2

    τx′y′​=−(σx​−σy​)sinθcosθ+τxy​(cos2θ−sin2θ)

    💡
    τx′y′​=−2σx​−σy​​sin2θ+τxy​cos2θ

    #Principal Stresses

    Description of principal stresses:

    • Maximum and minimum normal stresses
    • Occurs when element is rotated such that shear stresses are zero

    Point A corresponds to the maximum value of normal stress σx′​. Point B corresponds to its minimum value.

    💡
    σavg​=2σx​+σy​​

    Both points also correspond to
    a zero value of shear stress τx′y′​.

    Principal plane of stress:

    💡
    tan2θp​=σx​−σy​2τxy​​

    The stresses on these principal planes are called principal stresses.

    Principal stresses:

    💡
    σ=2σx​+σy​​±(2σx​−σy​​)2+τxy​2​

    #Maximum Shear Stress

    Points D and E correspond to the maximum shear stress τx′y′​.

    Plane for maximum shear stress:

    💡
    tan2θs​=−2τxy​σx​−σy​​

    Corresponding maximum shear stress:

    💡
    τmax​=(2σx​−σy​​)2+τxy​2​

    Corresponding average normal stress:

    💡
    σavg​=2σx​+σy​​

    #Mohr’s Circle for Plane Stress

    Sign conventions:

    • θ is positive in the anticlockwise direction
    • Tensile stresses are positive while compressive stresses are negative
    • Shear stresses are positive if they tend to create an anticlockwise rotation

    Let point X be (σx​, −τxy​).

    Let point Y be (σy​, τxy​).

    Assuming τxy​ is positive shear stress,
    point X will be below the σ-axis while
    point Y will be above the σ-axis.

    Points A and B represents the principal stresses σmax​ and σmin​.

    Mohr’s circle can be used to verify that the planes of maximum shear stress are
    at 45° to the principal planes.

    If σmax​ and σmin​ are of opposite signs, there is only in-plane shear stress.

    If σmax​ and σmin​ are of the same signs, there is out-of-plane shear stress.

    When a body is stressed three-dimensionally, there exists 3 normal stresses and 6 shear stresses. If we consider only one plane (2D), the shear stress on that specific plane is called
    in-plane shear stress while the other two shear stresses are called out-of-plane shear stress.

    #Failure Criteria Under Plane Stress

    #Yield Criteria for Ductile Materials

    Structural elements and machine components made of a ductile material are usually designed so that the material will not yield under the expected loading conditions.

    If factor of safety is required, use FSσyield​​.

    #Tresca Criterion

    Recall that ductile materials usually fail by shear. The maximum value of shear stress under a centric axial load is equal to half the value of the corresponding normal, axial stress.

    The prove for the following equation can be found in the Stress chapter.

    💡
    τmax​=21​σyield​

    #Von Mises Criterion

    A given structural component is safe as long as the maximum value of the distortion energy per unit volume in the material is smaller than the distortion energy per unit volume required to cause yield in a tensile-test specimen of the same material.

    For two-dimensional elements, the yield stress is given by the following equation.

    💡
    σmax​2−σmax​σmin​+σmin​2<σyield​2

    #Example

    Coordinates of point X is (80,−25).

    Coordinates of point Y is (−40,25).

    Center of circle is located at (20, 0).

    R=602+252​=65 MPa

    σmax​=20+65=85 MPa

    σmin​=20−65=−45 MPa

    τmax​=65 MPa

    Tresca Criterion

    τmax​=21​(FSσy​​)

    65=21​(FS250​)

    FS=1.92

    Von Mises Criterion

    (σmax​)2−σmax​σmin​+(σmin​)2=(FSσy​​)2

    (85)2−85(−45)+(−45)2=(FS250​)2

    FS=2.19

    #Fracture Criteria for Brittle Materials

    Brittle materials are characterized by the fact that, when subjected to a tensile test, they fail suddenly through rupture or fracture, without any prior yielding.

    When a structural element or machine component made of a brittle material is under uniaxial tensile stress, the value of the normal stress that causes it to fail is equal to the ultimate strength of the material.

    💡
    ∣σmax​∣<σyield​
    💡
    ∣σmin​∣<σyield​

    #Stresses Under Combined Loading

    This section focuses on determining the maximum normal and shear stress on an object that is subjected to a combination of loads — axial, torsion, bending and shear.

    #Stresses due to Loads

    Stresses due to P1​

    Axial stress in the z-axis.

    Stresses due to P2​

    Bending moment about the x-axis.

    Shear due to bending along the y-axis.

    Torque about the z-axis.

    Stresses due to P1​

    σaxial​=AF​=AP1​​

    Stresses due to P2​

    σbending​=IMc​=I(P2​z)c​

    τbending​=ItVQ​=ItP2​Q​

    τtorque​=JTc​=J(P2​x)c​

    Element A

    σx​=0

    σy​=σaxial​+σbending​

    σy​=AP1​​+I(P2​z)c​

    τxz​=τtorque​

    τxz​=J(P2​x)c​

    Element B

    σx​=σaxial​

    σx​=AP1​​

    σy​=0

    τyz​=τtorque​+τbending​

    τyz​=J(P2​x)c​+ItP2​Q​

    #Stresses in Thin-Walled Pressure Vessels

    Thin walls offer little resistance to bending and thus, can be assumed that the internal forces exerted on a given portion of the wall are tangent to the surface of the vessel. The resulting stresses on an element of wall will be contained in a plane tangent to the surface.

    #Cylindrical Vessel

    Consider a cylinder of inner radius r and wall thickness t containing a fluid under pressure.

    The principal stresses are σ1​ and σ2​.

    σ1​ is known as the hoop stress.

    σ2​ is known as the longitudinal stress.

    Cut the vessel along the x-axis.

    We get internal forces σ1​ dA on the wall and pressure forces p dA from the fluid.

    p denotes the gauge pressure of the fluid.

    σ1​ dA=σ1​(2tΔx)

    p dA=p(2rΔx)

    ∑Fz​=0

    0=σ1​ dA−p dA

    0=σ1​(2tΔx)−p(2rΔx)

    💡
    σ1​=tpr​

    To determine σ2​, we cut the vessel perpendicular to the x-axis.

    We get internal forces σ2​ dA on the wall and pressure forces p dA from the fluid.

    σ2​ dA=σ2​(2πrt)

    p dA=p(πr2)

    ∑Fx​=0

    0=σ2​ dA−p dA

    0=σ2​(2πrt)−p(πr2)

    💡
    σ2​=2tpr​
    💡
    σ2​=21​σ1​

    Drawing Mohr’s circle through the points A and B that corresponds to σ1​ and σ2​.

    💡
    τmax(in−plane)​=2σ2​​=4tpr​

    This shear stress is exerted on the element within the plane tangent to the surface.

    💡
    τmax(out−of−plane)​=2σ1​​=2tpr​

    #Spherical Vessel

    Consider a sphere of inner radius r and wall thickness t containing a fluid under pressure.

    To determine the stresses, we cut through the center of the vessel and consider the free body.

    💡
    σ1​=σ2​=2tpr​

    Since the principal stresses are equal, Mohr’s circle for transformations of stress within the plane tangent to the surface reduces to a point.

    💡
    τmax​=2σ1​​=4tpr​
