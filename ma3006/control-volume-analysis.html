    Control Volume Analysis

    #Conservation of Mass

    #Derivation of Continuity Equation

    The continuity equation is key to tracking flowing fluid.

    💡
    Msys​=∫sys​ ρ dV

    The Reynolds transport theorem with B=mass and b=1 allows us to state that,

    💡
    dtdMsys​​=∂t∂​∫CV​ ρ dV+∫CS​ ρ(V⋅n) dA

    The time rate of change of the system mass can be expressed as the sum of the time rate of change of the mass of the contents of the control volume, and the net mass flowrate through the control surfaces.

    The net mass flow across the control surfaces is,

    ∫CS​ ρVcosθ dA=∑m˙out​−∑m˙in​

    If the integral is positive, the net flow is out of the control volume. If the integral is negative, the net flow is into.

    💡
    0=∂t∂​∫CV​ ρ dV+∫CS​ ρVcosθ dA

    m˙=ρV=ρAVavg​

    m˙=∫A​ ρVcosθ dA

    💡
    Vavg​=ρA∫A​ ρVcosθ dA​

    Average of the velocity component normal to the area.

    If the velocity is uniformly distributed over the area,

    💡
    Vavg​=V

    #Fixed, Non-Deforming Control Volume

    When a flow is steady, all field properties, including density, remain constant with time.
    The time rate of change of the mass of the contents of the control volume becomes zero.

    0=∂t∂​∫CV​ ρ dV+∫CS​ ρVcosθ dA

    0=0+∫CS​ ρVcosθ dA

    0=∑m˙out​−∑m˙in​

    💡
    ∑m˙out​=∑m˙in​

    If the steady flow is also incompressible,

    💡
    ∑Qout​=∑Qin​

    When the flow is unsteady, the instantaneous time rate of change of the mass of the contents of the control volume is not necessarily zero and can be an important variable.

    #Example 1

    V=10(62)=360 ft3

    Q=3360​=120 ft3/min

    Q=A1​Vin​=A2​Vout​

    Vin​=π(4/12)2120​=344 ft/min=5.73 ft/s

    Vout​=π(2/12)2120​=1375 ft/min=22.9 ft/s

    #Example 2

    m˙2​=m˙1​

    ρ2​A2​V2​=ρ1​A1​V1​

    Since the diameter of the pipe is constant, A2​=A1​.

    ρ2​V2​=ρ1​V1​

    V1​=V2​ρ1​ρ2​​

    Using the ideal gas law,

    ρ=RTp​

    V1​=V2​p1​T2​p2​T1​​

    V1​=1000[100(453)18.4(540)​]

    V1​=219 ft/s

    #Example 3

    0=∂t∂​∫CV​ ρd V+∫CS​ ρVcosθ dA

    0=[∂t∂​∫air​ ρair​ dVair​+∂t∂​∫CO2​​ ρCO2​​ dVCO2​​]+[−m˙CO2​​+m˙air​]

    The time rate of change of mass of the contents in the control volume must equal the mass flowrate out of the control volume. This applies to both the air mass and CO2​.

    Splitting the continuity equation into individual parts,

    0=∂t∂​∫air​ ρair​ dVair​+m˙air​

    0=∂t∂​∫CO2​​ ρCO2​​ dVCO2​​−m˙CO2​​

    From the equation for carbon dioxide, the first term can be simplified to,

    ∫CO2​​ ρCO2​​ dVCO2​​=ρCO2​​V=ρCO2​​[h(10)(5)]=ρCO2​​(50h)

    0=dtd​ ρCO2​​(50h)−m˙CO2​​

    50ρCO2​​dtdh​=m˙CO2​​

    dtdh​=50QCO2​​​

    dtdh​=5010​

    dtdh​=0.2 ft/min

    th=6​=0.26​=30 min

    #Moving, Non-Deforming Control Volume

    It is sometimes necessary to use a non-deforming control volume attached to a moving reference frame. Examples include control volumes containing a gas turbine engine on
    an aircraft in flight; the exhaust stack of a ship at sea; and the gasoline tank of a car.

    💡
    Vabs​=W+VCV​

    V is absolute velocity as seen by a stationary observer in a fixed coordinate system.
    W is the relative velocity as seen by an observer moving with the control volume.
    VCV​ is the control volume velocity as seen from a fixed coordinate system.

    The Reynolds transport theorem for a moving control volume is,

    💡
    dtdMsys​​=∂t∂​∫CV​ ρ dV+∫CS​ ρWcosθ dA

    The continuity equation for a moving control volume is,

    💡
    0=∂t∂​∫CV​ ρ dV+∫CS​ ρWcosθ dA

    #Example

    The control volume surrounds the engine and its contents, and includes all fluids involved at an instant. The flow relative to a moving control volume is considered steady on a time-average basis.

    0=dtd​∫CV​ ρ dV+∫CS​ ρWcosθ dA

    0=∫CS​ ρWcosθ dA

    0=m˙2​−m˙1​−m˙fuel​

    m˙fuel​=ρ2​A2​W2​−ρ1​A1​W1​

    The intake velocity W1​ is equal in magnitude to the velocity of the aircraft.

    The exhaust velocity W2​ is the velocity relative to the control volume.

    V=W2​+VCV​

    V=W2​+Vplane​

    1050=W2​+(−971)

    W2​=2021 km/h

    m˙fuel​=0.515(0.558)(2021×103)−0.736(0.8)(971×103)

    m˙fuel​=9050 kg/hr

    #Momentum Equation

    #Linear Momentum

    From Newton’s second law, F=ma=dtd​ mV. Thus, let B=mV and b=V.

    Note that b is an intensive property that is independent of mass.

    Substituting B and b into the Reynolds transport theorem,

    dtd​ mV=∂t∂​∫CV​ Vρ dV+∫CS​ Vρ(V⋅n) dA

    💡
    ∑F=∂t∂​∫CV​ Vρ dV+∫CS​ Vρ(V⋅n) dA

    Note that rate of change of linear momentum is force.

    Assume the flow is steady, the first integral becomes zero.

    ∑F=∫CS​ Vρ(V⋅n) dA

    ∑F=∫out​ Vρ(V⋅n) dA+∫in​ Vρ(V⋅n) dA

    Assume V⊥dA,

    ∑F=∫out​ Vρ(V) dA+∫in​ Vρ(−V) dA

    ∑F=∫out​ V(ρV dA)−∫in​ V(ρV dA)

    Assume V is uniform across the inlets and outlets,

    💡
    ∑F=∑(m˙V)out​−∑(m˙V)in​

    Note that ∫ρV dA=m˙.

    #Example 1

    Mass flowrate of water,

    m˙=ρAV

    m˙=1000(0.05)(2)

    m˙=100 kg/s

    Assume FH​ and FV​ are in the positive directions.

    Linear momentum in the x-direction,

    ∑Fx​=m˙(V2x​−V1x​)

    FH​+P1,gauge​A1​=m˙(V2x​−V1x​)

    FH​+(5×103)(0.05)=100(−2)

    FH​=−450 N (to the left)

    Linear momentum in the y-direction,

    ∑Fy​=m˙(V2y​−V1y​)

    FV​+P2,gauge​A2​−mg=m˙(V2y​−V1y​)

    FV​+(16.31×103)(0.1)−100(9.81)=100(−1)

    FV​=−750 N (downwards)

    Note that the sign of the velocity vector is dependent on its direction. If it’s
    pointing in the positive direction of the axis, it is positive and vice versa.

    #Part (a)

    water relative to the control volume.

    #Angular Momentum

    From Newton’s second law, linear momentum is defined as mV=(ρ dV)V.
    Then, angular momentum, which is moment of linear momentum, is r×mV.
    Thus, let B=r×mV and b=r×V.

    Substituting into the Reynolds transport theorem,

    dtd​ r×mV=∂t∂​∫CV​ (r×V)ρ dV+∫CS​ (r×V)ρ(V⋅n) dA

    💡
    ∑T=∂t∂​∫CV​ (r×V)ρ dV+∫CS​ (r×V)ρ(V⋅n) dA

    Note that rate of change of angular momentum is moment.

    Assume the flow is steady, the first integral becomes zero.

    ∑T=∫CS​ (r×V)ρ(V⋅n) dA

    ∑T=∫out​ (r×V)ρ(V⋅n) dA+∫in​ (r×V)ρ(V⋅n) dA

    Assume V⊥dA,

    ∑T=∫out​ (r×V)ρV dA              +∫in​ (r×V)ρ(−V) dA

    ∑T=∫out​ (r×V)ρV dA              −∫in​ (r×V)ρV dA

    Assume V is uniform across the inlets and outlets,

    💡
    ∑T=∑[m˙(r×V)]out​−∑[m˙(r×V)]in​

    Note that ∫ρV dA=m˙.

    #Example 1

    #Part (a)

    Mass flowrate into sprinkler,

    m˙1​=ρAV

    m˙1​=1000(1000×10−6)

    m˙1​=1 kg/s

    Mass flowrate out of nozzles,

    m˙2​=m˙3​=21​m˙1​

    m˙2​=m˙3​=0.5 kg/s

    Velocity of water out of nozzles,

    V2​=V3​=ρAm˙​

    V2​=V3​=1000(0.00003)0.5​

    V2​=V3​=16.7 m/s

    Using the angular momentum equation,

    ∑T=∑[m˙(r×V)]out​−∑[m˙(r×V)]in​

    Tshaft​=∑[m˙(r×V)]out​−∑[m˙(r×V)]in​

    Tshaft​=m˙2​(r×V)+m˙3​(r×V)

    Tshaft​=m˙2​(−rV2​k)+m˙3​(−rV3​k)

    Tshaft​=−0.5(0.2)(16.7)−0.5(0.2)(16.7)

    Tshaft​=−3.33 Nm (clockwise moment)

    Note that the cross product r×V is negative as the
    resultant vector points downwards (right-hand rule).

    #Part (b)

    Tangential velocity of nozzles,

    U=rω

    U=0.2(500×602π​)

    Tshaft​=m˙2​(−rV2​k)+m˙3​(−rV3​k)

    Tshaft​=−0.5(0.2)(6.1947)−0.5(0.2)(6.1947)

    #Energy Equation

    Reynolds Transport Theorem (Energy)How to apply the Reynolds Transport Theorem to the conservation of energy equation.https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Edh-imbzrPc

    From the first law of thermodynamics, energy of a system is E=U+KE+PE.
    Expressing on a per unit mass basis, the equation becomes e=u+21​V2+gz.

    Let B=E and b=e. Substituting into the Reynolds transport theorem,

    💡
    dtd​ E=∂t∂​∫CV​ eρ dV+∫CS​ eρ(V⋅n) dA

    Total energy of a system is dependent on:

    • Rate of heat transfer Q˙​net,in​ into the system
    • Rate of work done W˙net,in​ on the system

    The heat transfer rate represents ways in which energy is exchanged between the control
    volume contents and surroundings due to a temperature difference. The work transfer rate (power) represents the work done on the contents of the control volume by the surroundings.

    Substituting E˙=Q˙​net,in​+W˙net,in​ into the above,

    💡
    (Q˙​net,in​+W˙net,in​)CV​=∂t∂​∫CV​ eρ dV+∫CS​ eρ(V⋅n) dA

    Assume the control volume is adiabatic,

    W˙in​−W˙out​=∂t∂​∫CV​ eρ dV+∫CS​ eρ(V⋅n) dA

    Assume the flow is steady, the first integral becomes zero.

    W˙in​−W˙out​=∫out​ eρ(V⋅n) dA+∫in​ eρ(V⋅n) dA

    Assume V⊥dA and velocity is uniform,

    W˙in​−W˙out​=∫out​ eρV dA−∫in​ eρV dA

    💡
    W˙in​−W˙out​=∑(m˙e)out​−∑(m˙e)in​

    Note that ∫ρV dA=m˙.

    Recall from thermodynamics that
    total energy of a flowing fluid is,

    e=u+Pv+21​V2+gz

    Note that fluid entering or leaving a CV
    possesses an additional form of energy     Pv.

    The average energy is determined using:

    • Pavg​ — fluid pressure at the pipe centerline
    • Vavg​ — flow velocity based on Q=AVavg​
    • zavg​ — fluid height at the pipe centerline

    Substituting into the above equation,

    💡
    W˙in​−W˙out​=∑[m˙(ρP​+21​V2+gz)]out​−∑[m˙(ρP​+21​V2+gz)]in​

    Dividing the equation by m˙g, it can be expressed in terms of height.

    💡
    hin​−hout​=(ρgP​+2gV2​+z)out​−(ρgP​+2gV2​+z)in​

    where hin​ represents head gain and hout​ represents head loss,
    1st term on the LHS represents energy head entering control volume
    2nd term on the LHS represents energy head leaving control volume

    #Example 1

    Using the energy equation,

    hpump​−hL​=(ρgPB​​+2gVB​2​+zB​)−(ρgPA​​+2gVA​2​+zA​)

    hpump​−hL​=zB​−zA​

    hpump​−4.6=19.2−10

    hpump​=13.8 m

    Note that gauge pressure at the water surface is zero since the tanks are open to
    the atmosphere. Also, since the tank is large, velocity at the water surface is zero.

    Volume flowrate of water,

    ηpump​=Wmech​Win​​=W˙mech​m˙ghpump​​=W˙mech​ρghpump​Q​

    0.75=10×1031000(9.81)(13.8)Q​

    Q=0.0554 m3/s

    Note that m˙=ρV˙=ρAV=ρQ.

    Power loss of system,

    W˙L​=ρghL​Q

    W˙L​=1000(9.81)(4.6)(0.0554)

    W˙L​=2500 W
