    The Bernoulli Equation

    #Newton’s Second Law

    As a fluid particle moves from one location to another, it usually experiences an acceleration. The fluid motion is assumed to be governed by pressure and gravity forces only.

    #Streamlines

    The motion of each fluid particle can be described by its velocity vector V. For steady
    flows, each particle slides along a path (streamline), which is always tangent to the path.

    The particle’s motion can be described in terms of its distance s=s(t) and the local radius of curvature of the streamline R=R(s). The radius of curvature is related to
    the shape of the streamline.

    Acceleration along a streamline,

    as​=dtdV​

    as​=∂s∂V​dtds​

    💡
    as​=∂s∂V​V

    Acceleration normal to a streamline,

    💡
    an​=RV2​

    For incompressible flow, velocity is inversely proportional to the streamline spacing. Thus, converging streamlines produce positive stream-wise acceleration. To produce this acceleration, there must
    be a non-zero net force on the fluid particle.

    To determine the forces necessary to produce a given flow, consider the FBD of a
    small fluid particle. The particle of interest is removed from its surroundings, and
    the reactions of the surroundings on the particles are labelled accordingly.

    #Along a Streamline

    Consider a small fluid particle of size ds by dn.

    The component of Newton’s second law in the streamline direction is,

    ∑dFs​=dm as​

    ∑dFs​=ρ dV⋅∂s∂V​V

    The component of weight in the streamline direction is,

    dWs​=−dWs​ sinθ

    dWs​=−γ dVsinθ

    Due to fluid weight, pressure is not constant throughout a stationary fluid. Likewise, in a flowing fluid, pressure is usually not constant. In general, for steady flow, p=p(s,n).

    dps​=∂s∂p​2ds​

    The net pressure force in the streamline direction is,

    dFps​​=(p−dps​)dndy−(p+dps​)dndy

    dFps​​=−2 dps​ dndy

    dFps​​=−∂s∂p​ dsdndy

    dFps​​=−∂s∂p​ dV

    The equation of motion along the streamline direction is,

    dWs​+dFps​​=dFs​

    −γ dVsinθ−∂s∂p​ dV=ρ dV⋅∂s∂V​V

    −γsinθ−∂s∂p​=ρ⋅∂s∂V​V

    💡
    −γsinθ−∂s∂p​=ρas​

    A change in fluid particle velocity requires the appropriate combination of particle weight
    and pressure gradient along the streamline. In a flowing fluid, the weight and pressure
    forces do not necessarily balance, thus     as​=0 and there is particle motion.

    −γdsdz​−dsdp​=ρ 21​dsd(V2)​

    dp+21​ρ d(V2)+γ dz=0

    ∫dp+∫21​ρ d(V2)+∫γ dz=∫0

    💡
    p+21​ρV2+ρgz=constant

    The 1st term is called the static pressure, 2nd term dynamic pressure, and 3rd term hydrostatic pressure. The first two terms are also referred to as stagnation pressure.

    Dividing the pressure form of Bernoulli’s equation by ρg,

    💡
    ρgp​+2gV2​+z=constant

    Terms are referred to as pressure head, velocity head, and elevation head respectively.

    Assumptions of Bernoulli’s equation:

    • Applicable along a streamline
    • Density of fluid is constant
    • No heat or work input
    • Flow is assumed to be steady
    • Flow is assumed to be incompressible
    • Viscous effects are assumed negligible

    #Normal to a Streamline

    Consider the forces on the fluid particle in the direction normal to the streamline.

    The component of Newton’s second law in the normal direction is,

    ∑dFn​=dm an​

    ∑dFn​=ρ dV RV2​

    The component weight in the normal direction is,

    dWn​=−dWn​ cosθ

    dWn​=−γ dVcosθ

    The net pressure force in the normal direction is,

    dFpn​​=(p−dpn​)dsdy−(p+dpn​)dsdy

    dFpn​​=−2 dpn​ dsdy

    dFpn​​=−∂n∂p​ dsdndy

    dFpn​​=−∂n∂p​ dV

    The equation of motion along the normal direction is,

    dFn​=dWn​+dFpn​​

    −γ dVcosθ−∂n∂p​ dV=ρ dV RV2​

    💡
    −γcosθ−∂n∂p​=ρ RV2​

    A change in the direction of flow of a fluid particle requires an appropriate
    combination of particle weight and pressure gradient normal to the streamline.

    −γdndz​−dndp​=RρV2​

    dndp​+RρV2​+γdndz​=0

    dp+RρV2​ dn+γ dz=0

    ∫dp+∫RρV2​ dn+∫γ dz=∫0

    💡
    p+ρ∫RV2​ dn+ρgz=constant

    Alternatively, the equation of motion can be simplified to,

    −γdndz​−dndp​=RρV2​

    ​dn1​ (γ dz+dp)=−RρV2​

    💡
    dnd(p+γz)​=−RρV2​

    Since positive n direction is taken towards the center of the curvature, pressure
    decreases as radius decreases — pressure is lower closer to the center of a vortex.

    #Interpretation of Bernoulli’s Equation

    Each term in the Bernoulli equation can be interpreted as a form of pressure.

    💡
    p+21​ρV2+ρgz=constant

    #Static, Dynamic, and Stagnation Pressure

    The first term p is the actual thermodynamic pressure of the fluid as it flows. To measure
    its value, one could move along with the fluid, thus being “static” relative to the moving fluid. Hence, the term static pressure.

    The second term 21​ρV2 is the dynamic pressure. As the fluid is brought to a rest at the
    end of a small tube inserted into the flow and pointing upstream, the fluid will fill the
    tube to a height of H — stagnation pressure.

    Static pressure at point (1)

    p3​=p4​+γh4−3​

    p1​=p3​+γh3−1​

    p1(abs)​=p4​+γh

    p1(gage)​=γh

    Stagnation pressure at point (2)

    p2​=p1​+21​ρV1​2

    The third term ρgz is the hydrostatic pressure as a result of elevation changes.
    If elevation effects are neglected, stagnation pressure is the largest pressure
    obtainable along a given streamline.

    #Pitot-Static Tube

    Description of a pitot-static tube:

    • Two concentric tubes attached to two pressure gages (or a differential gage)
    • Center tube measures stagnation pressure at its open tip
    • Holes on outer tube measure static pressure

    p4​=p1​=p

    p3​=p+21​ρV2

    p3​−p4​=21​ρV2

    💡
    V=ρ2(p3​−p4​)​​

    An accurate measurement of static pressure requires that none of the fluid’s kinetic
    energy is converted into a pressure rise at the point of measurement. This requires
    a smooth hole with no burrs or imperfections.

    #Applications of the Bernoulli Equation

    Between any two points, on a streamline in steady, inviscid, incompressible flow,

    💡
    p1​+21​ρV1​2+ρgz1​=p2​+21​ρV2​2+ρgz2​

    #Free Jets

    #Vertical Nozzle

    Consider a jet of liquid with diameter d that flows from a nozzle with velocity V.

    Using Bernoulli’s equation between points (1) and (2) on the streamline,

    ρgh=21​ρV2

    💡
    V=2gh​

    Based on the equation, p+ρ∫RV2​ dn+ρgz=constant, if the streamlines at the tip of the nozzle are straight, R=∞, it follows that p2​=p4​. Since (4) is on the surface of the jet, p4gage​​=0. Thus, p2gage​​=0.

    Outside of the nozzle, the stream continues to fall as a free jet with zero pressure throughout. Between points (1) and (5), velocity increases,

    💡
    V=2g(h+H)​

    #Horizontal Nozzle

    For a horizontal nozzle, velocity of the fluid at the centerline will be slightly greater
    than that at the top, and slightly less than that at the bottom, due to differences in elevation.

    If the exit is not a smooth, well-contoured nozzle, the diameter of the jet will be less than the diameter of the hole . This results in the vena contracta effect, due to the inability of the fluid to turn the sharp 90° corner.

    The highest pressure occurs along the centerline, and the lowest pressure at the
    edge of the jet, where p1​=p3​=0. Velocity of the streamlines is assumed to
    be uniform in the plane of the vena contracta, at section a-a.

    #Confined Flows

    Consider a fluid flowing through a fixed volume that has one inlet and one outlet.
    If the flow is steady (no accumulation of fluid within the volume), the rate of fluid
    flow into the volume must equal the rate of fluid flow out of the volume.

    m1​=m2​

    ρ1​V1​=ρ2​V2​

    ρ1​A1​V1​ dt=ρ2​A2​V2​ dt

    💡
    A1​V1​=A2​V2​

    If fluid density remains constant, the equation becomes the continuity equation.

    #Example 1

    Using Bernoulli’s equation between points (1) and (2),

    p1​+21​ρV1​2+ρgz1​=p2​+21​ρV2​2+ρgz2​

    21​ρV1​2+ρgh=21​ρV2​2

    21​V1​2+gh=21​V2​2

    Using the continuity equation,

    V1​A1​=V2​A2​

    V1​(πr1​2)=V2​(πr2​2)

    V1​=V2​(r1​r2​​)2

    V1​=V2​(Dd​)2

    21​V2​2(Dd​)4+gh=21​V2​2

    2gh=V2​2[1−(Dd​)4]

    V2​=1−(0.20.01​)42(9.81)(0.2)​​

    V2​=1.9809 m/s

    Q1​=Q2​=V2​A2​

    Q1​=1.9809π(20.01​)2

    Q1​=1.56×10−4 m3/s

    #Part (a)

    p1​=21​ρV3​2

    V3​=ρ2p1​​​

    #Part (b)

    p1​+21​ρV1​2+ρgz1​=p2​+21​ρV2​2+ρgz2​

    p1​=p2​+21​ρV2​2

    p2​=p1​−21​ρV2​2

    p1​−p2​=ρg(z2​−z1​)+21​ρ(V2​2−V1​2)

    p2​=p1​−ρg(z2​−z1​)−ρg(l+h)+SG⋅ρgh+ρgl

    #Flowrate Measurement

    The Bernoulli equation can be used to measure fluid velocities and flow rates. An
    effective way to measure flowrate is to measure the pressure difference between
    the low-velocity, high-pressure upstream region, and high-velocity, low-pressure downstream region.

    Assuming the flow is horizontal, z1​=z2​, steady, inviscid, and incompressible.

    Q=V1​A1​=V2​A2​

    V1​=V2​A1​A2​​

    p1​+21​ρV1​2=p2​+21​ρV2​2

    p1​+21​ρV2​2(A1​A2​​)2=p2​+21​ρV2​2

    2(p1​−p2​)=ρV2​2[1−(A1​A2​​)2]

    💡
    V2​=ρ[1−(A1​A2​​)2]2(p1​−p2​)​​
    💡
    Q=A2​ρ[1−(A1​A2​​)2]2(p1​−p2​)​​

    Thus, for a given flow geometry, flowrate can be determined if the pressure difference is known. The actual measured flowrate will be smaller than the theoretical result due to differences between the “real world” and the assumptions made.

    #Example

    ρkero​=0.85(1000)=850 kg/m3

    Q=A2​ρ[1−(A1​A2​​)2]2(p1​−p2​)​​

    p1​−p2​=2A2​2Q2ρ[1−(A2​/A1​)2]​

    Pressure Difference for Smallest Flowrate

    Δpmin​=2[π(0.03)]2(0.005)2(850)[1−(0.03/0.05)4]​

    Δpmin​=1157 Pa

    Pressure Difference for Largest Flowrate

    Δpmax​=2[π(0.03)]2(0.05)2(850)[1−(0.03/0.05)4]​

    Δpmax​=115.7 kPa

    Range in Pressure Difference

    1.157 kPa≤p1​−p2​≤115.7 kPa

    The flowrate varies as the square root of the pressure difference. A 10x increase in flowrate requires a 100x increase in pressure difference. This non-linear relationship
    can cause difficulties — pressure transducers with a wide range of operation.

    #Vapor Pressure and Cavitation

    If differences in fluid velocity are considerable, the differences in pressure can also
    be considerable. For gases, this may introduce compressibility effects. For liquids,
    this may result in cavitation.

    An increase in fluid velocity will result in a decrease in static pressure, assuming no differences due to elevation. If the pressure decrease is large enough, the pressure
    in the liquid can be reduced to its vapor pressure.

    #Example

    p1​+21​ρV1​2+ρgz1​=p3​+21​ρV3​2+ρgz3​

    g(z1​−z3​)=21​V3​2

    V3​=2g(z1​−z3​)​

    V3​=2(32.2)[15−(−5)]​

    V3​=35.889 ft/s

    From the continuity equation,

    V2​A2​=V3​A3​

    V2​=V3​=35.889 ft/s

    For cavitation to not occur, p2​≥pvapor​.

    Vapor pressure of water at 60°F is 0.256 psia.

    p2​=0.256 psia

    p2gage​​=0.256−14.7

    p2gage​​=−14.444 psi=−2079.9 lb/ft2

    p1​+21​ρV1​2+ρgz1​=p2​+21​ρV2​2+ρgH

    p2​=ρg(z1​−H)−21​ρV2​2

    p2​=γ(z1​−H)−21​ρV2​2

    −2079.9=62.4(15−H)−21​(1.94)(35.889)2

    H=28.3 ft

    For larger values of H, vapor bubbles will form and the siphon action may stop.
