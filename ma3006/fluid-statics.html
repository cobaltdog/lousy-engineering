    #Pressure at a Point

    Fluid Mechanics - Fluid/Hydrostatic Pressure in 11 Minutes!https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6btDiYZ5xa0

    Consider a wedge-shaped element exposed on all sides to a fluid. The dimensions dx, dy and dz are small and tend to zero as the element shrinks to a point. The only forces acting on the element are pressure and gravity.

    #Pascal’s Law

    Applying Newton’s second law F=ma. The net force equals zero in a static fluid.

    ∑Fy​=py​dxdz−ps​dxds sinθ=may​

    ∑Fy​=py​dxdz−ps​dxds sinθ=ρ2dxdydz​ay​

    0=py​dxdz−ps​dxds sinθ

    ∑Fz​=pz​dxdy−ps​dxds cosθ−γ(21​dxdydz)=maz​

    ∑Fz​=pz​dxdy−ps​dxds cosθ−γ2dxdydz​=ρ2dxdydz​az​

    0=pz​dxdy−ps​dxds cosθ−γ2dxdydz​

    Using geometry, dz=ds sinθ and dy=ds cosθ.

    0=py​dx(ds sinθ)−ps​dx(ds sinθ)

    0=py​−ps​

    💡
    ps​=py​

    0=pz​dx(ds cosθ)−ps​dx(ds cosθ)−γ2dxdz(ds cosθ)​

    0=pz​−ps​−γ2dz​

    💡
    ps​=pz​

    The third term can be neglected as it is very small in comparison to the other terms.

    Thus, the pressure at a point in a fluid at rest, or in motion, is independent of direction as
    long as there are no shearing stresses present. This observation is known as Pascal’s law.

    #Equation of Pressure Field

    Consider a rectangular element of fluid removed from some arbitrary position within the mass of fluid of interest. The only forces acting on the element are surface forces due to pressure and a body force equal to the weight of the element.

    Let the pressure at the center of the element be p.

    dFz​=(p−∂z∂p​2dz​)dxdy−(p+∂z∂p​2dz​)dxdy−γ(dxdydz)

    dFz​=−∂z∂p​dxdydz−γ dxdydz

    Using Newton’s second law,

    maz​=−∂z∂p​dxdydz−γ dxdydz

    ρ(dxdydz)az​=−∂z∂p​dxdydz−γ dxdydz

    ρaz​=−∂z∂p​−γ

    ∂z∂p​=−ρ(g+az​)

    Similarly, resolving the y-components,

    dFy​=(p−∂y∂p​2dy​)dxdz−(p+∂y∂p​2dy​)dxdz

    dFy​=−∂y∂p​dxdydz

    ρ(dxdydz)ay​=−∂y∂p​dxdydz

    ∂y∂p​=−ρay​

    Thus, the pressure variation in the various directions are,

    💡
    ∂x∂p​=−ρax​               ∂y∂p​=−ρay​               ∂z∂p​=−ρ(g+az​)

    The resultant surface forces can be expressed in vector form.

    dFs​=dFx​i+dFy​j+dFz​k

    dFs​=−(∂x∂p​i+∂y∂p​j+∂z∂p​k)dxdydz

    The group of terms in the parentheses represents the pressure gradient.

    💡
    ∇p=∂x∂p​i+∂y∂p​j+∂z∂p​k

    The weight of the element can be expressed as,

    −dWk=−γ dxdydz k

    Using Newton’s second law,

    dFs​−dWk=dm a

    −∇p dxdydz−γ dxdydz k=ρ dxdydz a

    💡
    −∇p−γk=ρa

    #Pressure Variation in a Fluid at Rest

    For a fluid at rest, a=0.

    💡
    ∇p+γk=0

    Expressing the above equation in vector form,

    ∂x∂p​i+∂y∂p​j+∂z∂p​k=−γk

    ∂z∂p​=−γ

    For a fluid at rest, pressure varies only in the z-direction.

    💡
    dzdp​=−γ

    Since p depends only on z, the equation can be written as an ordinary differential equation. Thus, pressure at any point on a horizontal plane is constant.

    #Incompressible Fluid

    In general, a fluid with constant density is called an incompressible fluid. For liquids, the variation in density is usually negligible. Thus, the assumption of constant specific weight for liquids is valid.

    ∫p1​p2​​dp=−γ∫z2​z1​​dz

    p2​−p1​=−γ(z2​−z1​)

    p1​−p2​=γ(z2​−z1​)

    💡
    p1​=p2​+γh

    This type of pressure distribution is called a hydrostatic distribution. For an incompressible fluid at rest, the pressure varies linearly with depth. The pressure must increase with depth
    to “hold up” the fluid above it.

    Pressure head h is the height of a column of fluid required to give a pressure difference.

    💡
    h=γp1​−p2​​

    When one works with liquids, there is often a free surface, and it is convenient to use this surface as a reference plane.

    The reference pressure p0​ would correspond
    to the pressure acting on the free surface.

    💡
    p=p0​+γh

    The pressure in a homogenous, incompressible fluid at rest depends on the depth of the fluid relative to some reference plane, and is not influenced by the size of shape of the container.

    #Measurement of Pressure

    Absolute pressure is measured relative to a perfect vacuum, whereas gage pressure is measured relative to the local atmospheric pressure. Absolute pressure are always positive but gage pressures can be either positive or negative.

    #Piezometer Tube

    The simplest type of manometer consists of a vertical tube, open at the top, and attached to the container in which the pressure is desired.

    Since the tube is open at the top, the pressure measured with height h1​ is gage pressure.

    Disadvantages of piezometer tube:

    • Suitable only if pressure in the container is greater than atmospheric pressure
    • Pressure to be measured must be small so the height of column is reasonable
    • Fluid in the container in which the pressure is to be measured must be a liquid

    #U-Tube Manometer

    The fluid in the manometer is called the gage fluid. To find pA​ in terms of the various column heights, we start at one end of the system and work our way to the other end.

    0=pA​+γ1​h1​−γ2​h2​

    pA​=γ2​h2​−γ1​h1​

    If pA​ is large, then a heavy gage fluid, such as mercury, can be used and a reasonable column height can still be maintained. Alternatively, if pA​ is small, a lighter gage fluid can be used such that a relatively large column height can be achieved.

    The U-tube manometer is also widely used to measure the difference in pressure between two containers or two points in a system.

    pA​+γ1​h1​−γ2​h2​−γ3​h3​=pB​

    pA​−pB​=γ2​h2​+γ3​h3​−γ1​h1​

    #Example 1

    p1​=p2​=pair​+ρoil​g(h1​+h2​)

    0=p2​−ρHg​gh3​

    0=pair​+ρoil​g(h1​+h2​)−ρHg​gh3​

    0=pair​+(SGoil​)(ρwater​g)(h1​+h2​)−(SGHg​)(ρwater​g)h3​

    pair​=−0.9(62.4)(1236+6​)+13.6(62.4)(129​)

    pair​=440 lb/ft2

    pgage​=3.06 psi

    #Example 2

    pA​−γ1​h1​−γ2​h2​+γ1​(h1​+h2​)=pB​

    pA​−pB​=γ2​h2​+γ1​h1​−γ1​(h1​+h2​)

    pA​−pB​=h2​(γ2​−γ1​)

    pA​−pB​=0.5(15.6−9.8)

    pA​−pB​=2.90 kPa

    It is to be noted that the only column height of importance is the differential reading h2​. The differential manometer could be placed at any height above the pipe and the value of h2​ would remain the same.

    #Inclined-Tube Manometer

    To measure small pressure changes, an inclined-tube manometer is used. One leg of the manometer is inclined at an angle θ, and the differential reading l2​ is measured along the inclined tube.

    pA​+γ1​h1​−γ2​l2​sinθ−γ3​h3​=pB​

    pA​−pB​=γ2​l2​sinθ+γ3​h3​−γ1​h1​

    If pipes A and B contain a gas,

    pA​−pB​=γ2​l2​sinθ

    l2​=γ2​sinθpA​−pB​​

    The differential reading l2​ of an inclined-tube manometer can be increased over that obtained with a conventional U-tube manometer by the factor sinθ1​.

    #Hydrostatic Force on a Surface

    When a surface is submerged in a fluid, forces develop on the surface due to the fluid.

    #Hydrostatic Force on a Plane Surface

    Consider an inclined plane surface submerged in a fluid. We will assume that the fluid surface is open to the atmosphere. Let the plane in which the surface lies intersect the
    free surface at 0 and make an angle θ with this surface.

    dF=p dA

    dF=γh dA

    dF=γysinθ dA

    FR​=∫γh dA

    FR​=∫γysinθ dA

    FR​=γsinθ∫y dA

    The integral ∫y dA is the first moment of area with respect to the x axis, so we can write ∫y dA=yc​A.

    💡
    FR​=γ(yc​sinθ)A=γhc​A

    Although intuition might suggest that the resultant force should pass through the centroid of the area, this is not actually the case. The y coordinate yR​ of the resultant force can be determined by summation of moments about the x axis. The moment of the resultant force must equal the moment of the distributed pressure force.

    Taking moments about O,

    FR​⋅yR​=∫y dF

    FR​⋅yR​=∫y(γysinθ dA)

    FR​⋅yR​=∫γy2sinθ dA

    γ(yc​sinθ)A⋅yR​=γsinθ∫y2 dA

    yR​=yc​A∫y2 dA​

    The integral ∫y2 dA is the second moment of area with respect to an axis formed by the intersection of the plane containing the surface and the free surface (x axis).

    💡
    yR​=yc​AIx​​

    The second moment of area is with respect to the free surface.

    Using the parallel axis theorem, we can express Ix​ as,

    💡
    Ix​=Ixc​​+Ayc​2

    Ixc​​ is the second moment of area with respect to an axis passing through the centroid.

    yR​=Ayc​Ixc​​+Ayc​2​

    💡
    yR​=yc​AIxc​​​+yc​

    The resultant force is always located below the centroid since Ixc​/Ayc​>0.

    The x coordinate xR​ can be determined similarly by summing moments about the y axis.

    FR​⋅xR​=∫γxysinθ dA

    xR​=Ayc​∫xy dA​=Ayc​Ixy​​

    💡
    xR​=yc​AIxyc​​​+xc​

    The point through which the resultant force acts is called the center of pressure. As yc​ increases, the center of pressure moves closer to the centroid of the area.

    Since yc​=sinθhc​​, the distance yc​ will increase if the depth of submergence hc​ increases. Or, for a given depth, the area is rotated such that the angle θ decreases.

    Thus, the hydrostatic force on the right-hand side of the gate acts closer to the centroid of the gate than the force on the left-hand side.

    #Example 1

    FR​=γhc​A=γhc​(πr2)

    FR​=9800(10)π(2)2=1.2315 MN

    yR​=Ayc​Ixc​​​+yc​

    yR​=π(2)2(sin60°10​)4π(2)4​​+sin60°10​

    yR​=11.634 m (from axis)

    For the coordinate system shown, xR​=0 since the area is symmetrical, and the center of pressure must lie along the diameter.

    Mc​=FR​(yR​−yc​)

    Mc​=(1.2315×106)(11.634−sin60°10​)

    Mc​=106.65 kNm

    #Example 2

    FR​=γhc​A

    FR​=64(0.9)[21​(0.3)(0.3)]

    FR​=2.592 lb

    Ixyc​​=720.3(0.3)2​(0.3)=720.0081​ ft4

    xR​=Ayc​Ixyc​​​+xc​

    xR​=21​(0.3)2(0.9)0.0081/72​

    xR​=0.00278 ft (from xc​)

    Ixc​​=360.3(0.3)3​=360.0081​ ft4

    yR​=Ayc​Ixc​​​+yc​

    yR​=21​(0.3)2(0.9)0.0081/36​+0.9

    yR​=0.906 ft (from axis)

    The center of pressure is 0.00278ft to the right of and 0.00556ft below the centroid.

    #Pressure Prism of a Plane Surface

    Consider the pressure distribution along a vertical wall of a tank of constant width b,
    which contains a liquid having a specific weight γ. The pressure is equal to zero at
    the upper surface and equal to γh at the bottom.

    The average pressure occurs at the depth h/2 and, therefore, the resultant force acts on the rectangular area A=bh. Thus, the magnitude of the resultant force acting on the rectangular surface is equal to the volume of the pressure prism.

    FR​=pavg​A=21​γhA

    FR​=21​(γh)(bh)=21​γhA

    The same graphical approach can be used for plane rectangular surfaces that do not extend up to the fluid surface. The location of FR​ can be determined by summing moments about some convenient axis.

    FR​=F1​+F2​

    FR​yA​=F1​y1​+F2​y2​

    The resultant fluid force on a surface is due only to the gage pressure contribution of the liquid in contact with the surface — patm​ does not contribute. If the surface pressure of
    the liquid is different from patm​, there will be a change in magnitude caused by the gage pressure at the liquid surface.

    #Example

    F1​=(ps​+γh1​)A

    F1​=[(50×103)+0.9(9.81×103)(2)](0.6)2

    F1​=24.356 kN

    F2​=21​γ(h2​−h1​)A

    F2​=21​(0.9)(9.81×103)(2.6−2)(0.6)2

    F2​=0.95353 kN

    FR​=F1​+F2​

    FR​=25.3 kN

    ∑MO​=0

    25.310yR​=24.356(0.3)+0.95353(0.2)

    yR​=0.296 m

    The resultant force acts 0.296m above the bottom of the plate along the vertical axis of symmetry.

    #Hydrostatic Force on a Curved Surface

    Consider a small section ds of the gate. Pressure acts perpendicular to the segment.

    Let s represent the area of the gate.

    dFR​=p ds=γh ds

    dFH​=dFR​ cosθ=γh ds cosθ

    dFV​=dFR​ sinθ=γh ds sinθ

    ds cosθ is the projected
    area on the vertical plane.

    FH​=γhc​Aproj​

    yH​=yc​Aproj​Ixc​​​+yc​

    h ds sinθ is the volume above ds.

    FV​=γV

    Vertical component of the force equals the volume of fluid above the curved surface extending to the free surface. It can be a real or an imaginary volume of fluid.

    The total resultant force is then given by,

    💡
    FR​=FH​2+FV​2​

    #Archimedes’ Principle

    When a stationary body is completely submerged in a fluid, or floating such that it is partially submerged, the resultant force acting on the body is called the buoyant force.

    The net upward vertical force results because pressure increases with depth and the pressure forces acting from below are larger than pressure forces acting from above.

    Assuming the body is weightless,

    FB​=F2​−F1​

    FB​=γh2​A−γh1​A

    FB​=γ(h2​A−h1​A)

    💡
    FB​=γV

    The buoyant force is equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by the body and is directed vertically upwards. The point through which it acts is called the center of buoyancy.

    #Example

    FB​=Wvest​+Wfoam​+FU​

    γwater​V=1.3+γfoam​V+22

    V(γwater​−γfoam​)=1.3+22

    V=64−223.3​

    V=0.376 ft3

    #Stability

    A body is said to be in a stable equilibrium position if, when displaced, it returns
    to its equilibrium position. Conversely, it is an unstable equilibrium position if,
    when displaced, it moves to a new equilibrium position.

    For floating bodies, the stability problem is more complex. As the body rotates, location
    of the center of buoyancy may change. A large floating body, such as a barge, can be
    stable even though its center of gravity lies above its center of buoyancy.

    As the body rotates, the buoyant force shifts to pass through the centroid of the
    newly formed displaced volume. Combined with the weight, a couple is formed
    that will cause the body to return to its original equilibrium position.

    However, for a relatively tall, slender body, a small rotational displacement can cause the buoyant force and the weight to form an overturning couple — unstable equilibrium.

    #Stability of a Floating Body (Metacenter)

    Let G be the center of gravity, C the center of buoyancy and M the metacenter.

    The x-axis of the body makes an angle α with the waterline as it tilts to the right.

    The buoyancy force FB​ acts through the centroid of this new displaced volume.

    New volume can be found by subtracting yellow area V2​ and adding red area V1​
    from the original area     V0​ (in green).

    Taking a differential element dx along the x-axis,

    dA=l dx (where l is the length of the body into the plane)

    dV=h dA

    dV1​=x⋅tanα dA

    dV2​=−x⋅tanα dA

    xV=x0​V0​+x1​V1​+x2​V2​

    xV=∫V1​​ x dV1​−∫V2​​ x dV2​

    xV=∫A1​​ x(x⋅tanα dA)−∫A2​​ x(−x⋅tanα dA)

    xV=tanα(∫A1​​ x2 dA+∫A2​​ x2 dA)

    xV=tanα(∫Atot​​ x2 dA)

    xV=tanα⋅IO​

    tanα=CMx​

    x=CM⋅tanα

    Substituting x=CM⋅tanα,

    (CM⋅tanα)V=tanα⋅IO​

    CM=VIO​​

    CG+GM=VIO​​

    💡
    GM=VIO​​−CG

    If the length of GM>0, metacenter is above center of gravity, body is stable.
    If the length of     GM<0, metacenter is below center of gravity, body is unstable.

    #Pressure Variation in a Fluid with Motion

    Even though a fluid may be in motion, if it moves as a rigid body, there will be no shearing stresses present. Thus, the analysis and resulting pressure relationships for rigid-body
    motion or rigid-body rotation are similar to those for fluids at rest.

    #Linear Motion

    −∇p−γk=ρa

    −(∂x∂p​i+∂y∂p​j+∂z∂p​k)−γk=ρa

    ∂x∂p​i+∂y∂p​j+(∂z∂p​+ρg)k=−ρa

    💡
    ∂x∂p​=−ρax​          ∂y∂p​=−ρay​          ∂z∂p​=−ρ(g+az​)
    💡
    dp=∂x∂p​dx+∂y∂p​dy+∂z∂p​dz

    Consider an open container of a liquid translating along a straight path with a constant acceleration a. Since ax​=0, the pressure gradient in the x-direction is zero.

    The change in pressure between two closely spaced points,
    y and y+dy, and z and z+dz, can be expressed as,

    dp=∂y∂p​ dy+∂z∂p​ dz

    dp=−ρay​ dy−ρ(g+az​) dz

    Along a line of constant pressure, dp=0.

    💡
    dydz​=−g+az​ay​​

    Along a free surface, the pressure is constant, such that for an accelerating mass, the free surface will be inclined if ay​=0. In addition, all lines of constant pressure will be parallel to the free surface.

    If ay​=0 and az​=0, which corresponds to acceleration in the vertical direction, the fluid surface will be horizontal. However, the pressure distribution is not hydrostatic.

    💡
    dzdp​=−ρ(g+az​)

    The above equation shows that pressure will vary linearly with depth, but the variation
    is due to the combined effects of gravity and the externally induced acceleration.

    #Example

    Since there’s only acceleration in the y direction, az​=0.

    dydz​=−ρ(g+az​)ρay​​

    dydz​=−gay​​

    −0.75z1​​=−gay​​

    z1​=0.75(gay​​)

    Since az​=0, the pressure along the wall varies hydrostatically.

    p=γh

    p=0.65(62.4)[0.5−0.75(gay​​)]

    p=20.28−30.42(gay​​)

    Fuel level drops below the transducer when z1​→0.5ft.

    0.5=0.75(gaymax​​​)

    aymax​​=32g​

    aymax​​=32(32.2)​

    aymax​​=21.5 ft/s2

    The pressure in horizontal layers is not constant since ∂y∂p​=0. Thus, p1​=p2​.

    #Rigid-Body Rotation

    After an initial “start-up” transient, a fluid contained in a tank that rotates with constant angular velocity ω about an axis will rotate with the tank as a rigid body. Since the paths
    of the fluid particles are circular, we will use cylindrical polar coordinates r, θ, and z.

    💡
    ∇p=∂r∂p​er​+r1​∂θ∂p​eθ​+∂z∂p​ez​

    −∇p−γez​=ρa

    −(∂r∂p​er​+r1​∂θ∂p​eθ​+∂z∂p​ez​)−γez​=ρa

    ∂r∂p​er​+r1​∂θ∂p​eθ​+(∂z∂p​+γ)ez​=−ρa

    💡
    ∂r∂p​=ρrω2          ∂θ∂p​=0          ∂z∂p​=−ρ(g+az​)
    💡
    dp=∂r∂p​dr+∂θ∂p​dθ+∂z∂p​dz

    Since normal acceleration points inwards, opposite to the convention of er​, the partial derivative of the pressure in the radial direction is positive: −ρ(−rω2)=ρrω2.

    The pressure is a function of two variables r and z.

    dp=∂r∂p​ dr+∂z∂p​ dz

    dp=ρrω2 dr−ρ(g+az​) dz

    On a horizontal plane, dz=0.

    💡
    drdp​=ρrω2

    Due to centrifugal acceleration, the pressure increases in the radial direction.

    Along a surface of constant pressure, such as the free surface, dp=0.

    💡
    drdz​=g+az​rω2​

    Integrating the above equation gives the equation for surfaces of constant pressure.

    💡
    z=2(g+az​)r2ω2​+const

    The surfaces of constant pressure are parabolic.

    ∫dp=∫ρrω2 dr−∫ρ(g+az​) dz

    💡
    p=2ρr2ω2​−ρ(g+az​)z+const

    This result shows that the pressure varies with the distance from the axis of rotation, but at a fixed radius, the pressure varies hydrostatically in the vertical direction.

    #Example

    The height h of the free surface above the tank bottom is,

    h=2gr2ω2​+h0​

    Initial volume of fluid in the tank is equal to,

    Vi​=πR2H

    Volume of the fluid with the rotating tank is,

    Vrotating​=2π∫rh dr

    Vrotating​=2π∫0R​ r(2gr2ω2​+h0​) dr

    Vrotating​=2π∫0R​ r32gω2​+rh0​ dr

    Vrotating​=2π[8gr4ω2​+2r2h0​​]0R​

    Vrotating​=4gπω2R4​+πR2h0​

    Since the volume of the fluid must remain constant,

    πR2H=4gπω2R4​+πR2h0​

    πR2(H−h0​)=4gπω2R4​

    H−h0​=4gω2R2​

    The above equation shows that the change in depth can be used to determine the rotational speed, although the relationship is not a linear one.
