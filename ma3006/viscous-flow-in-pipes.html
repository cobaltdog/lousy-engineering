    Viscous Flow in Pipes

    #Characteristics of Pipe Flow

    For pipe flow, the most important dimensionless parameter is the Reynolds number.

    💡
    Re=μρVD​=νVD​

    where V is the average velocity in the pipe

    #Entrance Region and Fully Developed Flow

    Any fluid flowing in a pipe has to enter the pipe at some location. The region
    of flow near where the fluid enters the pipe is termed the entrance region.

    Entrance region:

    • Fluid enters the pipe with a nearly uniform velocity profile at (1)
    • As fluid moves through the pipe, viscous effects cause it to stick to the pipe wall
    • A boundary layer forms such that velocity changes with x-distance along the pipe
    • Beyond section (2), end of the entrance region, velocity profile does not vary with x

    The shape of the velocity profile in the pipe is dependent on the type
    of flow (laminar or turbulent) and the length of the entrance region le​.

    For laminar flow, the relation is,

    💡
    Dle​​=0.06Re

    For turbulent flow, the relation is,

    💡
    Dle​​=4.4(Re)1/6

    Note that for very low Re flows, the entrance length can be quite short, whereas for very
    high     Re flows, it can be extremely long (equal to many pipe diameters). For practical
    engineering problems,     104<Re<105 such that 20D<le​<30D.

    Beyond entrance region:

    • Velocity is a function of only distance r from the pipe centerline
    • Flow is said to be fully developed between sections (2) and (3)
    • Velocity profile remains constant until a change in the pipe diameter, or
      the fluid flows through a bend, valve, or some other component at (3)

    #Fully Developed Pipe Flow

    Consider a fluid element of length l and elemental cross-sectional area dA=πr2.

    Applying Newton’s second law,

    ∑Fs​=mas​=0

    P1​ dA−P2​ dA+τ(2πrl)−mgsinθ=0

    P1​(πr2)−P2​(πr2)+τ(2πrl)−ρg(πr2l)sinθ=0

    Pressure drop per unit length of pipe is then,

    (P1​−P2​)(πr2)=−τ(2πrl)+ρg(πr2l)sinθ

    ΔP⋅(πr2)=l(−2τπr+ρgπr2sinθ)

    lΔP​=−r2τ​+ρgsinθ

    lΔP​=−r2τ​+ρg(lz2​−z1​​)

    💡
    lΔP​=−r2τ​−lρgΔz​

    where ΔP=P1​−P2​ and Δz=z1​−z2​

    #Fully Developed Laminar Flow

    Recall that shear stress is defined as,

    💡
    τ=μdydu​

    Rearranging the pressure drop equation,

    r2τ​=−lΔP+ρgΔz​

    💡
    τ=−2l(ΔP+ρgΔz)r​

    The velocity profile can be obtained through integration.

    μdrdV​=−2l(ΔP+ρgΔz)r​

    dV=−2μl(ΔP+ρgΔz)r​ dr

    V=∫−2μl(ΔP+ρgΔz)r​ dr

    V=−4μl(ΔP+ρgΔz)r2​+C

    At the pipe wall, where r=2D​ and V=0,

    C=16μl(ΔP+ρgΔz)D2​

    The equation for the velocity profile is then,

    💡
    V=16μl(ΔP+ρgΔz)D2​[1−(D2r​)2]

    Velocity at the centerline of the pipe is,

    💡
    Vc​=16μl(ΔP+ρgΔz)D2​

    The volume flow rate of the pipe is then,

    Q=∫V dA=∫0D/2​ V(2πr) dr

    💡
    Q=128μl(ΔP+ρgΔz)πD4​

    Corresponding average velocity is,

    Vavg​=πr2Q​

    💡
    Vavg​=32μl(ΔP+ρgΔz)D2​

    #Example 1

    Average velocity of fluid,

    Vavg​=21​Vc​=21​(2)=1 m/s

    Reynolds number,

    Re=μρVD​

    Re=0.4900(1)(0.05)​

    Re=112.5

    Since Re<2100, it is a laminar flow.

    Pressure drop per unit length,

    Vc​=16μl(ΔP+ρgΔz)D2​

    lΔP​=D216μVc​​−lρgΔz​

    lΔP​=D216μVc​​−lρg(lsinθ)​

    lΔP​=D216μVc​​−ρgsinθ

    lΔP​=0.05216(0.4)(2)​−900(9.81)sin30°

    lΔP​=705.5 Pa/m

    #Example 2

    Pressure difference,

    lΔP​=−r2τ​

    ΔP=−0.0252(−202.5)(40)​

    ΔP=648 kPa

    Note that ΔP=P1​−P2​.

    Volume flowrate through pipe,

    Q=128μl(ΔP)πD4​

    Q=128(0.8)(40)648000π(0.05)4​

    Q=3.11×10−3 m3/s

    Reynolds number of fluid flow,

    Re=μρVD​=μπr2ρQD​

    Re=0.8π(0.025)2900(3.11×10−3)(0.05)​

    Re=89.0

    Since Re<2100, it is a laminar flow. Thus, the equations are valid.

    #Fully Developed Turbulent Flow

    In practical engineering problems, turbulent flow is more likely to occur than laminar flow. Turbulent flows involve randomly fluctuating parameters. This irregular, random nature is the distinguishing feature of turbulent flow.

    Shear stress experienced by the fluid in a turbulent flow is,

    💡
    τ=μdydVˉ​+τturb​

    whereby the 1st term represents laminar shear stress, and the
    2nd term turbulent shear stress due to velocity fluctuation

    The time-averaged velocity profile is,

    💡
    Vˉ=Vc​(1−D2r​)1/n

    where n is a function of Reynolds number

    Volume flowrate of the pipe is,

    💡
    Q=2Vc​(4πD2​)(n+1)(2n+1)n2​

    Corresponding average velocity is,

    💡
    Vavg​=2Vc​[(n+1)(2n+1)n2​]

    #Example

    Reynolds number of fluid flow,

    Re=μρVD​=μπr2ρQD​

    Re=0.001π(0.25)21000(0.1)(0.5)​

    Re=2.55×103 (turbulent flow)

    From the graph, at Re=2.55×103,

    n=7.6

    Velocity at pipe centerline,

    Q=2Vc​(4πD2​)(n+1)(2n+1)n2​

    Vc​=2n2(πr2)Q(n+1)(2n+1)​

    Vc​=2π(7.6)2(0.25)20.1(8.6)(16.2)​

    Vc​=0.61422 m/s

    #Head Loss in Pipes

    It is necessary to determine the head loss hL​ that occurs in a pipe flow so
    that the energy equation can be used in the analysis of pipe flow problems.

    Overall head loss for a pipe system consists of:

    • Head loss due to viscous effects — major loss
    • Head loss in the various pipe components — minor loss

    💡
    hL​=hL,major​+hL,minor​

    #Major Head Loss

    Consider flow through a horizontal pipe.
    The energy equation for the flow is then,

    ρgP1​​+2gV1​2​+z1​−hL​=ρgP2​​+2gV2​2​+z2​

    ρgP1​​−hL​=ρgP2​​

    ρgP1​−P2​​=hL​

    Without head loss, ΔP=P1​−P2​=0.
    With frictional loss, ΔP=P1​−P2​>0.
    Thus, friction causes a pressure drop.

    To determine major loss, perform
    a dimensional analysis of the flow.

    ΔP=F(ρ, V, D, μ, l, ε)

    where ε is surface roughness of the pipe wall

    The resulting dimensionless relation is,

    21​ρV2ΔP​=ϕ~​(μρVD​, Dε​, Dl​)

    where Dε​ is relative pipe roughness

    Let friction factor be denoted as f=ϕ(Re, Dε​).

    💡
    ΔP=f⋅Dl​⋅2ρV2​

    The equation is valid only for horizontal pipes.

    Dividing the above equation by ρg,

    💡
    hL,major​=f⋅Dl​⋅2gV2​

    The equation is valid for any fully developed, steady, incompressible pipe flow.

    Friction factor for a laminar flow, where Re≤2100, is,

    ρgP1​​+2gV1​2​+z1​−hL​=ρgP2​​+2gV2​2​+z2​

    hL​=ρgP1​−P2​​+z1​−z2​

    hL​=ρgΔP+ρgΔz​

    hL​=ρgD232Vμl​

    hL​=ρVD/μ64​⋅Dl​⋅2gV2​

    💡
    f laminar​=Re64​

    The friction factor is derived using the energy equation
    and average velocity of a fully developed laminar flow.

    Friction factor for a turbulent flow, where Re>2100, is,

    💡
    f​1​=−2.0 log(3.7ε/D​+Ref​2.51​)

    Colebrook formula is valid for transitional and turbulent flow.

    Friction factor for a turbulent flow in smooth pipes, where Re>2100, is,

    💡
    f turbulent​=Re1/40.316​

    Blasius formula is valid if Re<105 and Dε​=0.

    #Example 1

    Reynolds number of flow,

    Re=μρVavgD​​=μρ(0.5Vc​)D​

    Re=0.4900(1)(0.05)​

    Re=112.5

    Since Re<2100, it is a laminar flow.

    Applying the energy equation,

    ρgP1​​+2gV1​2​+z1​−hL​=ρgP2​​+2gV2​2​+z2​

    ρgP1​​+2gV1​2​+z1​−(f⋅Dl​⋅2gV2​)=ρgP2​​+2gV2​2​+z2​

    ρgP1​​+2gV1​2​+z1​−(Re64​⋅Dl​⋅2gV2​)=ρgP2​​+2gV2​2​+z2​

    ρgP1​−P2​​=(Re64​⋅Dl​⋅2gV2​)+z2​−z1​

    ΔP=(Re64​⋅Dl​⋅2ρV2​)+ρg(z2​−z1​)

    where V=V1​=V2​=Vavg​=0.5Vc​

    Pressure drop over the 1-meter length pipe is,

    ΔP=[112.564​⋅0.051​⋅2900(1)2​]+900(9.81)(−sin30°)

    ΔP=705.5 Pa

    #Example 2

    Applying the energy equation,

    hin​−hout​=(ρgPB​​+2gVB​2​+zB​)−(ρgPA​​+2gVA​2​+zA​)

    hpump​−(f1​⋅D1​l1​​⋅2gV1​2​)−(f2​⋅D2​l2​​⋅2gV2​2​)=2gVB​2​+zB​−zA​

    hpump​=(f1​⋅D1​l1​​⋅2gV1​2​)+(f2​⋅D2​l2​​⋅2gV2​2​)+2gVB​2​+zB​−zA​

    Note that since the reservoir and exit are exposed to the atmosphere, then
    gauge pressure     PA​=PB​=0. Also, using large tank assumption for the
    reservoir, then velocity at the water surface     VA​=0.

    Friction factor of the pipes

    Pipe 1

    D1​ε1​​=0.0004

    Re1​=μA1​ρQD1​​=1.02×105

    From the Moody chart,

    f1​=0.02

    Pipe 2

    D2​ε2​​=0.005

    Re2​=μA2​ρQD2​​=2.55×105

    From the Moody chart,

    f2​=0.03

    Required pump pressure head,

    hpump​=[0.02⋅0.2520​⋅2(9.81)0.407442​]+[0.03⋅0.110​⋅2(9.81)2.54652​]+2(9.81)2.54652​+4

    hpump​=5.3356 m

    #Minor Head Loss

    Consider flow in a pipe system consisting of straight pipes, bends, valves, etc.
    These additional components add to the overall head loss of the system.

    Minor loss is defined in terms of the loss coefficient KL​.

    💡
    hL,minor​=KL​⋅2gV2​

    where KL​ is dependent on the geometry of the component considered and
    may also be dependent on the fluid properties (e.g. Reynolds number)

    For many practical applications, the Reynolds number is large enough such that the flow
    through a component is dominated by inertia effects and viscous effects are negligible.
    Hence, the loss coefficient of most components are only dependent on geometry.

    #Entrance and Exit Losses

    Pipe systems may contain transition sections in which the pipe diameter changes
    from one size to another. Any change in flow area contributes to the minor loss. Extreme cases involve flow into a pipe from a tank, or out of a pipe into a tank.

    Entrance regions:

    • Sharp-edged entrance may result in a vena contracta region
    • Flow separation occurs as the fluid cannot turn a sharp corner
    • Velocity in the vena contracta is higher than that downstream
    • Some kinetic energy of the fluid is lost due to viscous effects which
      results in a pressure drop downstream of the vena contracta region

    Exit regions:

    • All the kinetic energy of the exiting fluid is dissipated through viscous effects
      as the exiting fluid mixes with fluid in the tank and eventually comes to a rest
    • Exit loss is therefore equivalent to one velocity head, or KL​=1

    #Example

    Applying the energy equation,

    ρgPA​​+2gVA​2​+zA​−hL,major​−hL,minor​=ρgPB​​+2gVB​2​+zB​

    zA​−zB​=hL,major​+hL,minor​

    zA​−zB​=f1​D1​l1​​2gV1​2​+KL,in​2gV1​2​+KL,out​2gV1​2​

    zA​−zB​=2gV1​2​(f1​D1​l1​​+KL,in​+KL,out​)

    2=2(9.81)V1​2​(0.02⋅0.1100​+0.5+1)

    V1​=1.3510 m/s

    Volume flow rate through pipe,

    Q=V1​A

    Q=1.3510π(0.05)2

    Q=0.010610 m3/s

    Energy grade line equation,

    EGL=ρgP​+2gV2​+z

    Hydraulic grade line equation,

    HGL=ρgP​+z

    #Expansion and Contraction Losses

    Consider flow through a pipe with a sudden increase in diameter (expansion).
    Let the control volume be from the exit to some point downstream.

    Continuity equation for the control volume is,

    m˙=ρA1​V1​=ρA2​V2​

    Momentum equation in the x-direction is,

    ∑Fx​=∑m˙out​Vout​−∑m˙in​Vin​

    P1​A2​−P2​A2​=m˙(V2​−V1​)

    (P1​−P2​)A2​=m˙(V2​−V1​)

    Energy equation for the control volume is,

    ρgP1​​+2gV1​2​+z1​−KL​2gV1​2​=ρgP2​​+2gV2​2​+z2​

    Minor loss due to sudden change in diameter is then,

    💡
    KL​=(1−A2​A1​​)2

    If the expansion or contraction is gradual (conical diffuser/nozzle), the loss coefficient varies with the angle of the cone. For small angles, most of the head loss is due to the wall shear stress. For large angles, losses are due to dissipation of kinetic energy.

    For a nozzle, which accelerates a fluid, the loss coefficient ranges
    from     KL​=0.02 for θ=30° and KL​=0.07 for θ=60°.

    Pipe bends also contribute to total head loss. The loss coefficient of pipe bends
    depend strongly on the shape of the component and weakly on the Reynolds
    number for typical large Re flows.

    #Flowrate Measurement

    A differential pressure flow meter determines the fluid’s flowrate through
    a pipe by measuring the pressure difference across a pipe restriction.

    #Orifice Meter

    Consider an ideal flow (incompressible, inviscid and steady) through an orifice meter.

    Applying the energy equation,

    ρgP1​​+2gV1​2​+z1​=ρgP2​​+2gV2​2​+z2​

    Note that there is no energy head entering or leaving the control volume.

    Making V2​ the subject,

    2gV2​2​=ρgP1​−P2​​+2gV1​2​+(z1​−z2​)

    V2​2=ρ2(P1​−P2​)+2ρg(z1​−z2​)​+V1​2

    V2​=ρ(1−β4)2(P1​−P2​)+2ρg(z1​−z2​)​​

    where β=Dd​

    Flowrate of this ideal fluid is then,

    💡
    Qideal​=A2​ρ(1−β4)2(P1​−P2​)+2ρg(z1​−z2​)​​

    where A2​ is the cross-sectional area of the orifice

    Errors in flowrate measurement are the result of:

    • Vena contracta area is less than the area of the orifice
    • Swirling and turbulent flow near the orifice plate cause energy loss (head loss)

    The actual flowrate is determined using an orifice discharge coefficient.

    💡
    Qactual​=Co​Qideal​

    where Co​ is a function of β and Reynolds number

    #Example

    Coefficient β,

    β=Dd​=0.080.05​=0.625

    Reynolds number,

    Re=μρVD​=μρ(Q/A1​)D​

    Re=1.97×10−51.4(0.022/0.00503)(0.08)​

    Re=2.4883×104

    Discharge coefficient C0​,

    C0​=0.622

    Ideal flowrate,

    Qactual​=C0​Qideal​

    Qideal​=0.6220.022​

    Qideal​=0.035370 m3/s

    Velocity before the orifice,

    Qideal​=A1​V1​

    V1​=π(0.04)20.035370​

    V2​=7.0366 m/s

    Velocity after the orifice,

    Qideal​=A2​V2​

    V2​=π(0.025)20.035370​

    V2​=18.014 m/s

    Applying the energy equation,

    ρgP1​​+2gV1​2​+z1​=ρgP2​​+2gV2​2​+z2​

    P1​−P2​=2ρ​(V2​2−V1​2)

    P1​−P2​=21.4​(18.0142−7.03662)

    P1​−P2​=192.49 Pa

    #Nozzle Meter

    A nozzle meter uses a contoured nozzle instead of a simple flat plate with a hole as
    in the orifice meter. The resulting flow pattern for the nozzle meter is closer to ideal
    than an orifice meter.

    Viscous effects are accounted for using a nozzle discharge coefficient.

    💡
    Qactual​=Cn​Qideal​

    Note that Cn​>Co​ as the nozzle meter is more efficient than the orifice meter.

    #Example

    Reynolds number,

    Re=μρVD​=νVD​

    Re=1×10−6(0.003/0.0028274)(0.06)​

    Re=6.3662×104

    Actual flowrate,

    Qactual​=Cn​A2​V2​

    Qactual​=Cn​⋅4πd2​ρ(1−β4)2(P1​−P2​)+2ρg(z1​−z2​)​​

    0.003=Cn​⋅4πd2​1000(1−β4)2(4000)​​

    0.0013505=1−β4​Cn​d2​

    1st iteration

    Assume the flow is ideal such that Cn​=1.

    Assume 1−β4≈1,

    0.0013505=1−β4​d2​

    d=0.036749 m

    Coefficient β,

    β=0.060.036749​=0.61248

    From the graph,

    Cn​≈0.978

    Since computed Cn​< assumed Cn​, it contradicts our assumption.

    2nd iteration

    Assume Cn​=0.978.

    0.0013505=1−β4​0.978d2​

    d=0.037160 m

    Coefficient β,

    β=0.060.037160​=0.61934

    From the graph,

    Cn​≈0.978

    Since Cn​ for the 2nd iteration is approximately
    the same, there is no need to continue iterating.

    #Venturi Meter

    The venturi meter is the most precise and most expensive flowmeter. The geometry of
    the meter is designed to reduce head losses to a minimum by providing a relatively streamlined contraction, and a gradual expansion downstream of the throat.

    Head loss is largely due to friction losses along the walls of the meter.

    💡
    Qactual​=Cv​Qideal​

    Note that Cv​ is dependent on the shape of the converging and diverging sections.
