    Fluid Kinematics

    #Velocity Field

    The representation of fluid parameters as functions of spatial coordinates is known
    as a field representation.

    To describe a flow at different times, the fluid parameters are also represented as
    a function of time.

    💡
    V=u(x,y,z,t)i+v(x,y,z,t)j+w(x,y,z,t)k

    u, v and w are the x, y and z components of the velocity vector.

    #Eulerian Approach

    The Eulerian approach uses field concept to analyze fluid mechanics problems by representing the fluid parameters
    as functions of space and time, V=f(r,t).

    An example is the flow past an airfoil. The pressure field is indicated by using a contour plot showing lines of constant pressure, with gray shading indicating the intensity.

    #Lagrangian Approach

    The Lagrangian approach involves following individual fluid particles as they move about and determining how the fluid properties change as a function of time. The
    fluid particles are “tagged”, and their properties determined as they move.

    In the Lagrangian approach, to measure the temperature of the smoke, one would attach a temperature-measuring device to a particular fluid particle — particle A,
    and record that particle’s temperature as it moves about.

    Thus, we would obtain that particle’s temperature as a function of time TA​=TA​(t). The use of many such devices moving with various particles would provide the temperature of these particles as a function of time.

    #Streamlines, Streaklines, and Pathlines

    In a steady flow, streamlines, streaklines and pathlines are the same.

    #Streamline

    A streamline is a line that is everywhere tangent to the velocity field. If the flow is steady, nothing at a fixed point changes with time, thus the streamlines are fixed in space. If the flow is unsteady, the streamlines may change shape with time.

    Streamlines are obtained analytically by integrating the equations defining the lines tangent to the velocity field.

    💡
    dxdy​=uv​          dxdz​=uw​          dydz​=vw​

    #Streakline

    A streakline is an instantaneous line whose points are occupied by all particles originating from some specified point in the flow. If the flow is steady, a steady streakline is formed, which is exactly the same as a streamline.

    #Pathline

    A pathline is the line traced out by a given particle as it flows from one point to another. The pathline is a Lagrangian concept that can be produced in the laboratory by marking
    a fluid particle and taking a time exposure photograph of its motion.

    Pathlines are obtained by integrating the pathline equations.

    💡
    u=dtdx​          v=dtdy​          w=dtdz​

    #Systems vs Control Volumes

    Fluid Mechanics: Topic 6.1 - Systems vs Control VolumesWant to see more mechanical engineering instructional videos? Visit the Cal Poly Pomona Mechanical Engineering Department's video library, ME Online (http://www.cpp.edu/meonline).https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pOxkOIO41p0

    Description of a system:

    • A collection of matter of fixed identity — same atoms or fluid particles
    • May continually change size and shape, but always contains the same mass
    • Molecules of the system are “tagged” such that they can be identified as they move

    Description of a control volume:

    • Volume in space through which fluid may flow, and is independent of mass
    • Control volumes, in general, are fixed, non-deformable volumes
    • Control surfaces surround the control volume — boundary

    #Reynolds Transport Theorem

    Fluid Mechanics: Topic 6.2 - Reynolds transport theoremhttps://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kUGxaPafeRA

    Let B represent any fluid parameters and b represent the amount per unit mass.

    💡
    B=mb=ρVb

    The parameter B is an extensive property that is proportional to the amount of mass being considered whereas b is an intensive property that is independent of the amount of mass.

    The amount of an extensive property that a system possesses at a given instant Bsys​ can
    be determined by adding up the amount associated with each fluid particle in the system.

    Bsys​=dV→0lim​ ∑i​ bi​(ρi​ dVi​)=∫sys​ ρb dV

    Thus, for a system, the rate of change of B is given by,

    💡
    dtdBsys​​=dtd(∫sys​ ρb dV)​

    For a control volume, the limits of integration cover the control volume of interest.

    💡
    dtdBCV​​=dtd(∫CV​ ρb dV)​

    #Derivation of Theorem (Simplified Version)

    Consider the control volume to be the stationary volume between (1) and (2).
    The system is the fluid occupying the control volume at some initial time t.

    A short time later, at time t+dt, the system has moved slightly to the right. The fluid particles at (2) moved a distance of V2​ dt to the right while the particles at (1) moved
    a distance of V1​ dt, also to the right.

    Let the outflow from the control volume be II, the inflow be I, and the control volume itself be CV. Assume the fluid flows across (1) and (2) in a direction normal to the
    control surfaces and that V1​ and V2​ are constant across the sections.

    At time t,

    At time t+dt,

    Bsys​(t)=BCV​(t)

    Bsys​(t+dt)=BCV​(t+dt)−B1​(t+dt)+B2​(t+dt)

    The change in the amount of B in the system in the time interval dt is given by,

    dtdBsys​​=dtdBsys​(t+dt)−Bsys​(t)​

    dtdBsys​​=dt[BCV​(t+dt)−B1​(t+dt)+B2​(t+dt)]−BCV​(t)​

    💡
    dtdBsys​​=dtBCV​(t+dt)−BCV​(t)​−dtB1​(t+dt)​+dtB2​(t+dt)​

    The first term is the rate of change of B within the control volume.

    dt→0lim​ dtBCV​(t+dt)−BCV​(t)​=dtd(∫sys​ ρb dV)​=∂t∂BCV​​

    During the time interval dt, the volume of fluid across (1) and (2) is given by,

    dV=A dl=AV dt

    Thus, the amount of B within (1) and (2) is given by,

    B1​(t+dt)=ρ1​b1​ dV1​=ρ1​A1​V1​b1​ dt

    B2​(t+dt)=ρ2​b2​ dV2​=ρ2​A2​V2​b2​ dt

    Hence, the rate of inflow and outflow of B into and out of the control volume is,

    B˙in​=ρ1​A1​V1​b1​

    B˙out​=ρ2​A2​V2​b2​

    Combining the terms, we get the equation for Reynolds transport theorem.

    💡
    dtdBsys​​=∂t∂BCV​​−ρ1​A1​V1​b1​+ρ2​A2​V2​b2​

    The above equation is valid for a fixed control volume with one inlet and one outlet having uniform properties — density, velocity, and b, across the inlet and outlet with the velocity normal to the control surfaces.

    #Derivation of Theorem (General Version)

    A flow field may be simple (one-dimensional flow), or it may involve a complex,
    unsteady, three-dimensional flow, such as the flow through a human heart.

    Consider the system to be the fluid within the control volume at the initial time t.
    A short time later, a portion of the fluid has exited from the control volume, and
    additional fluid has entered the control volume.

    The flowrate of B into and out of the control volume is the sum of the infinitesimal area element of size dA on the portion of the control surface dividing the regions and the control volume.

    During the time interval dt, the volume of fluid that passes across an element is,

    dV=dln​ dA

    dV=(dl cosθ) dA

    dV=(Vcosθ dt) dA

    Thus, the amount of B within (1) and (2) is given by,

    dB=ρb dV

    dB=ρb(Vcosθ dt) dA

    Hence, the rate of inflow and outflow of B into and out of the control volume is,

    dtdB​=ρb(Vcosθ) dA

    dB˙=ρbVcosθ dA

    ∫dB˙=∫ρbVcosθ dA

    B˙=∫CS​ ρbVcosθ dA

    Vcosθ is the velocity component normal to the area element dA (dot product).
    From the definition of a dot product, this can be written as     Vcosθ=V⋅n.

    The net flowrate of B across the entire control surface is,

    B˙out​−B˙in​=∫CSout​​ ρb(V⋅n) dA+∫CSin​​ ρb(V⋅n) dA

    B˙net​=∫CS​ ρb(V⋅n) dA

    B˙net​=∫CS​ ρbVcosθ dA

    For outflow regions −90°<θ<90°, the integral over the control surface is positive.
    For inflow regions     90°<θ<270°, the integral over the control surface is negative.

    The general form of the theorem for a fixed, nondeforming control volume is,

    💡
    dtdBsys​​=∂t∂​∫CV​ ρb dV+∫CS​ ρb(V⋅n) dA
